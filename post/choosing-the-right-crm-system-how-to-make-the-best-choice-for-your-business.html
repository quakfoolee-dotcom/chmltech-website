Choosing a CRM for a Small Business: Requirements, 2026 Prices and a Payback Example

Quak Foo LeeMar 11, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

A customer relationship management (CRM) system keeps everything you know about customers and prospects in one place: who they are, what you have discussed, what you have quoted, and what happens next. For a small business the main benefit is simple: fewer leads and follow-ups lost because they lived in someone's inbox or memory. This guide, first published in 2023, has been rewritten with a requirements checklist, 2026 price ranges and a worked cost example.

Signs you need a CRM

  • Leads come in by email, phone and web form, and nobody can say how many there were last month.
  • Follow-ups are missed when someone is busy or away.
  • Quotes go out but nobody tracks which are still open.
  • Customer history leaves with the salesperson who leaves.
  • Forecasting next quarter's sales is a guess.

Decide what you need before you look

Start from how you sell, not from feature lists. Map your sales process from first contact to signed order, list the steps, and note what information each step needs. Then score these core requirements as must-have, nice-to-have or not needed.

RequirementWhat it means in practice
Contacts and companiesOne record per customer, with history of calls, emails and meetings
PipelineDeals by stage, with value and expected close date

QuotesQuotes from the CRM, or a link to the system that makes them
ReportingPipeline by stage, win rate, activity, forecast
IntegrationsAccounting, website forms, e-commerce, marketing email
MobileUsable on a phone by people who visit customers

2026 price ranges

Entry-level CRM plans for small teams cost roughly $14 to $25 per user per month in 2026, with several vendors offering free tiers that limit users or contacts. Professional tiers with automation and better reporting typically cost $40 to $100 per user per month, and enterprise tiers more. For 8 users on an entry plan, a year's licences look like this (US list prices, billed annually; confirm current pricing with each vendor):

Licences are rarely the biggest cost. Set-up, importing and cleaning existing contacts, connecting email and accounting, and training usually cost as much as the first year's licences, and the time your team spends using it matters most of all.

Worked example: what does a CRM pay back?

A small engineering firm receives about 30 enquiries a month and wins work worth an average of $18,000 gross margin per job. Reviewing a quarter, it finds that about one enquiry in ten never received a follow-up. If a CRM with reminders and a shared pipeline recovers even a quarter of those, and the firm wins one in five of them, that is roughly two extra jobs a year, about $36,000 of margin, against licences of a few thousand dollars. The numbers are illustrative; the point is that the value of a CRM comes from follow-up discipline, not features.

DataBring it?Notes

Implementation in six steps

  1. Define your pipeline stages in plain words, with what must be true to move a deal to the next stage.
  2. Clean your contacts before importing: remove duplicates and old leads.
  3. Connect email and calendar so activity is logged without typing.
  4. Start with three reports: new leads by source, pipeline by stage, and overdue follow-ups.
  5. Train in short sessions on real deals, not demo data.
  6. Use it in the weekly sales meeting. If the meeting runs from the CRM, people keep it current.

Why CRM projects fail

  • Bought for management reports, so salespeople see it as extra admin.
  • Too many required fields and stages from day one.
  • No connection to email, so activity has to be typed twice.
  • Nobody owns the data, so duplicates and old deals pile up.
  • The owner keeps managing from a spreadsheet, so the team does too.

AI features

Most CRMs now offer AI features: drafting follow-up emails, summarizing calls and email threads, scoring leads and suggesting next steps. They are useful once the basic data is good, and often sold as paid add-ons. Turn them on after the team uses the CRM consistently, not as a reason to buy one.
