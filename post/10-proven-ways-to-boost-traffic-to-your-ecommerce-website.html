Growing a Small Online Store: Traffic Channels and the Conversion Basics That Make Them Pay

Quak Foo LeeApr 2, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

For a small online store, traffic is only half the problem. A store converting 1% of visitors needs twice the traffic of one converting 2% to make the same sales, so improving the store often pays faster than buying more visitors. This guide, first written in 2023 as ten traffic tips, has been rewritten to cover both: the main traffic channels, and the conversion basics that decide whether the traffic pays off.

Traffic channels compared

ChannelWhat it takesTime to resultsCost

Paid socialTargeted ads to look-alike and retargeting audiencesDaysMedium to high

Partnerships and creatorsReviews, bundles and collaborations with complementary brandsWeeksVaries

Most small stores do best with two or three channels done well: search and Google listings for people already looking, email for repeat sales, and one social channel for discovery.

Product pages that rank and sell

  • Write your own descriptions. Manufacturer text is on hundreds of other sites; your own words, sizing advice and use cases help both search and buyers.
  • Answer the questions that stop a purchase: dimensions, materials, compatibility, delivery time and cost, returns.
  • Show it properly: several photos, a short video, and the product in use.
  • Collect and show reviews, including photos from customers.
  • Add product structured data so search engines can show price, availability and ratings.

Conversion basics

  1. Speed on mobile. Most visits are on phones; slow pages lose buyers. See technical SEO and Core Web Vitals.
  2. Clear shipping costs early. Unexpected costs at checkout are a leading reason for abandoned carts.
  3. A short checkout with guest checkout and common payment options.
  4. Trust signals: real contact details, returns policy, secure payment, reviews.
  5. Abandoned-cart emails, sent within hours, often recover a meaningful share of lost sales.

Worked example: traffic or conversion?

A store has 20,000 sessions a month, converts 1.2% and has an average order of $85, giving about 240 orders and $20,400 a month. Raising conversion to 1.6% through faster pages, clearer shipping costs and abandoned-cart emails gives about 320 orders and $27,200, an extra $6,800 a month with no extra traffic. Getting the same increase from traffic alone would need about 6,667 more sessions a month. The figures are illustrative; your own analytics will show which lever is cheaper.

Measure what matters

Track sessions, conversion rate, average order value and revenue by channel in Google Analytics 4, with e-commerce events set up so you can see where shoppers drop off: product view, add to cart, begin checkout, purchase. Tag links from email and social with UTM parameters. Review monthly and move effort towards the channels that bring buyers, not just visitors. See our GA4 guide.

Repeat customers

Winning a first order is expensive; repeat orders are where many small stores make their margin. A good post-purchase email, a reason to come back (replenishment reminders, new arrivals, a loyalty offer) and excellent service when something goes wrong all raise repeat rates. Keep commercial email within Canada's anti-spam legislation (CASL) by collecting consent at checkout.

A 90-day plan for a small store

WhenFocus
Weeks 1–2Set up GA4 e-commerce events and Search Console; check mobile speed; fix shipping-cost clarity at checkout
Weeks 3–4Rewrite descriptions for the 20 best-selling products; add product structured data; submit a Merchant Center feed
Month 2Launch welcome and abandoned-cart emails; ask recent buyers for reviews; start one social channel with a weekly rhythm
Month 3Test a small paid campaign on the best-converting products; review results by channel; drop what does not convert

Common mistakes

  • Spending on ads before the store converts the traffic it already has.
  • Copying manufacturer descriptions, which gives search engines no reason to rank your page.
  • Hiding delivery costs until the last step of checkout.
  • Judging channels by visits rather than orders and margin.
  • Ignoring existing customers, who are usually the cheapest source of the next order.
