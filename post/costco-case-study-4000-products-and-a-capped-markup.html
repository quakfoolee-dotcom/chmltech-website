Costco Case Study: 4,000 Products and a Capped Markup

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Jim Sinegal, Costco's co-founder and chief executive, in the spring of 2004. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

In March 2004 Costco reported results that beat Wall Street's expectations, and its share price fell anyway. The complaint was not about sales. It was about how the company chose to spend its margin. Costco paid its warehouse staff well above its main rival, Walmart's Sam's Club, covered most of their health insurance, and refused to mark up any product by more than a fixed percentage. A Deutsche Bank analyst summed up the frustration in BusinessWeek: at Costco, he said, it was better to be an employee or a customer than a shareholder.

Jim Sinegal had set those rules himself when he co-founded the company in 1983. Now investors wanted a choice from him. Costco could raise its margins a little, trim wage and benefit costs toward the industry norm, and give the share price the lift analysts were asking for. Or it could hold the model and keep explaining why it worked.

Company background

Costco runs membership warehouses. Shoppers pay an annual fee to get in, and in return the company promises prices that no conventional retailer can match. Three operating rules make that promise possible:

  • A capped markup. No branded item may be marked up more than 14% over cost, and no item under its own label (later Kirkland Signature) more than 15%. Supermarkets and department stores commonly mark up by 25% to 50%.
  • A narrow range. A warehouse carries about 4,000 products. A typical supermarket carries 30,000 or more, and a Walmart Supercenter well over 100,000. Costco offers one or two choices in each category, bought in very large volumes.
  • A low-cost box. Goods arrive on pallets and are sold from them, with no shelf stocking, little décor and limited advertising.

The narrow range is what turns the capped markup into a profit. Each product sells in huge volume, so Costco has buying power with suppliers far beyond its size, and inventory turns over so fast that the company often sells goods before it has to pay for them. The membership fee then supplies most of the profit: the merchandise is priced to cover costs plus a thin margin, and the fees, which cost almost nothing to collect, flow through to earnings. The company also became known for small symbols of the same idea, such as the hot dog and soda combo it has sold for $1.50 since 1985.

The situation in 2004

Costco's critics had a point that could be measured. Its pay was well above Sam's Club's. Its supporters had a point that could be measured too: Costco got much more out of each employee.

Costco's management argued that the wages paid for themselves through lower staff turnover, less theft and more productive warehouses. Its chief financial officer told BusinessWeek that paying higher wages translated into more efficiency. The analysts' reply was that the same efficiency at normal wages would be worth even more to shareholders. Both sides agreed on one thing: Costco's expenses as a share of sales were the lowest in the industry. Walmart's selling, general and administrative costs were about 17% of sales and Target's about 24%, while Costco's were about 9.8%.

There was a second pressure. Sam's Club, with Walmart's purchasing power behind it, had improved its stores and its merchandise, and it was competing hard on price. Raising markups would make Costco's prices easier to beat. Not raising them meant that any growth in profit had to come from more members, more sales per member, or a higher fee.

The options

Option A: Raise margins a little

Lift the markup cap by one or two points on selected categories. On net sales of about $47 billion, each point of margin is worth more than $400 million a year, roughly half of Costco's net income at the time. The risk is that members notice, and that the price gap to Sam's Club narrows.

Option B: Bring wages and benefits toward the industry norm

Slow pay increases and ask staff to pay more of their health costs. This answers the analysts directly, but it puts at risk the low turnover and high productivity that Exhibit 2 shows.

Option C: Hold the model and grow the fee base

Keep the markup cap and pay policy, open more warehouses, and raise the membership fee every few years instead. Profit growth is slower in the short run, and the company has to keep defending its approach to investors.

Option D: Widen the range

Add more products and categories to raise sales per visit. This would dilute the buying power of the narrow range and add inventory, store space and complexity.

Discussion questions

  1. What is Costco actually selling: products, or the membership? How does your answer change which option looks right?
  2. Use Exhibit 1. If Costco cut its hourly pay to Sam's Club's level, what would it save, and what would it risk losing?
  3. Why does a narrow product range make a capped markup affordable? Trace the link through buying power, inventory turns and labour.
  4. Who should the company serve first: members, employees or shareholders? Is that a real trade-off over ten years, or only over one?
  5. If you were Sinegal, how would you answer the analyst's remark at the next earnings call?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Sinegal held the model. He kept the markup cap and the pay policy, told reporters that raising prices to please Wall Street would be the company's downfall, and described the wage policy as good business rather than generosity. Profit growth came from new warehouses, more members and periodic fee increases instead. The Gold Star membership fee was raised in 2006, 2011 and 2017, and again in September 2024, from $60 to $65 (Executive from $120 to $130), the first increase in seven years. Renewal rates barely moved.

Two decades later the numbers make the case. In fiscal 2025 Costco had net sales of $269.9 billion, total revenue of $275.2 billion and net income of $8.1 billion, from 914 warehouses. Membership fees were $5.3 billion, less than 2% of revenue but about two-thirds of net income. The renewal rate was 92.3% in the United States and Canada and 89.8% worldwide, with 145 million cardholders. Kirkland Signature, the own label held to the 15% cap, had grown into one of the largest consumer brands in North America.

The share price that analysts worried about in 2004 rose many times over in the years that followed. The model the analysts wanted changed is now the one competitors are told to copy.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

A narrow range can be a strategy, not a limitation. Fewer products means larger orders, better prices from suppliers, faster turns and simpler operations. An ABC analysis of your own range often shows that a small share of products earns most of the margin.

Decide what you sell, then price for it. Costco sells access and trust; the goods are priced to protect them. A service firm that sells a retainer, or a manufacturer that sells a maintenance contract, can apply the same logic.

Measure the return on people, not only their cost. Sales per employee, turnover and error rates tell you whether higher pay is buying productivity. Track them before you cut.

Publish your rules. A visible rule, such as a markup cap or a delivery promise, is easy for customers to trust and hard for managers to erode one exception at a time.
