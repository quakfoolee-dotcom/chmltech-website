The Core Values of Process Improvement: Ten Principles and How to Apply Them

Quak Foo LeeSep 9, 2021Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

Process improvement methods such as Lean, Six Sigma and business process management give you tools. Core values decide whether the tools stick. A team that maps a process brilliantly but blames people for errors, or fixes one department at another's expense, will be back where it started within a year. This article replaces a two-part series first published on this site in 2021.

The ten values below follow the "process improvement manifesto" set out by Tristan Boutros and Tim Purdie in The Process Improvement Handbook (McGraw-Hill, 2014). The wording and the examples here are ours; read the book for the authors' full treatment.

The ten values and what they look like in practice

ValueWhat it meansWhat you see when it is working
AgilityImprove in small steps and adjust as you learnChanges are trialled for weeks, not planned for a year
QualityBuild quality into the process, people and technology from the startDefects are traced to a step, not blamed on a person
LeadershipLeaders set a clear direction and stay open to ideasThe owner or manager attends the improvement review
CommunicationInvolve the people affected in decisionsFront-line staff help map the process before it is changed
RespectKeep working relationships safe and cooperativePeople raise problems without fear of blame
DisciplineRun processes the same way every time and follow throughAgreed changes become the written standard
Whole-business viewDecide what is best for the company, not one departmentA fix in sales is checked for its effect on operations
ServiceImprovement work serves customers and colleaguesEach project names the customer it helps
Continuous learningTrain people in improvement methods and share lessonsEach project ends with a short lessons-learned note
Human-centred designDesign processes around the people who use themNew forms and screens are tested with real users

Why values matter more in a small business

In a large company, a process office and formal governance can keep improvement going even when the culture is uneven. A business with 20 or 80 people has no such structure: the habits of the owner and two or three managers are the system. That makes the values above less a poster and more a description of how the leadership team behaves when a problem is found.

A ten-minute self-check

Score each value from 1 (rarely true) to 5 (consistently true) for the last improvement your business made. Then look at the two lowest scores.

  • Low on respect or communication: start meetings about problems with the process, not the person, and invite the people who do the work.
  • Low on discipline: finish one change properly, with the new standard written down, before starting the next. Process documentation that people use shows how.
  • Low on whole-business view: map the process across departments, not inside one. A swim lane map makes the hand-offs visible.
  • Low on learning: after each change, hold a 15-minute review: what worked, what did not, what we will do differently.

Putting the values into a project

A practical way to use them is as a short charter checklist at the start of each improvement project: who is the customer (service), who from each affected team is involved (communication, whole-business view), how the change will be trialled (agility), how it will become the standard (discipline), and what the team will learn (learning). The two-part series on how to implement process improvements walks through a full project.
