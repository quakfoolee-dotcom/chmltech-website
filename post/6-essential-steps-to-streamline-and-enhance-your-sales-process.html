How to Build and Streamline a Sales Process: Stages, Measures and a Worked Example

A sales process is the agreed set of steps your business takes from first contact to a signed order, with clear rules for what happens at each step. Without one, every salesperson works differently, follow-ups depend on memory, and forecasting is a guess. This article brings together two posts from late 2025, one on streamlining the sales process and one on why a strong process matters. Both have been rewritten, with a stage table and a worked example; statistics we could not trace to a source have been removed.

Why a defined process pays

  • Fewer lost opportunities: every lead has an owner and a next step.
  • Faster onboarding: new salespeople learn one way of working.
  • Better forecasts: deals in each stage can be counted and weighted.
  • Clear problems: when deals stall, you can see at which stage.
  • Customer experience: buyers get consistent, timely responses.

Step 1: map how you sell today

Take five recent wins and five losses and trace each from first contact to outcome: who did what, how long each step took, and where it waited. The real process is often different from the one people describe, and the differences point to the fixes.

Step 2: define the stages

Each stage needs an entry rule (what must be true to be in it), a required action and a measure. A typical business-to-business process looks like this:

StageDefinitionWhat must be recordedMeasure
1. LeadA person or company has shown interest or fits your targetLogged in CRM with sourceResponse within one working day
2. QualifiedReal need, budget or funding, decision-maker known, timingQualifying call notes; next step agreedShare of leads qualified
3. Needs understoodRequirements, constraints and success criteria agreedDiscovery notes or site visit; scopeTime from lead to proposal
4. ProposalPrice and scope sentQuote or proposal in CRM; follow-up dateProposal win rate
5. NegotiationQuestions, changes and terms being settledRevised quote; decision dateDays in stage
6. Won or lostOrder received, or a clear noOrder entered; loss reason recordedWin rate; reasons for loss

Step 3: choose a few measures

Track new leads by source, conversion from stage to stage, average deal size, sales cycle length and win rate. Customer acquisition cost (what you spend on sales and marketing per new customer) is worth calculating once a quarter. Compare yourself with your own trend rather than general industry averages, which vary too widely to be useful.

Step 4: build follow-up into the process

Most lost deals are not lost to a competitor but to silence. Agree a follow-up rule for each stage, such as a call within two days of sending a proposal and a check-in every week until a decision, and let your CRM create the reminders.

Step 5: use technology to support it

A CRM holds the pipeline, reminders and history; email and calendar integration logs activity without typing; quoting tools speed up proposals. See choosing a CRM. Configure the CRM to match your stages, not the other way round.

Step 6: forecast and improve

Multiply the value of deals in each stage by the historical win rate from that stage to get a weighted forecast. Review the pipeline weekly and the process quarterly: which stage loses most deals, and why?

Worked example: finding the weak stage

An industrial equipment supplier reviews a year of deals: 240 leads, 120 qualified, 90 with needs understood, 60 proposals and 15 wins. The drop from proposal to win is the steepest: only one proposal in four is won. Loss reasons show most lost proposals went quiet after the quote was sent. The team adds a follow-up call within two days of every proposal and a short summary of the customer's success criteria at the top of each quote. If the win rate on proposals rises from 25% to 33%, the same pipeline produces about 20 wins instead of 15. The figures are illustrative.

Common mistakes

  • Stages that describe what the salesperson did rather than where the buyer is.
  • No loss reasons recorded, so nothing is learned from lost deals.
  • A CRM set up before the process is agreed.
  • Reviewing revenue only, not the stage conversion that drives it.

Qualifying questions that save time

Qualifying early stops salespeople spending weeks on deals that were never going to close. Simple frameworks such as BANT (budget, authority, need, timing) are a useful starting point; adapt the questions to how your customers buy.

TopicQuestion to askWhat a good answer sounds like
NeedWhat problem are you trying to solve, and what happens if you do nothing?A specific problem with a cost or deadline attached
DecisionWho else is involved in choosing, and how will the decision be made?Named people and a clear process
BudgetHave you set aside funds, or does this need approval?A budget range or a known approval route
TimingWhen do you need this in place, and why then?A date tied to an event, such as a shutdown or a season
FitWhat would make this project a success for you?Measurable success criteria you can meet

The hand-off between marketing and sales

Many leads are lost in the gap between marketing and sales. Agree in writing what counts as a lead ready for sales (for example, a quote request, or a contact from a target industry who has downloaded a guide and visited the pricing page), how fast sales will respond, and what happens to leads that are not ready yet. Review together each month how many leads were passed over and how many became qualified opportunities. See measuring content performance for the marketing side.

A 30-minute weekly pipeline review

  1. New this week (5 minutes): leads and opportunities added, by source.
  2. Deals that moved (10 minutes): what advanced or was won, and what helped.
  3. Deals that stalled (10 minutes): anything with no activity for two weeks; agree a next step or close it as lost.
  4. Forecast (5 minutes): weighted pipeline against target for the month and quarter.

Keep the meeting about decisions and next steps, not reading out the CRM. Over time, the stalled-deal discussion is where most of the process improvements come from.
