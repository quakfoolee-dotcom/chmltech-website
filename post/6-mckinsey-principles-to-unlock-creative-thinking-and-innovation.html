Six Practices to Build Creative Thinking and Innovation in a Small Business

Creative thinking and innovation are not reserved for large companies with research labs. In a small or mid-sized business, most useful innovation is practical: a better way to serve a customer, a new service built on existing skills, a product variant, a process that removes a common frustration. This article was first published in November 2025 under a title that attributed six principles to McKinsey & Company. We could not find a McKinsey source for that list, so the article has been rewritten and retitled; the six practices below are widely used approaches, described in our own words.

Six practices that build innovation

1. Start from the customer's problem

Design thinking is a structured way of doing this: understand the people you serve, define their problem precisely, generate many ideas, build quick prototypes and test them with real users. The discipline is in not jumping to solutions. A clear problem statement, such as "contractors cannot get a firm delivery date when they order", produces better ideas than "improve the customer experience".

2. Make curiosity part of leadership

Teams copy what leaders do. A manager who asks why, listens to the answer and changes course when the evidence says so gives others permission to do the same. One practical habit: in every review, ask what assumption the plan depends on most, and how it could be tested cheaply.

3. Mix perspectives

People who do the same job tend to see the same options. Bring together sales, operations, finance and someone who talks to customers daily. In a small firm, inviting a trusted customer or supplier to an idea session can be the most productive change of all.

4. Run small, cheap experiments

Most ideas fail, so the goal is to fail cheaply and learn quickly. Before building a new service, describe it on one page and ask five customers whether they would buy it. Before changing a process across the company, trial it with one team for two weeks. Set a clear success measure before the test starts.

5. Make ideas visible

A rough sketch, a storyboard of a customer's experience or a cardboard model gets better feedback than a description. Visible ideas are easier to criticize constructively and improve. Tools can be as simple as a whiteboard and sticky notes.

6. Work in short cycles

Agile methods, borrowed from software development, work for many kinds of improvement: agree a short goal, work on it for two to four weeks, review what was learned and decide the next step. Short cycles keep momentum and limit the cost of a wrong turn.

Worked example: a new service from existing skills

A small engineering firm hears the same request from several clients: help preparing documents for a regulatory audit. Instead of launching a new service line, the team writes a one-page offer, tests it with three existing clients at a reduced price, and documents what each engagement needed. After the three pilots, they have a standard checklist, a realistic price and two testimonials, and the new service is launched with evidence behind it. The example is illustrative; the pattern of problem, small test, learning and then scale is the point.

Protecting time for it

Innovation competes with daily work and usually loses. Protect a small, regular slot: an hour a month for the leadership team to review ideas, or a quarterly half-day workshop with a mixed group. Keep a simple idea log, decide on each idea within a month, and tell people what happened to theirs.

A simple idea log

An idea log keeps suggestions visible and makes sure each one gets a decision. A spreadsheet or a shared board is enough.

Measuring whether it is working

MeasureWhy it helps
Ideas raised per quarter, and by whomShows whether people across the business feel able to contribute
Share of ideas that get a decision within a monthShows whether ideas are taken seriously
Experiments run per quarterMeasures learning activity, not just talk
Revenue or savings from ideas launched in the last two yearsLinks innovation to results over a realistic period

Avoid judging the programme on a single big win. Most value comes from many small, tested improvements, and a few experiments that fail cheaply are a sign the process is working.

Common traps

  • Brainstorming without a problem. Idea sessions without a clear question produce lists that go nowhere.
  • The loudest voice wins. Have people write ideas down individually before discussing them.
  • Testing too late. Spending months polishing an idea before any customer has seen it.
  • No follow-through. People stop suggesting ideas once they see that nothing happens to them.

The fix for most of these is a small, steady routine rather than a big programme: a clear problem to work on, a short time each month to review ideas, a cheap test for the promising ones, and a visible record of what was decided and why.
