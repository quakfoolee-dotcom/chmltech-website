Management Review That Leadership Finds Useful

Quak Foo LeeNov 9, 20265 min read

Ask a leadership team what they think of the ISO 9001 management review and the usual answer is a polite grimace. Once a year the quality manager presents forty slides, everyone agrees the system is "suitable, adequate and effective", the minutes are filed for the auditor, and nothing changes.

That is a wasted opportunity. The review is the one point at which the standard requires top management to look at how the organization is performing and to decide what to do about it. Run as a decision meeting, it is among the most useful hours leaders spend.

What the standard requires

Clause 9.3 requires top management to review the quality management system at planned intervals, to ensure that it remains suitable, adequate, effective and aligned with the organization's strategic direction. The clause lists what the review must consider and what it must produce. Certification bodies report that this clause changed very little in the 2026 edition.

Required inputsQuestion it answers for leaders
Status of actions from previous reviewsDid we do what we decided?
Changes in external and internal issuesWhat has changed around us and inside the business?
Customer satisfaction and feedback from interested partiesWhat do customers and others think of us?
Extent to which quality objectives have been metAre we hitting our targets?
Process performance and conformity of products and servicesAre our processes delivering?
Nonconformities and corrective actionsWhat went wrong, and has it stopped?
Monitoring and measurement resultsWhat do the numbers show?
Audit resultsWhat did internal and external audits find?
Performance of external providersAre our suppliers reliable?
Adequacy of resourcesDo we have the people, equipment and budget we need?
Effectiveness of actions on risks and opportunitiesDid our precautions and initiatives work?
Opportunities for improvementWhat should we do next?

The required outputs are decisions and actions on three things: opportunities for improvement, any need for changes to the quality management system, and resource needs. You must keep a record of the results.

Why most reviews fail

  • They report and do not decide. Data is presented and noted. No one is asked to choose anything.
  • They happen once a year. By then the data is stale and the list is too long to discuss properly.
  • They belong to the quality manager. Leaders attend as an audience and not as owners.
  • They are separate from running the business. The same people discuss the same performance in a different meeting the following week.

Turn it into a decision meeting

Send the data beforehand. A one- or two-page pack with trends, issued a week ahead, lets the meeting start from questions. Show each measure as a trend against its target, and mark the items that need a decision.

Have process owners present their own results. The sales manager speaks to customer feedback, the production manager to process performance, the purchasing manager to suppliers. The quality manager coordinates and presents audit results.

Frame each item as a decision. "On-time delivery fell from 94% to 88% in the quarter; the main cause is late material from two suppliers. Options: dual-source, or hold more stock. Recommendation: dual-source." Leaders then choose.

Record decisions in the room. Each action gets an owner and a date before the meeting ends. That record is the evidence the standard asks for.

Review more often. "Planned intervals" does not mean annually. A quarterly review of 60 to 90 minutes works better than an annual half-day. You can also cover the inputs across your existing monthly management meetings, provided every input is addressed over the year and you keep records.

The agenda covers every required input in 90 minutes. If you are moving to the 2026 edition, add an item on transition progress; see the transition plan. Quality culture and ethical behaviour, which the new edition makes a responsibility of top management, also belong on this agenda once or twice a year.

What good minutes look like

Keep them to one page: date and attendees, the data pack attached, and a table of decisions with owner and date. An auditor will look for three things: that the required inputs were considered, that decisions were made, and that actions from the previous review were followed up. A long narrative adds nothing.

Signs the review is working

  • Leaders ask for the data pack when it is late.
  • Each review produces a small number of decisions that would not otherwise have been made.
  • Actions are closed by the next review.
  • Resource requests are argued from data and are sometimes approved.
  • The findings of internal audits and the risk register change as a result of the discussion.
