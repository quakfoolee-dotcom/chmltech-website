Communicating Goals and Performance: Scorecards and a Meeting Rhythm That Work

Quak Foo LeeMar 20, 2022Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Most communication problems in a growing business are not about people talking too little. They come from too many things being tracked in too many places, so nobody is sure what matters this week or whether the team is on track. This article replaces two earlier ones on this site, on communicating goals and performance and on closing team communication gaps, with one practical approach: a short list of goals, a weekly scorecard, and a regular meeting rhythm.

Step 1: agree a few goals, each with one owner

Three to five goals for the year are enough. Each needs a number, a date and one named owner. Ten goals with shared ownership produce activity but little progress. Break each yearly goal into a few 90-day priorities, because a quarter is long enough to finish something and short enough to stay focused.

Step 2: a weekly scorecard

A scorecard is a one-page table of five to fifteen numbers that tell you, each week, whether the business is on track. Each number has an owner and a target. The example below is for a small fabrication shop; the figures are illustrative.

The value of the scorecard is in what happens next: the two off-track numbers go on the issues list for the weekly meeting. Nobody needs to defend the others. 50 KPIs with formulas has ideas for which measures to choose.

Step 3: a meeting rhythm people can rely on

The weekly meeting does most of the work. Keep the same day, time and agenda every week: a quick check of the scorecard and quarterly priorities, updates on customer and staff issues, then most of the time spent solving the issues on the list, one at a time. Each solved issue ends with an action, an owner and a date. This structure follows the Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS), which we describe on our EOS page, but the same idea works with OKRs or any simple framework.

Step 4: make results visible

  • Share the scorecard with everyone it affects, not only managers. People work better when they can see the score.
  • Show trends, not just this week's figure, so one bad week does not cause panic.
  • Keep one source of truth: one scorecard, one issues list, one place for priorities.
  • Celebrate finished priorities at the end of each quarter.

Common mistakes

  • Measures nobody controls, or that change only monthly.
  • Meetings that report status but never solve anything.
  • Skipping the meeting in busy weeks, which are the weeks it matters most.
