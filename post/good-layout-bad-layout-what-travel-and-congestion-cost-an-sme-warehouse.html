Good Layout, Bad Layout: What Travel and Congestion Cost an SME Warehouse

Most small warehouses were never designed. The racks went up along the longest wall, packing went wherever there was room, and the dock doors were shared because that was how the building came. The layout was decided once, years ago. It is paid for every day, in walking, waiting and trucks that sit at the door.

The warehouse operations series fixed one thing at a time inside the same building: slotting, pick methods, the dock, counting. This series steps back and looks at the whole floor. Part 1 runs the same Vancouver distributor through two layouts of the same racks, side by side, and measures what layout alone costs.

What a layout decides

  • How far people walk. Aisle length, cross-aisles and where the fast movers sit set the walk for every order.
  • Where people and forklifts meet. Every crossing point is lost time and a safety risk.
  • How fast a truck is emptied. Door use and staging space decide whether unloading flows or stalls.
  • Where stock waits. Pallets parked in aisles block traffic and hide stock.
  • How much room there is to grow. A layout that is full on day one has no slack for a busy season.

Two layouts of the same building

The example is the distributor from the warehouse operations series: a 72 by 52 m building (about 40,300 sq ft) with 780 SKUs picked by the case from pallet racks, 300 orders a day and 14 inbound trucks. It is a receiving-heavy day, as in Part 3 of that series: about 190 pallets come in while about 15 pallets of orders go out, the kind of day when stock is built up ahead of a busy season. Both layouts hold about the same racks (780 and 840 pallet positions a level), the same five dock doors, the same crew, the same orders and the same trucks. Only the arrangement changes.

* Trucks still at a door or waiting at 15:00 are counted up to 15:00, so the as-is figures are a lower bound.

Four things stand out.

  • Walking is the cost. As-is, a picker walks about 233 m for a three-line order, because every line means a trip into a 65 m dead-end aisle and back out the same end. Walking is 66% of the picker-hours. The redesign cuts the walk to 70 m and the day from 32.9 to 17.7 picker-hours, 46% less.
  • Slotting does much of it, and costs least. Re-slotting the as-is racks alone takes the walk to 95 m and the day to 20.1 picker-hours: about 84% of the picking gain, with no rack moved. The share depends on the order of the changes. Build the new aisles first with random slotting and the day falls to 23.7 picker-hours; re-slotting then adds the last 39%. Either way, slotting is the cheapest step, so do it first.
  • Fewer meetings, mainly a safety gain. Give-way stops fall from 261 to 41 a shift. The time lost to them is small at this volume (about 4 minutes of picker time a day as-is), but each one is a moment when a person and a forklift, or two pickers with carts, share the same metre of aisle.
  • At the dock, the forklift is the bottleneck. As-is, the single forklift spends about 2.9 minutes on each pallet, most of it driving into 65 m dead-end aisles to random locations. That is about 9.1 hours of put-away for an 8-hour shift. Staging fills, unloading stops, trucks average 86 minutes on site, and 29 pallets are still waiting at 15:00. Shared doors were not the problem on this day: trucks waited under a minute for a door. Re-slotting alone brings the forklift's trip down to 2.4 minutes and clears the backlog; the redesign brings it to 2.2 minutes and 34 minutes a truck.

What it is worth

Picking labour a year is the picker-hours a day times the loaded wage and the working days:

\[C = H \times w \times d\]

where \(H\) is picker-hours a day, \(w\) is $40 an hour loaded and \(d\) is 250 days a year. As-is that is 32.9 × $40 × 250 = $329,000; redesigned, $177,000. The difference is about $152,000 a year, plus at least $52,000 of truck detention (charged at $65 an hour after two hours on site, as in Part 3 of the operations series).

Against that sits the one-off cost of the change: taking down and re-erecting racks, rack engineering and permits where required, relabelling, and the days of disruption while it happens. Part 5 of this series builds that payback, and shows when a better layout also frees cash tied up in stock.

The gap grows with volume. At 150 orders a day the redesign saves 7.9 picker-hours a day; at 600 it saves 30.9, the work of about 4 pickers on 7-hour shifts. The dock follows the same pattern: as-is, trucks average 113 minutes at 600 orders a day, even with a second forklift.

One simulated day is one sample. Across ten other days with different orders and truck times, the as-is layout needed 30.6 to 35.3 picker-hours and the redesign 17.2 to 18.2, so the picking result holds. The dock swings much more: as-is, trucks averaged 46 to 95 minutes on site and 8 to 34 pallets were left at 15:00, while the redesign stayed at 31 to 34 minutes with none left. Treat the detention figure as an order of magnitude, not a forecast.

Six faults to look for in your own building

Table 3. Layout faults, how to spot them and the first fix

FaultHow to spot itFirst fix
Dead-end aislesPickers walk into an aisle and come back out the same endCut a cross-aisle through the middle of long rows, or open the far end
Random slottingYour 50 busiest SKUs are spread across the buildingRe-slot by picks a day, busiest closest to packing (operations Part 1)
Crossing flowsForklifts and pickers use the same aisle to reach the same entrancesSend put-away in from the back or the far side; mark pedestrian lanes
Staging in aislesPallets parked "for now" in the main aislePaint staging lanes in the dock zone and keep the main aisle clear
Shared doors and lanesOutbound pallets wait where trucks unload; trucks queue for a door at peakAssign doors and lanes to receiving or shipping, at least at peak hours
Packing far from shippingEvery outbound pallet crosses the buildingMove packing next to shipping staging; packing benches move easily

Check your layout in one afternoon

  1. Draw the floor to scale. Racks, aisles, doors, packing, staging. Graph paper or a spreadsheet with one cell per metre is enough.
  2. Trace ten orders. Follow a picker and draw each trip on the plan: a spaghetti diagram. Measure the walk per order.
  3. Mark the crossings. Watch the main aisle for an hour and mark every time a person stops for a forklift or another person.
  4. Log the dock. For one day, note each truck's arrival, door time and departure, and when staging was full.
  5. Find your top 50 SKUs. Rank by picks a day (ABC analysis) and mark where they sit. If they are scattered, re-slot before anything else.
  6. Look for pallets that live in aisles. Each one is a missing staging lane or a slotting problem.

What the model leaves out

  • One shift, one fixed seed. A real day has more variation: late trucks, rush orders, absences.
  • Single-order picking from the floor level of the racks. Batch, zone and wave picking (operations Part 2) would cut walking in both layouts.
  • People and vehicles move on a 1 m grid and give way within a metre. Real congestion depends on aisle width, sight lines and site rules.
  • Replenishment of the pick faces, cycle counts and returns are not simulated.
  • A receiving-heavy day: far more pallets come in than go out, which loads the forklift. On a balanced day the dock gap would be smaller; the picking gap would not change.
  • The redesign keeps the same building, about the same racks and the same number of doors. It does not count the cost of moving them.

Key points

  • The same building and about the same racks can need about half the picker-hours with a different layout: 32.9 against 17.7 a day in the example.
  • Slotting is the cheapest step and a large part of the picking gain. Start there.
  • At the dock, long put-away trips make the forklift the bottleneck. Shorter trips clear the backlog before more doors would.
  • Separating flows cuts the moments when people and forklifts share an aisle, which is a safety gain as much as a time gain.
  • Measure first: a spaghetti diagram of ten orders and a day's dock log tell you which fault you have.
