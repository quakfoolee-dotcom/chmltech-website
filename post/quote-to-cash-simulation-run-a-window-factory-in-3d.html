Quote-to-Cash Simulation: Run a Window Factory in 3D

Quak Foo LeeNov 14, 20264 min read

Part 1 mapped the quote-to-cash process at Example Windows Co., a made-up window maker. An order takes 66 days to become cash, and about $1.06 million sits in open orders. This post lets you test changes to that process before you spend money on them.

The model below is a working simulation. Orders arrive, wait, pass through five machines and get paid. The results change when you change the settings.

The simulator

Every tile or window frame is one customer order worth $20,000. Its colour shows where the money is. Start with the buttons in the Try row, then move the sliders.

Open the simulator on its own page for a larger view.

How to read the floor

The floor follows the order of the process. Work starts at the back right, runs through the line in the middle and ends at the bank at the front left.

Table 1. What the colours mean

ColourWhere the money isWhere you see it
GreyQuoted, nothing spentQuote desk and the customer's decision
OrangeCash spent, not billedMaterials, the line, delivery and delivered orders waiting for an invoice
PurpleBilled, not paidThe block of unpaid invoices
GreenPaidTiles moving to the bank

The purple block is the largest group on the floor. At the starting settings it holds about 64 orders, or $1.28 million of unpaid invoices.

Each machine has a light: green when it is running, yellow when it is waiting, blue during a changeover and red when it has stopped for repair.

Three experiments

1. Invoice on delivery day

Click Invoice on delivery day. The pad of delivered orders waiting for an invoice empties. Quote to cash falls from 66 to 61 days and about $96,000 of cash is freed. No machine changed.

2. Halve the batch size

Click Halve the batch size. Orders stop waiting for a batch of four, so they reach the line sooner. Deliveries on time rise from 80% to 97%. Quote to cash moves by one day and the cash tied up barely changes.

3. Machine trouble

Click Machine trouble. Uptime drops from 90% to 75% and window frames queue in front of the first machine. Only 15% of orders now arrive on time. Quote to cash rises by just 3 days.

Table 2. Results of the experiments

SettingQuote to cashCash tied upDelivered on time
Baseline66 days$1,061,00080%
Invoice on delivery day61 days$966,00080%
Halve the batch size65 days$1,049,00097%
Machine trouble69 days$1,098,00015%
Fix the office50 days$386,00083%

The pattern is consistent. The factory decides whether the customer gets the order on time. The office decides how long the money takes. Fix the office combines a one-day quote, same-day invoicing, follow-up of late payers and a 30% deposit, and it frees about $675,000 without touching a machine.

What the model assumes

Table 3. Inputs of the model

ItemValue in the model
OrdersAbout 1.6 a day, each 20 windows worth $20,000
Costs$8,000 of materials bought when the order is confirmed, $4,000 of labour added on the line
Customer's decisionAbout 3 days after the quote is sent
Order entry and materialsAbout 7 days, varying from 5 to 9 with the supplier
The lineFive machines, 0.4 of a day per order each, with room for two orders between machines
StoppagesRandom, about 2 hours to repair
BatchesOrders wait for a full batch. The first machine has one changeover per batch.
Delivery2 days, promised within 15 days of the order
Payment30-day terms, paid about 10 days late on average

The model has limits. It shows won orders only, so it cannot tell you how many more quotes a faster answer would win. It ignores weekends and bad debts. Each change restarts the model and runs one year in the background, so the results are settled averages. They come from a random simulation and move a little from run to run.

Use your own numbers

Set the office sliders to your own quote turnaround, invoice lag, payment terms, late days and deposit. The order value and costs stay those of the example. Read the direction and the relative size of each change, not the exact dollars.

Takeaway. Test a change in the model before paying for it. In this example the office settings move the cash, and the factory settings move the delivery promise.
