Material Requirements Planning (MRP) Explained with a Worked Example

Material requirements planning (MRP) works out which parts and materials you need, how many, and when to order or start making them, so that finished products can be built on time without holding piles of stock. It was developed in the 1960s and 1970s and is still the core of every manufacturing ERP. This guide, first published in 2023, has been rewritten around a worked example, because MRP is easiest to understand by doing one by hand.

Dependent and independent demand

The key idea behind MRP is the difference between two kinds of demand. Independent demand comes from customers for finished products and must be forecast or taken from orders. Dependent demand is for the parts and materials that go into those products; it does not need to be forecast, because it can be calculated from the production plan and the bill of materials. Reorder-point methods treat every item as if its demand were independent, which leads to shortages of some parts and excess of others. MRP calculates dependent demand exactly.

The three inputs

  • Master production schedule: how many of each finished product you plan to make, and when.
  • Bill of materials (BOM): the parts and quantities that go into each product, level by level.
  • Inventory records: what is on hand and already on order, plus each item's lead time and ordering rule.

How the bill of materials is structured

A bill of materials is arranged in levels. Level 0 is the finished product; level 1 holds the parts and sub-assemblies that go directly into it; level 2 holds what goes into those, and so on. MRP processes one level at a time, from the top down, so that every item's total requirement, from all the products that use it, is known before its orders are planned. An item used at several levels is planned at its lowest level, which software handles with a "low-level code".

The gross-to-net logic

  1. Gross requirements: total demand for the item in each period, from customer orders or from parent items.
  2. Scheduled receipts: orders already placed and due in.
  3. Projected on hand: stock carried forward from the last period, plus receipts, minus requirements.
  4. Net requirements: what is still short after stock and receipts are used.
  5. Planned order receipts: net requirements rounded to the ordering rule (exact quantity, fixed lot, minimum).
  6. Planned order releases: the same quantities moved earlier by the lead time; these become requirements for the components one level down.

Worked example: a stool and its legs

A workshop makes stools. Each stool needs one seat and three legs. The master schedule calls for 40 stools in week 2, 60 stools in week 4, 50 stools in week 6. There are 30 finished stools in stock, and assembly takes 1 week. Legs are bought in lots of 150 with a 2-week lead time, and 100 are in stock.

MRP works top-down. First the stool: subtract stock from demand to get net requirements, then offset each planned order by the lead time to find when it must be released.

Comparing lot-sizing rules

For the legs, with an ordering cost of $50 per order and a holding cost of 0.05 dollars per leg per week (both illustrative), three ordering rules over the six weeks compare as follows.

RuleOrders placedLeg-weeks held in stockOrdering + holding cost
Lot for lot2140$107
Fixed lot of 1502300$115
Fixed lot of 3001600$80

Ordering exactly what is needed holds the least stock. One lot of 300 needs only one order but carries the most stock through the six weeks. With these costs the single large order is cheapest, because holding a leg costs little; for a bulky or expensive part, or one that can become obsolete, the answer flips. The right rule depends on the two costs and on practical limits such as supplier minimums, storage space and shelf life.

Choices that change the plan

  • Lot sizing: ordering exactly what is needed keeps stock lowest; fixed lots or economic order quantities reduce ordering and set-up costs. See reorder point and EOQ.
  • Safety stock: a buffer for items with unreliable supply or demand. See setting safety stock.
  • Lead times: the most common source of bad plans. If the system says 2 weeks and the supplier takes 4, every plan will be late.
  • Capacity: basic MRP assumes unlimited capacity. Check the plan against machine and labour hours, or use a system with capacity planning.

Capacity planning and MRP II

Basic MRP assumes that any planned order can be made in its time slot. Capacity requirements planning (CRP) adds up the hours that planned and released orders need at each work centre, period by period, so overloads show before they happen. When MRP was extended in the 1980s to include capacity, shop floor control and the financial side of the plan, it became manufacturing resource planning (MRP II), the direct ancestor of today's ERP systems.

Exception messages

An MRP system re-plans every time something changes and then tells planners what to do through exception messages: release an order, expedite one (bring it in), de-expedite one (push it out), or cancel one. Working the exception list every day is the planner's core job. A list with hundreds of messages usually means bad data or a master schedule that changes too often.

Why MRP plans go wrong

  1. Inaccurate inventory records. MRP is only as good as the stock count; aim for 95% record accuracy or better.
  2. BOMs that do not match how the product is actually built.
  3. A master schedule that changes every day, so the plan never settles. Freeze the near term.
  4. People overriding the plan with their own spreadsheets because they do not trust it.

For the software side, see ERP vs MRP and inventory and MRP software for small businesses.

Implementing MRP in a small manufacturer

StepWhat to do
1. Clean the item masterOne part number per item; units of measure; lead times checked with suppliers
2. Build accurate BOMsWalk the shop floor to confirm how products are really built; include packaging and consumables that matter
3. Fix inventory accuracyCycle count high-value and fast-moving items weekly; aim for 95% or better before go-live
4. Set planning rulesLot sizes, safety stocks and order minimums by item, starting simple
5. Run in parallelCompare the MRP plan with what planners would have done for a few weeks
6. Go live by product familyStart with one family; expand once the plan is trusted
