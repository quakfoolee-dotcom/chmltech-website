Automation ROI for SME Warehouses: Robots, Pick-to-Light and Vertical Lifts

The trade shows are full of robots. Mobile robots glide between racks, towers of trays deliver parts to a waist-high window, and every pick face glows green. The brochures promise two or three times the productivity and payback inside two years. For a warehouse of 15 people, the question is simpler: will it pay here, and when?

This last part of the series answers that for the same distributor as Parts 1 to 7, now running a cloud WMS (Part 7). Every automation below needs one: the robots, lights and lifts take their orders from it. The answer depends mostly on volume, and on whether the floor space matters.

Three automations an SME can actually buy

The prices sit inside published ranges. Pick-to-light hardware runs about $150 a light device, which is why it is usually limited to a subset of locations. Vertical lift modules typically cost $6,500 to $8,500 a linear foot of height, and vendors quote space savings of up to 85% against shelving. Robots as a service typically costs $2,000 to $8,000 a robot a month, and vendors quote two to three times the picking productivity with collaborative robots.

What each one saves

At 300 orders a day the example picks about 450 case lines and 450 small-item lines a day. With scanners and a WMS, a case line takes about 1.0 minutes and a small-item line about 1.1 minutes, most of it walking and finding. Each automation cuts part of that:

  • Pick-to-light cuts the time on the lit locations by 35%, within the 30% to 50% gain usually quoted, and halves the errors there.
  • Vertical lift modules cut small-item time by 45%, because the tray comes to the picker, and free about 85% of the shelving floor: about 476 m².
  • Mobile robots halve the time on case lines: the picker stops walking with a cart. Each robot handles about 70 lines an hour.

\[\text{Payback (years)} = \dfrac{\text{one-time cost}}{\text{labour saved} + \text{errors avoided} + \text{space freed} - \text{running cost}}\]

Labour is valued at $40 an hour, as in Parts 5 to 7. Freed floor is valued at $14.77 a square foot a year, the Canadian industrial rent used in Part 1, but only if the business needs the space: if it saves a move or an extension. Floor that sits empty saves nothing.

At today's volume, nothing pays back within three years. That is the honest answer for this warehouse, and for many SMEs. The example saves 1.9 hours a day with pick-to-light, 3.7 with the lifts and 3.8 with robots, a fraction of one person each, because the work is spread across a small team on one shift.

Robots cost more than they save. Three robots at $2,500 a month are $90,000 a year. They save $38,000 in labour. At 300 orders a day one robot would be enough for the work, but vendors set a minimum fleet, and on one shift the robots stand idle for two-thirds of the day.

Vertical lifts pay if the space is needed. At 4.0 years, the lifts come closest, but $76,000 of their $114,000 yearly saving is floor rent. If the business is not short of space, the payback stretches past ten years. If it is about to sign a lease on a second building, the lifts are worth a serious look.

Pick-to-light is cheap but has little to save. At $85,000 it is the cheapest to buy, but it only speeds up 292 lines a day.

When each one pays

Figure 3 shows the same calculation across volumes. Savings grow with every order, while most costs do not, so every option gets better with growth:

  • Pick-to-light pays back within three years from about 540 orders a day.
  • Vertical lift modules do so from about 560 if the space is needed, and only from about 1,140 if it is not.
  • Mobile robots do so from about 830, when the minimum fleet is fully used. Above that their payback keeps falling quickly, because the fleet grows only as fast as the work.

Before you automate

The earlier parts of this series cost far less than any of these machines, and each one makes automation cheaper later:

PartProblemLow-cost fix
1Out of spaceRe-beam the racks, combine partial pallets, clear dead stock and re-slot by velocity
2Pickers walk half the dayBatch and zone picking with better routes
3Trucks wait at the dockDock appointments, scanning at receiving and cross-docking
4Phantom stockCycle counting by ABC class
5Errors reach customersScan at pick and pack, weigh at ship
6Panic at the peakForecast in hours, planned temps and a cross-trained crew
7Spreadsheets stop scalingThe right system for the volume: the ERP module or a cloud WMS

Automation on a disorganised floor automates the disorder. Robots need clean locations and a WMS; lifts need a clean item master and slotting; lights need accurate stock.

How to buy automation without regret

  • Start from your own numbers. Lines a day by type, minutes a line, wage, space cost and growth. A vendor's case study is someone else's warehouse.
  • Test the payback at today's volume and at your growth plan. If it only pays at three times today's volume, wait or rent.
  • Rent before you buy. Robots as a service and short pilots let you prove the gain on your floor; ask for 60 to 90 days with an exit.
  • Count the floor only if you need it. Freed space is worth rent only if it saves a move or an extension.
  • Plan for the second shift. Machines that stand idle for 16 hours a day pay back slowly. A second shift can halve the payback.
  • Check the WMS link first. The integration between the automation and the WMS is where projects go late.

Common mistakes

  • Believing "two to three times the productivity" applies to the whole warehouse. It applies to the work the machine touches. In the example, robots touch case picking only.
  • Ignoring the minimum fleet. A small warehouse pays for robots it cannot keep busy.
  • Counting freed floor that will sit empty.
  • Automating before fixing. Slotting, batching and scanning are cheaper and often enough.
  • Forgetting maintenance and support. Lights, lifts and robots need spares, service contracts and someone on site who understands them.

Key points

  • Automation pays when there is enough work for it. Most savings grow with volume; most costs do not.
  • At the example's 300 orders a day, none of the three options pays back within three years.
  • Pick-to-light pays from about 540 orders a day, vertical lifts from about 560 if the space is needed, and mobile robots from about 830.
  • Rent and pilot before you buy, and count freed floor only if you need it.
  • Fix the process first. The seven earlier parts of this series cost far less and make automation work better later.
