Warehouse Automation for Small and Mid-Sized Operations: What to Automate First

Quak Foo LeeApr 7, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

Warehouse automation ranges from barcode scanners to fully automated storage systems. For small and mid-sized distributors and manufacturers, the right step is rarely the most advanced technology; it is the one that removes the biggest cost or error in the current operation. This article, first written in 2023, has been rewritten as a step-by-step guide with a worked payback example.

Five levels of automation

LevelExamplesFits
1. BasicsBarcode scanning, a WMS or inventory system, labelled locations, slotting by velocityMost small warehouses; low cost
2. Assisted pickingPick-to-light, put-to-light, voice picking, batch carts, mobile printersHigh order lines per day; many small items
3. Mobile robotsAutonomous mobile robots (AMRs) that carry totes or follow pickersLong walking distances; growing volumes
4. Goods-to-personShuttle or cube storage systems that bring bins to a picking stationDense storage, very high volumes, small parts
5. Full automationAutomated storage and retrieval, sortation, robotic pickingLarge, stable volumes; major capital

Skipping level 1 is the most common and expensive mistake. Robots and goods-to-person systems depend on accurate inventory records, clean item data and a warehouse management system. Without them, automation makes errors faster.

Where the time goes in a typical warehouse

In manual picking, much of a picker's time is spent walking between locations rather than picking; the exact share depends on layout and order profile, so measure it by timing a sample of picks. Searching, checking paperwork and correcting errors take much of the rest. That is why the first gains usually come from slotting fast movers near the dispatch area, batching orders, and scanning to confirm picks, all before any robot arrives.

Worked example: are mobile robots worth it?

A distributor has 6 pickers, each costing about $52,000 a year with benefits. Timing studies show they spend about 50% of their time walking. Collaborative mobile robots that carry totes between pick zones and the packing area could cut picker labour time by about 35% of that walking, saving roughly $54,600 a year, or allowing the same team to handle more volume. A small fleet with software and integration costing about $180,000 would pay back in about 3.3 years. Many vendors also offer robots as a subscription, which changes the decision from capital to operating cost. The figures are illustrative; the timing study is what makes the case.

A roadmap for a growing distributor

StageTypical volumeInvestments
Getting controlUp to a few hundred order lines a dayInventory system or WMS, barcode labels and scanners, labelled locations, cycle counting
Getting fasterSeveral hundred to a few thousand lines a daySlotting by velocity, batch and zone picking, pick carts, mobile printers, pick-to-light for fast movers
Scaling labourThousands of lines a day, labour hard to hireCollaborative mobile robots, conveyor to packing, automated packing aids
Dense and fastHigh volume of small items, space constrainedGoods-to-person storage, sortation

Leasing, subscription or purchase

Robots-as-a-service subscriptions let you start with a small fleet, add units for peak season and avoid a large capital outlay, at a higher cost over many years. Purchase suits stable, predictable volumes and businesses that can maintain equipment. Fixed systems such as goods-to-person storage are almost always capital projects and need a longer planning horizon, so check your lease, building and growth plans before committing.

Safety and people

Automation changes how people and machines share space. Update traffic plans, train staff on how robots behave and how to stop them, and review your health and safety program with your provincial requirements in mind. Pickers who move to robot supervision, exception handling or quality checks are often the best source of improvement ideas for the new system.

Questions to answer before buying

  • What are your order lines per day, peak versus average, and how fast are they growing?
  • How many SKUs, and how many account for most picks?
  • How accurate is your inventory today, and is it in a WMS?
  • What is the building like: ceiling height, floor quality, aisle widths, Wi-Fi coverage?
  • Will the system scale down in a slow year, or is it a fixed cost?
  • Who will maintain it, and what is the vendor's support in your region?

Implementation tips

  1. Fix the basics first: locations, scanning, inventory accuracy and slotting.
  2. Simulate with your own order data before committing; good vendors will.
  3. Pilot one zone or one shift and measure lines per hour, accuracy and safety.
  4. Involve the pickers early; they know where time is lost and will make or break adoption.
  5. Plan for peak: design for the busy season, not the average week.
