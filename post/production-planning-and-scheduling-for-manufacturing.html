Production Planning and Scheduling for Manufacturers: Planning Levels and Scheduling Rules

Production planning decides what to make and when; scheduling decides the order of jobs on each machine or line, day by day. Done well, they let a manufacturer promise realistic dates, keep inventory down and avoid the daily firefight of expediting. This article, first published in 2021, has been rewritten with the planning levels most manufacturers use and a worked example comparing common scheduling rules.

Four levels of planning

Small manufacturers often skip straight to the shop floor schedule. That works until demand changes, and then every day becomes a re-plan. Even a simple monthly sales and operations plan and a weekly master schedule make the daily schedule far calmer.

Make-to-stock, make-to-order and engineer-to-order

StrategyWhat triggers productionPlanning focus
Make-to-stockForecast and stock levelsForecast accuracy, finished goods safety stock, batch sizes
Make-to-orderA customer order for a standard productQuoted lead times, component availability, capacity
Assemble-to-orderA customer order, built from stocked sub-assembliesSub-assembly stock and final assembly capacity
Engineer-to-orderA customer order that needs design workEngineering capacity, long-lead materials, project milestones

Many small manufacturers run a mix. Plan each product family according to its strategy rather than forcing one approach on everything.

Inputs a schedule needs

  • Orders and forecasts, with due dates the customer actually needs.
  • Routings: the steps each job goes through and the time each takes, including set-up.
  • Capacity: machine and labour hours available, after planned maintenance and holidays.
  • Material availability, from the material plan and purchasing. See the MRP guide.
  • The constraint: schedule the bottleneck first and the other steps around it. The theory of constraints explains why.

Checking load against capacity

Before sequencing jobs, check that next week's work fits. The table compares the hours of work scheduled at each work centre with the hours available; the figures are illustrative.

Work centreHours of workHours availableLoad
Laser cutting3237.585%
Press brake4437.5117%
Welding607580%
Paint2037.553%

The press brake is loaded at 117%, so the schedule cannot be met as it stands. The options are overtime at the press brake, moving some jobs a week later, or splitting jobs so the most urgent part ships on time. Planning with finite capacity like this avoids promising dates the shop cannot meet.

Forward and backward scheduling

Forward scheduling starts each job as soon as possible and calculates when it will finish; it shows the earliest date you can promise. Backward scheduling starts from the due date and works back through the routing to find the latest start; it keeps inventory and work in progress low but leaves no slack. Many shops schedule backward from due dates and then check the result forward against capacity.

Worked example: which job goes first?

Five jobs wait for one machine. Job A takes 6 days and is due on day 8; B takes 2 days, due day 6; C takes 8, due day 18; D takes 3, due day 15; E takes 9, due day 23. Three common rules give different results.

RuleOrderAverage days in the systemJobs lateTotal days lateWorst lateness (days)
First come, first servedA → B → C → D → E15.43 of 5115
Shortest job firstB → D → A → C → E13.03 of 595
Earliest due dateB → A → D → C → E13.62 of 565

Shortest job first gets jobs out of the door fastest on average, which keeps work in progress low. Earliest due date makes the fewest customers wait past their date. First come, first served feels fair but gives the longest average time and the most total lateness here. In practice many shops use earliest due date as the default, then adjust for set-up: running similar jobs together can save more time than any sequencing rule.

Set-up and sequencing

Where changeovers are long, the order of jobs matters as much as the rule. Grouping jobs by material, colour, thickness or tooling into families, and running light-to-dark or thin-to-thick sequences, can save hours a week. Reducing the changeover itself, through a set-up reduction Kaizen, makes smaller batches and shorter lead times affordable.

A weekly planning meeting

Thirty minutes each week with sales, production, purchasing and the planner keeps the schedule real: review last week's adherence and misses, confirm next week's load against capacity, flag material shortages, and agree any priority changes before the frozen window starts.

Making the schedule stick

  1. Freeze the next few days. Changes inside the frozen window need the production manager's approval.
  2. Publish one schedule that everyone works from, on a board or screen at the line.
  3. Track schedule adherence (jobs completed on the planned day) and on-time delivery weekly.
  4. Review misses for causes: material late, machine down, wrong routing time. Fix the cause, not just the date.

Handling rush orders

Every shop gets urgent orders. The damage comes from accepting them without seeing what they push out. A simple rule helps: before saying yes, check the load at the constraint for the days affected, name the jobs that will move, and get agreement from whoever owns those customers. Some shops keep a small amount of capacity unscheduled each week, perhaps five to ten percent at the constraint, for genuine rush work. If rush orders are routine, the real problem is usually quoted lead times that are too long or a forecast that misses predictable demand.
