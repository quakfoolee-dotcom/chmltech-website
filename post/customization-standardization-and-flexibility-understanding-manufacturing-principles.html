Make-to-Stock, Assemble-to-Order, Make-to-Order or Engineer-to-Order? Choosing a Manufacturing Strategy

Quak Foo LeeMar 27, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

Every manufacturer has to decide how much work to do before a customer orders and how much after. Doing more in advance gives faster delivery but ties up money in stock that might not sell. Doing more after the order allows customization but makes customers wait. This trade-off between standardization and flexibility sits behind the four classic manufacturing strategies. This article, first written in 2023, has been rewritten with a figure, a comparison table and a worked example.

The four strategies

StrategyHow it worksStrengthMain challengeTypical products
Make-to-stock (MTS)Finished products built to a forecast and shipped from stockFast delivery; standard products; steady demandInventory cost and obsolescence; forecast errorsFasteners, consumer goods, standard spare parts
Assemble- or configure-to-order (ATO/CTO)Stocked sub-assemblies combined to the customer's choiceVariety with short lead timesManaging option rules and sub-assembly stockPumps with motor options, furniture, computers
Make-to-order (MTO)Standard designs built after the orderLittle finished stock; some customizationLonger lead times; capacity planning mattersFabricated parts, industrial equipment, printing
Engineer-to-order (ETO)Designed and built for each orderFully custom solutionsLongest lead times; engineering is often the bottleneckProcess skids, special machinery, structural steel

The decoupling point

The clearest way to see the difference is to ask where the customer order enters the process, sometimes called the order penetration or decoupling point. Everything before it is done to a forecast; everything after it is done to the order.

Moving the decoupling point is one of the most powerful choices a manufacturer can make. Moving it later (towards make-to-stock) shortens lead times but needs better forecasts and more stock. Moving it earlier (towards make-to-order) cuts stock but needs more flexible capacity and shorter internal lead times.

Standardize the parts, customize the product

The best manufacturers do not choose between standardization and flexibility; they combine them. Standard modules, common parts across product families and a limited menu of options let customers get what feels like a custom product, while the plant builds mostly standard components to stock. This is the logic of assemble-to-order, and it is often the most profitable move for a small make-to-order or engineer-to-order shop.

Worked example: moving from make-to-order to assemble-to-order

A manufacturer of small industrial pumps builds every unit to order, with a lead time of six weeks, most of it waiting for machined housings and motors. Analysing two years of orders shows that 80% use one of four housing sizes and three motor ratings. The company decides to stock machined housings and the common motors, and to assemble and test to order. Lead time for the common configurations falls to about one week; unusual configurations stay at six weeks. The extra stock is a few weeks of the most common parts, worth far less than the orders previously lost to competitors with shorter lead times. The figures are illustrative.

Choosing a strategy for each product family

  • How long will customers wait? If the market expects delivery faster than your total lead time, some work must be done before the order.
  • How predictable is demand? Stable, high-volume items suit stock; erratic, low-volume ones do not.
  • How many variants? Many variants of a few common parts point to assemble-to-order.
  • What does stock cost? Bulky, expensive or perishable items favour building later.
  • Where is your bottleneck? In engineer-to-order shops it is often engineering, so standard designs and pre-engineered modules save the most time.

Most small manufacturers run more than one strategy. Plan each product family on its own terms; see production planning and scheduling and setting safety stock for how this flows into the plan.

What it means for systems

Each strategy needs different things from your planning software. Make-to-stock leans on forecasting and replenishment. Assemble-to-order needs product configurators and planning bills of materials. Make-to-order and engineer-to-order need job costing, capacity planning and project-style tracking. When choosing an MRP or ERP system, test it against your actual mix of strategies.

Signs your strategy no longer fits

  • Lead times are longer than customers will accept, and you lose quotes on delivery rather than price.
  • Finished stock keeps growing while you are still short of the items customers want.
  • Engineering is always behind, because every order is treated as new even when it is similar to past ones.
  • Expediting is daily, a sign that work is started too late in the process for the lead time you promise.
  • Margins vary widely between similar jobs, often because custom work is priced like standard work.

Making the change

  1. Analyse two years of orders by product family: volume, variety, lead time quoted and achieved.
  2. Find the common parts and modules that appear in most orders.
  3. Pick the decoupling point for each family and decide which items to hold in stock and at what levels.
  4. Standardize the options customers can choose, and price custom work separately.
  5. Update the planning system: stocking rules, planning bills of materials and quoted lead times.
  6. Review quarterly: demand shifts, and the right decoupling point shifts with it.
