Lead-to-Cash: Mapping and Improving the Process from Enquiry to Payment

Quak Foo LeeMar 11, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

Lead-to-cash is the end-to-end process that turns interest into money in the bank: capturing a lead, qualifying it, quoting, taking the order, delivering, invoicing and collecting payment. It crosses marketing, sales, operations and finance, which is exactly why it leaks. Each team optimizes its own part, and the hand-offs between them lose time, information and sometimes the customer. This article was first written in 2023 about software companies that sell online without salespeople. It has been rewritten for any small or mid-sized business, with a section on that self-serve software model and a worked example.

The seven steps

Some companies split it into two linked processes: lead-to-order (marketing and sales) and order-to-cash (operations and finance). Looking at the whole chain matters because a decision in one part, such as non-standard payment terms agreed to win a deal, creates work and delay in another.

Where the process usually leaks

StepCommon leakTypical fix
LeadEnquiries not logged or answered lateResponse within one working day; every lead in the CRM
QualifyTime spent on poor-fit prospectsAgreed qualifying questions; disqualify early and politely
QuoteSlow quotes; prices or terms wrongPrice list and templates; approval only above a threshold
OrderOrder details re-keyed; missing informationQuote converts to order in one system; required fields
DeliverDates missed; partial shipments not communicatedRealistic promise dates; automatic shipping notices
InvoiceInvoices late, wrong or disputedInvoice on shipment or milestone automatically; match to PO
CashSlow collection; payments not appliedClear terms, reminders before and after due date, online payment

Measures for the whole chain

MeasureWhat it tells you
Lead response timeHow quickly a new enquiry gets a real reply
Lead-to-order conversionShare of qualified leads that become orders
Quote turnaroundDays from request to quote sent
Order-to-invoice timeDays from delivery to invoice; any gap is free credit to the customer
Invoice accuracyShare of invoices not disputed or corrected
Days sales outstanding (DSO)How long customers take to pay
Lead-to-cash cycle timeDays from first enquiry to payment received

Worked example: a distributor's order-to-cash

A distributor ships orders on the day they are packed, but invoices are raised in a weekly batch by the office, and statements go out monthly. Mapping the process shows invoices are issued an average of 4 days after shipment and customers pay, on average, 41 days after invoice. Two changes follow: invoices are created automatically when the shipment is confirmed in the system, and a polite reminder goes out five days before the due date with an online payment link. Days from shipment to cash fall from about 45 to about 34. On annual sales of $6 million, cutting 11 days frees roughly $180,822 of cash that was sitting with customers. The figures are illustrative.

The self-serve software model

Software-as-a-service companies that sell online without salespeople run the same chain, but most steps are automated and the customer moves through it alone: sign up for a trial, set the product up, choose a plan, pay by card, and renew. The leaks are in activation and retention rather than in quoting and invoicing. A simple example, with illustrative numbers:

StageCustomersConversion from previous stage
Trial sign-ups1,200—
Activated (completed setup)54045%
Converted to paid10820%
Still paying after 12 months8175%

Here the biggest loss is between sign-up and activation: more than half of trial users never complete setup, so they never see the value. Better onboarding (a guided first task, a short email sequence, a check-in at day three) usually does more for revenue than more sign-ups. After that, failed card payments and silent churn matter: automatic retries, reminders before a card expires and a quick check-in when usage drops all keep cash coming in.

Systems that support lead-to-cash

Most small businesses run lead-to-cash across a CRM, an accounting package and sometimes an ERP or e-commerce platform. The goal is to enter each piece of information once and let it flow: the CRM quote becomes the order, the order drives shipping, shipping triggers the invoice, and the payment updates the customer's record. Where systems do not connect, an integration often pays back quickly; see APIs for small businesses and choosing a CRM.

How to improve it

  1. Map the full chain with one person from each team, using a swim lane map so hand-offs are visible.
  2. Measure cycle time for a sample of recent orders, from enquiry to payment.
  3. Fix the longest wait first, which is often invoicing delay or slow quotes, not selling.
  4. Agree rules across teams, such as standard payment terms and who can approve exceptions.
  5. Review the measures monthly in one meeting with sales, operations and finance together.
