Sizing a Line: Velocity, Pressure Drop and Pipe Schedule, Worked Step by Step

Pipe is the least glamorous item on a plant and the one there is most of. Each line is a small trade: a thin pipe is cheap to buy and expensive to pump through for the next twenty years, and a fat pipe is the reverse. Line sizing is how that trade is settled, one line at a time, with three numbers: the velocity, the pressure drop and the wall thickness.

Part 1 of this series sized pump P-101 and simply stated that its discharge line was 1½-inch and its suction line 2-inch. This part shows where those sizes come from. The example is the discharge line from P-101 through the control valve of Part 4 and heater E-101 to reactor R-101, carrying 6,250 kg/h of feed.

Step 1: start from the flow and the fluid

Lines are sized at the same rated flow as the equipment they connect: 8.09 m³/h, 10% above the normal 7.35 m³/h. The feed has a density of 850 kg/m³ and a viscosity of 0.60 mPa·s. Those four numbers and a pipe table are all the calculation needs.

One trap comes first. A pipe's nominal size is a name, not a dimension. A 1½-inch schedule 40 pipe has an outside diameter of 48.3 mm, a wall of 3.68 mm and an inside diameter of 40.9 mm. The liquid only knows about the inside diameter, so that is the one to use.

Step 2: set the criteria

Most companies size liquid lines against two guidelines at once, a velocity range and a pressure drop per 100 m of pipe. Typical values for a thin liquid are:

ServiceVelocityPressure dropWhy
Pump suction0.5 to 1.5 m/sUnder about 10 kPa per 100 mProtects NPSH; every kilopascal lost here is taken from the pump's suction margin
Pump discharge1.5 to 3 m/s20 to 90 kPa per 100 mBalances pipe cost against pump head and energy
Gravity flow and drainsUnder 1 m/sSet by the head availableOnly the height difference drives the flow

Too fast, and the line wastes pump head, erodes at elbows, makes noise and hammers when a valve shuts. Too slow, and the plant pays for steel it does not need, and anything that can settle will settle. The ranges are guidelines, not law, and they shift for slurries, corrosive liquids and very large pipe.

Step 3: try the standard sizes

Pipe comes in fixed sizes, so the method is trial: take each candidate, work out the velocity, then the pressure drop. Velocity is the flow divided by the bore area:

\[v = \dfrac{Q}{A} = \dfrac{4Q}{\pi D^{2}}\]

The Reynolds number then says what kind of flow it is. Below about 2,100 the flow is laminar and slides in smooth layers. Above about 4,000 it is turbulent and full of eddies. Almost every line carrying a thin liquid in a plant is turbulent.

\[Re = \dfrac{\rho\, v\, D}{\mu}\]

The friction factor \(f\) depends on the Reynolds number and on the roughness of the pipe wall \(\varepsilon\), here 0.045 mm for commercial steel. For laminar flow it is simply \(f = 64 / Re\). For turbulent flow the Swamee–Jain equation gives it directly, within a few percent of the Moody chart:

\[f = \dfrac{0.25}{\left[\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{3.7\,D} + \dfrac{5.74}{Re^{0.9}}\right)\right]^{2}}\]

The pressure drop over a length \(L\) then follows from the Darcy–Weisbach equation:

\[\Delta P = f\,\dfrac{L}{D}\,\dfrac{\rho\, v^{2}}{2}\]

For the 1½-inch pipe: \(v = 1.71\) m/s, \(Re \approx 99{,}000\) and \(f = 0.0227\), so over 100 m:

\[\Delta P = 0.0227 \times \dfrac{100}{0.0409} \times \dfrac{850 \times 1.71^{2}}{2} = 69\ \text{kPa}\]

Repeating that for the neighbouring sizes, all in schedule 40:

1 in. (DN 25)26.64.03152,1230.0239619371
1½ in. (DN 40)40.91.7198,9880.022768.641.2

The 1-inch pipe is out at once: 4.03 m/s and 371 kPa over the line, almost as much as the pump's whole differential pressure. The 1½-inch pipe meets both guidelines at 1.71 m/s and 69 kPa per 100 m. The 2-inch pipe would also work, slowly, and the 3-inch pipe is steel for nothing.

Notice how sharply the pressure drop moves. For the same flow in turbulent conditions it varies roughly with the inverse fifth power of the diameter. Going from 1½ to 1 inch cuts the diameter by a third and multiplies the pressure drop by 9. That is why one size too small is a serious mistake and one size too large is only an expensive one.

Step 4: count the fittings

A line is not just straight pipe. Every elbow, tee and valve adds turbulence and loses pressure. The usual shortcut is to convert each fitting to the length of straight pipe that would lose the same pressure, using a length-to-diameter ratio, and add them up. The total is the equivalent length, and it goes into the Darcy–Weisbach equation in place of \(L\).

About a third of this line's resistance is fittings, which is typical of the short, crooked runs inside a plant. Over 60 m the line loses 41 kPa, the figure used in the pump's head build-up in Part 1. The control valve, the heater, the flow meter and the strainer are not fittings in this sense. They have their own pressure drops and were counted separately.

At the layout stage nobody knows the real number of elbows. A common allowance is to add 30% to 100% to the straight length, more for short runs, and to recheck when the piping drawings exist.

Step 5: size the suction line more gently

The pump's suction line is sized against the stricter guideline, because pressure lost there comes straight out of the NPSH margin. The usual result is a suction line one size larger than the discharge. Here that is 2-inch: 1.04 m/s and 20 kPa per 100 m, so the 8 m suction line loses only 1.6 kPa. That is above the 10 kPa per 100 m guideline, but the line is so short and the NPSH margin in Part 1 so large that the next size up would buy nothing. With a hot or volatile liquid the same check would push it to 3-inch.

Step 6: check whether a larger pipe pays

The 2-inch discharge line would save 29 kPa of pump head. At 8.09 m³/h and the pump's 45% efficiency that is about 0.15 kW at the shaft, or roughly 1,200 kWh in a year of continuous running. That is a small saving to set against a larger pipe, larger valves and a larger control valve body. For a line this size the 1½-inch choice stands. The balance tips the other way on big flows and long distances, where the energy runs to megawatt-hours and an economic pipe diameter study is worth doing.

Step 7: choose the schedule

The schedule is the wall thickness. For a given nominal size the outside diameter is fixed, so a heavier schedule means a thicker wall and a smaller bore. The wall has to hold the design pressure. For straight pipe, ASME B31.3 gives the pressure design thickness as:

\[t = \dfrac{P\,D}{2\,(S\,E\,W + P\,Y)}\]

Here \(P\) is the design pressure, \(D\) the outside diameter, \(S\) the allowable stress of the pipe material, \(E\) the weld quality factor, \(W\) the weld strength reduction factor and \(Y\) a coefficient from the code. The line's design pressure is set at 10 barg, comfortably above the 4.9 barg the pump can reach against a closed valve. For seamless carbon steel pipe at ambient temperature, \(S = 138\) MPa, \(E = W = 1\) and \(Y = 0.4\):

\[t = \dfrac{1.0 \times 48.3}{2\,(138 \times 1 \times 1 + 1.0 \times 0.4)} = 0.17\ \text{mm}\]

Pressure alone needs a wall of 0.17 mm. Add a corrosion allowance of 1.5 mm, then allow for the mill tolerance, since pipe may be delivered up to 12.5% thinner than nominal:

\[t_{\min} = \dfrac{0.17 + 1.5}{1 - 0.125} = 1.91\ \text{mm}\]

Schedule 40 has a wall of 3.68 mm, almost twice what is needed. This is the normal result for small pipe at modest pressure: the wall is chosen for mechanical strength, so that the line survives being stepped on, vibrated and threaded, and the pressure check simply confirms it. In practice the schedule comes from the project's piping class, which fixes material, schedule, flange rating and valve types for each service. Many classes call for schedule 80 on small threaded or socket-welded lines. That changes the bore: 1½-inch schedule 80 has an inside diameter of 38.1 mm, which raises the velocity to 1.97 m/s and the pressure drop to 98 kPa per 100 m. The hydraulics have to be done on the schedule that will actually be bought.

Common mistakes

  • Using the nominal size as the diameter. A 1½-inch pipe is not 38 mm inside. Use the inside diameter for the schedule chosen.
  • Forgetting the fittings. In plant piping they are often a third to a half of the resistance.
  • Sizing the suction like the discharge. Suction lines are short, large and gentle for a reason.
  • Sizing at normal flow. The line must pass the rated flow, and pressure drop rises almost with the square of the flow.
  • Applying liquid rules to other flows. Gases, steam, two-phase mixtures and slurries each have their own criteria and methods.

Key points

  • Size liquid lines against a velocity range and a pressure drop per 100 m, at the rated flow.
  • Use the inside diameter. Velocity is flow over bore area, and the Reynolds number says whether the flow is laminar or turbulent.
  • Pressure drop comes from the Darcy–Weisbach equation with the equivalent length, which includes the fittings.
  • In turbulent flow, pressure drop varies roughly with the inverse fifth power of diameter: one size down is a large step.
  • Small-bore wall thickness is usually set by the piping class and mechanical strength. The pressure calculation confirms it.
