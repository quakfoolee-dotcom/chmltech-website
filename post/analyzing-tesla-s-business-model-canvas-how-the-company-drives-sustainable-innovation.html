Tesla's Business Model and Operations: A 2026 Case Study with Lessons for Smaller Firms

Quak Foo LeeMar 18, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Tesla is one of the most discussed companies of the past two decades, both for what it got right and for its recent difficulties. This case study, first written in 2023, has been rewritten to combine the business model canvas with a look at Tesla's operations, replacing a separate 2024 article on its operational efficiency. It uses Tesla's reported 2025 results and ends with lessons a small or mid-sized business can apply. It is an analysis of the business model, not an investment view.

Tesla in 2025, in numbers

Tesla delivered about 1.64 million vehicles in 2025, down roughly 9% from 1.79 million in 2024, its second year of decline after a peak of 1.81 million in 2023. Total revenue was about $94.8 billion, its first annual decline. The energy business went the other way: storage deployments rose about 49% to a record 46.7 GWh, and energy generation and storage revenue grew about 27% to $12.8 billion. At year end Tesla operated 8,182 Supercharger stations. Free cash flow rose to about $6.2 billion.

The business model canvas

Building blockTesla
Customer segmentsBuyers of premium and mid-priced electric vehicles; utilities and businesses buying battery storage; homeowners buying solar and Powerwall
Value propositionsElectric cars with long range and software that improves over time; a dedicated fast-charging network; large-scale battery storage
ChannelsDirect online sales and company-owned stores instead of franchised dealers; over-the-air software updates
Customer relationshipsDirect ownership of the customer; app-based service; a strong brand community
Revenue streamsVehicle sales and leases, energy storage and solar, services, charging, software features, regulatory credits
Key resourcesBattery and powertrain technology, factories, software and vehicle data, the Supercharger network, the brand
Key activitiesVehicle and battery design, high-volume manufacturing, software development, energy project delivery
Key partnersBattery cell and raw material suppliers, governments and utilities, charging site hosts
Cost structureFactories and equipment, materials (especially batteries), research and development, the service and charging network

How the operations support the model

PracticeWhat Tesla doesTrade-off
Vertical integrationDesigns its own software, powertrains and many components, and makes some battery cellsControl of cost and pace of change; more capital and risk
Large castingsFew large cast parts replace many stamped and welded ones in the car bodyFewer parts, steps and robots; less flexibility to change designs
Software-defined productFeatures and fixes delivered over the airImprovements without a dealer visit; a direct relationship with the owner
Direct salesNo independent dealers in most marketsControl of price and experience; owns the cost of stores and service
Fast iterationFrequent, small changes on the lineRapid improvement; quality can vary between builds

What worked

  • Owning the customer relationship. Direct sales, the app and over-the-air updates give Tesla data and a channel no traditional automaker had.
  • Building the infrastructure customers needed. The Supercharger network removed the biggest objection to buying an electric car and became an asset others now connect to.
  • Treating cost as a design problem. Large castings and simplified designs cut parts and steps rather than only negotiating with suppliers.
  • A second business with different demand. Energy storage grew strongly in 2025 while vehicle sales fell, which is why its share of revenue increased.

What the recent decline shows

  • Competition caught up. Many manufacturers, especially in China, now sell capable electric vehicles at lower prices.
  • An ageing line-up. Most sales still come from two models first launched years ago.
  • Brand and policy risk. Sales fell sharply in parts of Europe and in Canada in 2025, and the end of some purchase incentives hurt demand.
  • Bets on the future. The company is spending heavily on autonomy and robots, which may or may not pay off on the timelines it has given.

Lessons for a small or mid-sized business

  1. Remove the customer's biggest obstacle, even if it means doing something outside your core product, as charging was for Tesla.
  2. Design cost out of the product and process instead of only squeezing suppliers. A Kaizen event on one product line is the small-business version.
  3. Keep a direct line to customers. Owning the relationship gives you data and loyalty that resellers keep for themselves.
  4. Do not rely on one product or one market. Tesla's energy business softened a weak year in cars.
  5. Refresh before competitors catch up. A strong position decays faster than it feels from the inside.

Build your own canvas in five steps

StepWhat to do
1. CustomersList your three most profitable customer groups and what each is trying to get done
2. ValueFor each group, write the one thing you do better than the alternatives
3. Channels and relationshipsHow each group finds you, buys from you and stays in touch
4. EngineKey activities, resources and partners that deliver the value; and the costs they drive
5. MoneyRevenue by stream; check which customers and activities actually make the margin

To build your own canvas, draw the nine blocks on one page and fill them in with your team. It takes an hour and often reveals that the most profitable customers and the most expensive activities are not the ones everyone assumed.
