Peak Season Without the Panic: Warehouse Labour Planning

Every year it is the same. Orders start climbing in October, the crew starts staying late, and by the third week of November people are working ten-hour days and Saturdays. Someone calls the temp agency, and the temps arrive in the busiest week, when nobody has time to train them. Errors go up, a good picker quits in December, and in January the warehouse is half idle.

Peak season does not have to go like that. It is the most predictable problem a warehouse has. This part uses the example distributor's year to compare four ways of staffing it, with the work each order brings taken from the earlier parts of the series.

Step 1: turn the forecast into hours

Labour planning starts with two numbers: how many orders are coming, and how many hours each order takes. The first comes from last year's sales by week, adjusted with what the sales team knows about this year. The second is a labour standard for each zone, measured on the floor. For the example, with the fixes from Parts 1 to 5 in place:

\[\text{People needed} = \dfrac{\text{orders a day} \times \text{hours an order}}{\text{productive hours a person a day}} = \dfrac{300 \times 0.153}{7} \approx 6.5\]

A day of 8 paid hours gives about 7 productive hours after breaks, meetings and set-up. So an average day of 300 orders needs about 6.5 people, a crew of 7. At the holiday peak of about 489 orders a day it needs about 10.7, a crew of 11.

Step 2: look inside the day

A daily total hides a second problem. Each zone has its own rhythm: receiving is busiest first thing, when the trucks arrive; packing builds through the late morning; and shipping is concentrated in the last two hours before the carriers come. A crew that is big enough for the day can still be short in shipping at 13:00 while receiving stands idle. This is why cross-training matters as much as headcount, and it is what Figure 2 shows hour by hour.

Four ways to staff the year

Plan 1: lean crew plus overtime. Keep a crew of 7, sized for the average day, and cover peaks with overtime. In BC, most employees earn time and a half after 8 hours in a day or 40 in a week, and double time after 12 hours in a day. The example allows up to two hours of overtime a person a day. When even that is not enough, orders ship a day late.

Plan 2: crew sized for the peak. Hire 11 people and keep them all year. Nothing ships late, but for most of the year a large share of paid time is idle.

Plan 3: core crew plus planned temps. Keep the crew of 7, and book agency temps from the forecast, starting each one 2 weeks before the week they are needed so they are trained before the rush. Temps are assumed to work at 60% of full pace in their first week and 85% in their second, and each one costs 8 paid hours of training and 2 hours of a supervisor's time.

Plan 4: temps plus a cross-trained crew. As Plan 3, but 60% of the core crew is trained in more than one zone and moves each hour to where the work is. The crew covers the zones' different rhythms itself, so fewer temps are needed and fewer hours go to overtime.

Overtime alone looks cheaper than it is. At $751,000 a year, Plan 1 costs less on paper than planned temps. But 2,836 orders ship late, and for 17 weeks of the year the crew averages more than eight hours of overtime a person a week, up to 10.0 hours. Research on long hours, best known from Pencavel's study of munitions workers, found output per hour falls once people work beyond about 50 hours a week, and that very long weeks add little. Long peaks also bring errors (Part 5) and resignations, which this table does not count.

Staffing for the peak is safe and expensive. Plan 2 never ships late, but it costs $192,000 a year more than Plan 1, and 42% of paid productive time has no work.

Planned temps buy reliability. Plan 3 ships everything on time with no heavy overtime weeks, for $772,000 a year. Without cross-training, though, temps are booked zone by zone and still leave gaps at each zone's busiest hour, which overtime fills.

Cross-training is the cheapest capacity there is. Plan 4 needs fewer temp weeks and less overtime, and its idle time falls to 15%. At $652,000 a year, or $8.36 an order, it is the lowest-cost plan and the most reliable.

Common mistakes

  • Booking temps in the peak week. They arrive when nobody can train them, and work at half pace when you need them most.
  • Planning by headcount, not hours. Ten people at seven productive hours is not the same as ten people at eight.
  • Ignoring the hours inside the day. The shortage is usually in one zone at one time, not in the day's total.
  • Running the crew on overtime for weeks. Output per hour falls, errors rise and people leave.
  • Skipping the post-peak review. Next year's plan starts with what this year's forecast missed.

Key points

  • Peak season is predictable. Turn the weekly forecast into hours with a labour standard for each zone.
  • Look inside the day. Each zone peaks at a different hour, so a crew that is big enough can still be short in one place.
  • Overtime alone is cheap on paper and expensive in late orders, errors and burnout. In the example, 2,836 orders ship late.
  • Book temps from the forecast and start them two weeks early, so they are trained before the rush.
  • Cross-train the core crew. In the example it is the lowest-cost and most reliable plan, at $652,000 a year.
