Process Documentation That People Use: Levels, Swim Lane Maps and Six Steps

Quak Foo LeeAug 26, 2021Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Process documentation is the written record of how work gets done: who does each step, in what order, with what inputs, and what result is expected. Good documentation shortens training, keeps results consistent when people change, and gives you a baseline to improve from. Poor documentation is written once for an audit and never read again. This article, first published in 2021, has been rewritten as a practical guide with a worked example.

Four levels, for four readers

The most common mistake is putting everything in one long document. Split it by reader.

LevelWhat it coversWho reads itTypical format
Level 1: process mapThe main steps and hand-offs, on one pageManagers, new staff, auditorsSwim lane map, SIPOC
Level 2: procedure (SOP)Who does what, when, with which records, and the rules for exceptionsThe people who run the processWritten procedure with a RACI
Level 3: work instructionHow to do one task step by step, with photos or screenshotsThe person doing the taskChecklist, photo guide, short video
Level 4: form or recordThe evidence that the task was doneSupervisors, auditors, customersForm, ticket, system screen

Start with a swim lane map

A swim lane map shows each role in its own horizontal lane, so the hand-offs, where most delays and errors happen, are easy to see. The example below is a quote-to-order process in a small engineering or fabrication business.

Three things stand out in the map. The decision at step 2 stops incomplete requests before engineering spends time on them. Steps 3 and 4 run in sequence; if they could run at the same time, the quote would go out faster. And the target at the bottom turns the map into something you can measure.

How to document a process in six steps

  1. Pick one process and set its boundaries. Name the trigger (a request arrives) and the end (production is scheduled). A SIPOC table, listing suppliers, inputs, process, outputs and customers, helps agree the scope.
  2. Walk it with the people who do it. Document how the work is actually done, not how the manager thinks it is done. Watch it, or record a walk-through and transcribe it.
  3. Draw the map first, then write. Agree the map in one short workshop before anyone writes a procedure.
  4. Write the procedure in plain words. One action per step, starting with a verb. Name roles, not people. Put the rules for exceptions where they apply.
  5. Add a measure and an owner. Each process needs one owner who keeps it current and one or two measures, such as quote turnaround time.
  6. Control and review it. Give it a version number and a review date, keep it where people work, and retire old copies. Document and revision control explains how.

Using AI to draft, with care

AI tools can turn a recorded walk-through or a screen recording into a first draft of a procedure or work instruction in minutes. They are good at structure and plain language. They also fill gaps with plausible steps that nobody actually does. Have the people who run the process check every step, and do not put customer or confidential data into a tool your business has not approved. Using AI tools inside a quality system covers the controls.

Signs your documentation is working

  • New staff use it in their first week without being told to.
  • People suggest changes to it, because it matches what they do.
  • When something goes wrong, the procedure is the first thing you check and update.
