How to Implement Process Improvements: Methods, an Eight-Step Project and a Worked Example

Process improvement means changing how work is done so it is faster, cheaper, more reliable or easier for customers. This guide brings together a two-part series first published on this site in 2021, rewritten as one article: which method to use, an eight-step project you can run without consultants, and the habits that make results last.

Signs a process needs improving

Most businesses do not lack improvement ideas; they lack a clear view of which process to fix first. These symptoms usually point to a process problem rather than a people problem:

  • The same mistake keeps happening, even with different people doing the work.
  • Work waits more than it moves. Orders, quotes or approvals sit in someone's inbox for days and take minutes to do.
  • Customers chase you for updates, dates or corrections.
  • Information is typed twice into different systems or spreadsheets.
  • Only one person knows how it is done, and things stall when they are away.
  • Overtime and expediting are normal even though demand has not grown.

Which method fits your problem

The methods overlap more than their names suggest. Most small businesses do best by starting with PDCA and a process map, and borrowing tools from the others as needed.

MethodWhat it doesUse it when
PDCA (plan, do, check, act)A simple loop for any changeSmall, frequent improvements by the team that does the work
LeanRemoves waiting, re-work and needless steps so work flowsLong lead times, many hand-offs, piles of work in progress
Six Sigma (DMAIC)Uses data to find and remove causes of variation and defectsA recurring defect whose cause is not obvious
Theory of ConstraintsFinds the one step that limits output and focuses on itOutput is capped and adding effort everywhere has not helped
Business process managementDocuments, owns and measures processes across the companyProcesses that cross several departments and drift over time
Kaizen eventA focused 3 to 5 day workshop on one processA clear, contained problem that a small team can fix quickly

Choosing the first project

List the candidate processes and score each one on two questions: how much would fixing it be worth (to customers, cash or capacity), and how hard is it to change? Start with a process that is high on value and low or medium on effort. A visible early win builds the support you need for the harder projects.

Low effortHigh effort
High valueDo first: quote turnaround, order entry, month-end closePlan carefully: ERP replacement, new plant layout
Low valueQuick fixes during normal workAvoid for now

Roles in an improvement project

RoleWhoWhat they do
SponsorA manager or the ownerSets the goal, removes obstacles, approves changes and spending, attends reviews
Process ownerThe person accountable for the process day to dayOwns the result after the project ends and keeps the standard current
TeamThree to six people who do the work, upstream and downstreamMap, measure, find causes, design and trial changes
FacilitatorAn internal improvement lead or an outside coachKeeps the method on track, runs workshops, coaches the team

An eight-step improvement project

StepWhat to do
1. Choose the processPick one that customers notice or that costs real time or money. Write the problem as a fact with a number.
2. Set a goal and a sponsorA measurable target, a date, and a manager who can remove obstacles.
3. Form a small teamThe people who do the work, plus someone from each step upstream and downstream.
4. Map the current processWalk it, time it, and note waits, re-work and hand-offs.
5. Find the causesAsk why the problem happens until you reach something you can change; check with data.
6. Design and trial the changeStart small: one shift, one product line, one customer. Measure the result.
7. Make it the standardWrite the new way down, train everyone, and update forms and systems.
8. Check and sustainReview the measure monthly for six months; it is easy to slide back.

Tools at each step

StepUseful tools
Choose and scopeProblem statement, SIPOC (suppliers, inputs, process, outputs, customers), project charter
Map and measureSwim lane map, value stream map, time study, check sheet, simple run chart
Find causes5 whys, fishbone diagram, Pareto chart, data by shift, product or customer
Design the changeBrainstorming, impact-effort matrix, mistake-proofing, standard work
Trial and standardizePilot plan, before-and-after comparison, written procedure, training record
SustainScorecard measure, monthly review, audit at 30 and 90 days

Worked example: quote turnaround

A machine shop takes an average of 6 working days to send quotes, and loses work to faster competitors. The team maps the process and finds that a quote waits in three queues: for the estimator, for a material price from the supplier, and for the owner's approval. The actual work is about 3 hours. Three changes follow: a price list for the ten most-used materials, refreshed monthly, so most quotes no longer wait for the supplier; approval only for quotes over $25,000; and a shared queue board so the estimator works oldest first. After a six-week trial, average turnaround is 2 days. The numbers are illustrative, but the pattern is typical: the work is short and the waits are long.

The before-and-after picture for the machine shop:

MeasureBeforeAfter six weeks
Average quote turnaround6 working days2 working days
Quotes waiting for a material priceAbout 60%About 15%
Quotes needing owner approvalAllOnly those over $25,000
Quote win rateTracked from now onReviewed monthly

Common pitfalls

  • Jumping to a solution, often new software, before the current process is mapped and measured.
  • A problem statement that blames a person instead of describing what happens and how often.
  • Too big a scope. "Fix operations" is not a project; "cut quote turnaround to two days" is.
  • No baseline, so nobody can show the change worked.
  • Running several projects at once with the same few people, so none finishes.

What makes improvements last

  • Leaders show up. A manager who attends the review and removes obstacles matters more than any method.
  • The new way is written down and replaces the old instructions everywhere. See process documentation that people use.
  • One measure is tracked on the team's scorecard after the project ends.
  • People are not blamed for problems the process caused, so they keep raising them. The core values of process improvement covers the culture side.
