Starbucks Case Study: Closing Every Store for an Afternoon

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Howard Schultz, who returned as Starbucks' chief executive in January 2008. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

In January 2008 the Starbucks board asked Howard Schultz, the man who had built the company, to return as chief executive. The share price had fallen about 42% in 2007. For the first time, the average number of transactions in U.S. stores was falling. Competitors from McDonald's to independent cafés were selling espresso drinks, and customers were telling Starbucks that the experience no longer felt special.

Schultz believed the problem started behind the counter. In the rush to open stores, Starbucks had automated its espresso machines, sped up its service and added products, and in his view baristas no longer knew how to make a great drink. He wanted to retrain all of them, at once, in a way the whole company and its customers would notice. The most direct way to do that was to close every company-owned store in the United States for an afternoon. It would cost millions of dollars in lost sales, hand customers to competitors for a day and invite ridicule.

Company background

Starbucks began in Seattle in 1971 as a seller of coffee beans. Schultz joined in 1982, was inspired by the espresso bars of Milan, and bought the company in 1987. His idea was a "third place" between home and work, built on high-quality coffee, well-trained staff (called partners) and a consistent experience. Starbucks offered health insurance and stock options to part-time staff, unusual in retail.

The company grew at a pace few retailers have matched. By 2007 it had more than 15,000 stores worldwide and was opening several new stores a day. Schultz stepped down as chief executive in 2000 and stayed on as chairman.

The situation in early 2008

In February 2007 Schultz had sent an internal memo to senior leaders, later leaked, warning that a series of decisions, each sensible on its own, had led to the "commoditization" of the Starbucks experience. Automatic espresso machines blocked the customer's view of the barista. Pre-ground, flavour-locked packaging had removed the aroma of freshly ground coffee. Stores were being designed for efficiency rather than warmth. Hot breakfast sandwiches, which sold well, filled the stores with the smell of burnt cheese instead of coffee.

Schultz had already moved quickly after his return: he replaced senior managers, announced job cuts at headquarters, introduced free wireless internet and said hot breakfast sandwiches would go. The question was whether the training needed a gesture this large. The alternatives were cheaper and less visible.

The options

Option A: Retrain quietly, store by store

Use slow hours and store managers to retrain staff over several months. No lost sales and no headlines, but no clear moment of change either.

Option B: Close every company-owned U.S. store for one evening

Retrain all baristas on the same evening, with a video message from Schultz, and tell the public why. Expensive, risky and impossible to ignore.

Option C: Focus on products and marketing instead

Leave training to the normal programs and put the effort into new drinks, prices and advertising to bring traffic back. Faster to show results, but it does not address what Schultz saw as the cause.

Discussion questions

  1. Schultz saw the decline as a quality problem behind the counter; others saw it as a recession and competition problem. How would you test which was right?
  2. Use Exhibit 2. What is the value of the signal sent by closing every store? How would you put a number on it?
  3. What could go wrong with a nationwide closure? How would you plan for it?
  4. Each of the changes in the 2007 memo made sense on its own. How does a growing company stop a series of sensible decisions from eroding what made it good?
  5. What would you do after the training evening to make sure the improvement lasted?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Schultz closed the stores. On Tuesday, February 26, 2008, Starbucks shut about 7,100 company-owned U.S. stores from 5:30 to 9:00 p.m. local time for what it called Espresso Excellence Training, retraining about 135,000 baristas in pulling espresso shots, steaming milk and serving customers. The 4,000 licensed stores stayed open. Dunkin' Donuts offered 99-cent espresso drinks that evening, and independent cafés gave away coffee. In a video that opened the session, Schultz told staff that the company had lost its way over the years.

Schultz later said the closure cost millions of dollars and was meant to show how serious he was about returning to the core. It was the first of many steps. In July 2008 Starbucks announced it would close about 600 underperforming U.S. stores. In October 2008, as the financial crisis deepened, he took about 10,000 store managers to a leadership conference in New Orleans costing about $30 million, over the board's reluctance. The company restructured its supply chain, brought back the smell of fresh coffee and later returned breakfast sandwiches with a new recipe.

The recovery took two years. By fiscal 2010 Starbucks was reporting record profits, and the turnaround, told in Schultz's book Onward, is taught at business schools, including in a Harvard Business School course on leadership.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

Growth can erode the process that made you good. Each shortcut can be justified on its own. A regular check of your core process against your own standard, through documented procedures and audits, shows the drift before customers do.

Training is an operational investment, not an overhead. Starbucks treated an evening of lost sales as the price of consistent quality. Measure what poor quality costs you in rework, complaints and lost customers before you decide training is too expensive.

Visible actions change behaviour faster than memos. People believe what leaders do. A change management model helps plan the follow-through, but one clear act shows what matters.

A single gesture is not a turnaround. The closure worked because it was followed by store closures, supply chain changes and manager training. Plan the next steps before the first one.
