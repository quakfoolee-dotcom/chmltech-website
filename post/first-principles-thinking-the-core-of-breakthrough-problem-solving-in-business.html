Most business decisions are made by analogy. We look at what competitors do, what we did last year or what the industry calls normal, and adjust it a little. That is fast and usually good enough. It also means every inherited assumption comes along for free.

First principles thinking does the opposite. It breaks a problem down to what must be true, sets aside what is only habit, and builds the answer up again from there. This article explains where the idea comes from, gives a five-step method, and works through two examples with numbers: a food plant that blamed ingredient prices for its costs, and a purchased part whose price nobody had questioned. A calculator lets you test the first example with your own figures. The article was first published in October 2025 and has been rewritten and extended.

What first principles thinking means

Aristotle described a first principle as the first basis from which a thing is known: a starting point that does not depend on anything more basic. Physics and engineering work this way. A chemical engineer sizing a plant does not start from what a similar plant used. The engineer starts from conservation of mass and energy, because every kilogram that goes in must come out somewhere, and builds the design up from there.

In business the idea is the same, applied to costs, constraints and customer needs. You ask what is actually required, what it would cost at its physical or contractual minimum, and why the current answer is so far from that minimum.

Table 1. Reasoning by analogy and from first principles

By analogyFrom first principles
Starting questionHow do others do it?What has to be true?
Starting dataBenchmarks, past practice, industry normsPhysical limits, laws, contracts, measured facts

Main riskCopies other people's mistakesReinvents things that already work

The best-known example

Elon Musk made the term popular in business. In a 2012 video interview with Kevin Rose he described how battery packs were then said to cost about $600 per kilowatt-hour, and the common view was that they would stay expensive. His team instead listed what a battery is made of (carbon, nickel, aluminium, polymers and a steel can) and priced those materials at commodity-exchange rates. The answer was about $80 per kilowatt-hour. The gap between $80 and $600 was not set by nature. It came from how cells were designed and made, which meant it could be attacked.

The lesson for a small business is not about batteries. It is the method: work out the floor set by materials, physics or contracts, compare it with what you pay or accept today, and ask what fills the gap.

Facts, constraints and assumptions

The core skill is sorting statements into three kinds. Teams usually treat all three as fixed. Only the first two are.

Table 2. Three kinds of statement and how to test each

KindWhat it isExampleHow to test it
Hard constraintPhysics, law, safety code or a signed contractPackaged food must contain its declared net quantity, within the tolerance set in the regulationsRead the rule or contract itself, not someone's summary of it
Measured factSomething observed and recorded9% of ingredients bought never reach a sold barCheck how it was measured, by whom and over what period
AssumptionA belief, habit or rule of thumb"Ingredient prices are the problem and we can't control them"Ask what evidence supports it and what would change if it were false

A useful sign of an assumption is a sentence that starts with "we always", "customers expect", "the industry standard is" or "we can't". Each may be true. None is a first principle until it has been checked.

The five steps

1. State the problem in numbers

"Margins are falling" is a complaint. "Ingredient cost rose from 38% to 43% of sales in two years" is a problem you can break down. Put a number, a unit and a time period on it.

2. Break it into its parts

Draw the problem as a tree. Cost splits into price times quantity; quantity splits into what the product needs and what is lost. Revenue splits into customers, orders per customer and value per order. Keep splitting until each branch is something you can measure.

3. Sort facts from assumptions

Go through each branch with Table 2. Mark what is a hard constraint, what has been measured, and what is believed. The believed items are where the opportunity usually is.

4. Test the assumptions

Find the cheapest way to check each important assumption: a week of measurement, a call to a supplier, a reading of the actual regulation, a small trial. Work out the theoretical minimum for the branch (the cost if nothing were lost, the time if nothing waited) so you know how big the gap is.

5. Rebuild and try it small

Put the parts back together using only what survived the test. Then try the new design on one product, one line or one customer before rolling it out. A first-principles answer is still a hypothesis until it works in practice.

Questions that expose assumptions

Table 3. Questions to ask at each branch of the tree

PurposeQuestion
ClarifyWhat exactly do we mean by this? How is it measured?
Find the sourceWhere did this rule come from? Who decided it, and when?
Check the evidenceWhat data shows this is true today, here, for us?
Find the floorWhat would this cost, or how long would it take, if nothing were wasted?
Test the oppositeWhat would have to be true for the opposite to work?
Check the consequenceIf this assumption is wrong, what changes, and how much is it worth?

Worked example 1: "ingredient prices are killing our margin"

A snack bar maker buys $2,400,000 of ingredients a year. Ingredient cost has risen as a share of sales, and the management team's view is that world commodity prices are to blame and nothing can be done internally.

Step 1, the problem in numbers. Ingredient cost is 43% of sales, up from 38%.

Step 2, the parts. Ingredient cost = price per kilogram × kilograms bought. Kilograms bought = kilograms the recipe needs + kilograms lost.

Step 3, facts and assumptions. Prices rising is a fact. "Nothing can be done" is an assumption. Nobody had measured the second branch, the kilograms lost.

Step 4, the test. A mass balance does the work. The recipes say how much of each ingredient goes into a bar, and the sales records say how many bars were sold. Multiplied together, they give what the plant should have bought if nothing were lost. It bought 9% more. That 9% is worth $216,000 a year, and a week of weighing at each step showed where it goes (Figure 2 and Table 4).

Table 4. Ingredient losses and the first round of fixes

LossNowA yearAfterFix
Changeover purges and start-up3.5%$84,0002.1%Schedule by recipe family: 6 changeovers a week instead of 10
Overfill (giveaway)2.5%$60,0001.0%Checkweigher feeds back to the depositor; fill target set from the measured weight variation and the net quantity rules, not a guess
Rejected bars2.0%$48,0001.5%Wrapper settings recorded per product; first-off check after every changeover
Spills, expiry and other1.0%$24,0001.0%No change in the first round
Total9.0%$216,0005.6%

Step 5, rebuild. None of the fixes touches the supplier or the price list. Yield, the share of ingredients that ends up in a sold bar, rises from 91% to 94.4%. The plant can make the same output while buying less:

\[\text{Saving a year} = M\left(1-\frac{Y}{Y^{*}}\right)\]

where \(M\) is the ingredients bought a year, \(Y\) the yield now and \(Y^{*}\) the yield after the fixes. Here \(2{,}400{,}000 \times (1 - 0.910/0.944)\) ≈ $86,441 a year. With an assumed $60,000 for a checkweigher, scheduling work and training, payback is about 8 months. The first round closes 40% of the gap to the theoretical minimum, and the rest is a target for the next round.

Two points in this example are worth noting. The overfill fix needed the actual rule, not the rule of thumb: the line had been set well above the label weight "to be safe", and nobody had read what the net quantity rules in the Safe Food for Canadians Regulations actually require. And the changeover fix is a scheduling decision, which is why the team that owned the schedule had never seen it as an ingredient cost. The same losses show up in OEE as quality and changeover losses; What OEE Losses Cost puts dollars on them.

Worked example 2: a price nobody had questioned

A machinery builder buys 2,000 aluminium brackets a year from one machine shop at $38.00 each, $76,000 a year. The price has risen with every renewal, and the buyer's view is that "that's what machining costs". The first-principles version is a should-cost: build the price up from what the part physically needs.

Table 5. Should-cost of the bracket, as drawn and after two changes

ElementAs drawn, 100 a monthPer partAfter changes, 500 a quarterPer part
Material0.95 kg of 6061 aluminium bar at $9.50/kg$9.030.95 kg of 6061 aluminium bar at $9.50/kg$9.03
Machining7 min on a CNC mill at $110/h$12.835 min on a CNC mill at $110/h$9.17
Set-up2 h at $110/h, spread over a batch of 100$2.202 h at $110/h, spread over a batch of 500$0.44
Deburr and inspect3 min at $60/h$3.003 min at $60/h$3.00
AnodizingOutside service, per part$2.20Outside service, per part$2.20
Overhead and profit15% of the above$4.3915% of the above$3.57
Should-cost$33.65$27.41

Three things come out of the build-up:

  • Material is under a third of the cost. Arguing about aluminium prices would have missed the point.
  • Machining time is the biggest element, and the drawing drives it. Every face carried a tight tolerance copied from an older part. Only two faces locate anything. Opening the others lets the shop cut faster and skip a finishing pass: 7 minutes becomes 5 in the example.
  • Ordering 100 a month repeats the set-up twelve times a year. A quarterly order of 500, with the shop holding a small stock, spreads the set-up across five times as many parts.

The should-cost does not tell the supplier what to charge. It gives both sides a shared picture of where the cost is. In the example, the buyer takes the revised drawing and the new order pattern to the supplier and agrees $30.00, which still leaves the shop a better margin than the should-cost assumes. That saves $16,000 a year on one part number, and the same review can be repeated on the next ten.

Make sure engineering approves any change to a drawing. A tolerance that looks arbitrary may protect a fit or a safety function. Checking that is part of step 4.

Common business assumptions worth testing

Table 6. Assumptions that often fail a first-principles check

AssumptionFirst-principles questionWhat is often found
"We need more capacity"How many hours does the bottleneck actually make good product?Lost hours to stoppages, slow running and changeovers, often 30 to 40% of planned time. See What OEE Really Measures.
"We need more customers"Revenue = customers × orders each × value per order. Which branch is weak?Customers leave or order less often; retention is cheaper to fix than acquisition
"Cash is tight because sales are down"How many days pass between paying for materials and being paid?Weeks lost to slow quotes, late invoices and unchased debts. See the quote-to-cash process.
"We need more stock to avoid running out"What does demand variation and supplier lead time actually require?Too much stock of slow items and too little of the critical ones. See setting safety stock.
"We need a new system"Which steps of the process fail, and why?A broken process that a new system would only speed up
"Our price must match the competitor's"What does the customer value, and what does it cost us to provide?Customers paying for speed, reliability or service the price list ignores

When not to use it

  • Routine, reversible decisions. Choosing a printer or a meeting time by analogy is fine. Save the effort for large costs and stubborn problems.
  • Rules that exist for a reason you have not found yet. Before removing a step, find out why it was put there. A check that looks pointless may be the one that stopped a recall years ago.
  • Proven methods. Accounting standards, safety codes and established engineering practice already encode first principles. Rebuilding them from scratch wastes time and adds risk.
  • Problems without data. If nothing can be measured, the method turns into opinion. Collect a week of data first.

The common failure is calling an opinion a first principle. "Customers only care about price" is not a fundamental truth because someone senior says it firmly. Test it like any other assumption.

Making it a team habit

Pick one recurring problem, such as a cost that keeps rising or a delay nobody can explain, and keep an assumption log while you work on it. One line per belief is enough.

Table 8. An assumption log

AssumptionKindEvidence todayHow we will test itOwner and dateResult
Nothing can be done about ingredient costAssumptionNone; losses never measuredMass balance from recipes and sales; one week of weighingPlant manager, 2 weeksFalse: 9% lost in the plant
Label weight needs a large safety marginAssumption"We've always done it"Read the net quantity rules; check the checkweigher dataQuality lead, 1 weekMargin can be cut by more than half
Bracket tolerances are needed on every faceAssumptionCopied from an older drawingEngineering review of which faces locateDesign engineer, 1 weekTwo of eight faces are critical

Three questions help in any meeting: "Is that a fact, a constraint or an assumption?", "How do we know?" and "What would it be if nothing were wasted?" Ask them about the problem, never about the person, so challenge feels safe. Leaders go first by putting their own assumptions in the log.

To practise the reasoning, try the critical thinking for business practice exam. For related reading, the review of Clear Thinking by Shane Parrish covers how to stop reacting and start reasoning, and OODA vs PDCA covers how to turn a decision into a test.

Takeaway. Put the problem in numbers, break it into parts, and find the floor set by physics, law or contract. Then ask what fills the gap between that floor and today. In the first example the gap was $216,000 of lost ingredients that everyone had blamed on prices; in the second it was a drawing and an order pattern that nobody had questioned.

The snack bar plant, the machined bracket and their figures are illustrative examples with round-number inputs. They do not describe any real company or supplier. Use your own data before acting on a result.
