The Quote-to-Cash Process in Manufacturing: Where the Days and the Cash Go

Quak Foo LeeNov 14, 20265 min read

The order was quoted, won, built and delivered. The money arrives two months later. Quote to cash is the name for that whole path, and most of it runs through the office, not the factory.

This series follows one example: Example Windows Co., a made-up 40-person window maker with sales of about $12 million a year. This post maps the process and shows where the days and the cash sit. Part 2 lets you run the factory in a live 3D simulation. Part 3 covers what to fix first and the payback.

What quote to cash means

Quote to cash (Q2C) covers every step from a customer's request for a price to the payment reaching the bank. Order to cash (O2C) is the same path without the quoting step. A made-to-order manufacturer should include the quote, because every job starts with one and nothing else can start until the customer accepts it.

Two numbers describe the process:

  • Quote-to-cash days. The time from the customer's request to the payment.
  • Cash tied up. The money already paid out for materials and labour on orders that customers have not yet paid for.

The eight stages

Six groups handle an order: the customer, sales, planning and purchasing, production, shipping and finance. Figure 1 shows who does each step and when it is finished in the example.

Every stage hands something to the next one. Days are lost at the handovers, where work waits in a queue or goes back for missing information.

Table 1. The eight stages, who owns them and where days are lost

StageOwnerWhat it hands overWhere the days are lost
1. QuoteSalesA price, a specification and a delivery dateRequests queue behind one estimator. Prices are worked out by hand.
2. OrderSalesA confirmed order that matches the quoteDetails are typed again from the quote. Sizes or colours are missing.
3. SchedulePlanningA production slot and a promised dateThe date is promised before capacity is checked.
4. ProcurePurchasingGlass, profiles and hardware for the jobMaterials are ordered late. Supplier lead times vary.
5. ProduceProductionFinished windowsOrders wait for a full batch. Changeovers and machine stoppages.
6. InspectProductionPacked windows that meet the specificationDefects are found at the end of the line and reworked.
7. ShipShippingDelivery to the site, with proof of deliveryPart loads wait for a full truck. The signed slip goes missing.
8. Invoice and collectFinanceAn invoice, then the paymentInvoices are raised in a weekly batch. Nobody follows up overdue accounts.

Where the days go

In the example, an average order takes 66 days from request to payment. The figures come from the simulation model in Part 2, run for one year at its starting settings.

Writing the quote5.0SalesNothing spent yet
Customer decides2.9CustomerNothing spent yet
Order entry and materials7.1Sales, purchasing, suppliersCash spent, not billed

Total66.3

Three points stand out.

  • Production is about 7% of the time. The windows spend under 5 days in the factory, and that includes waiting for a batch. A faster line would barely change the total. For what happens inside those days, see What OEE Really Measures.
  • Office waits take four times as long as production. Writing the quote takes 5 days, sending the invoice takes another 5, and customers pay about 10 days late. Those 20 days need no new equipment to fix.
  • Waiting to be paid is 60% of the total. The terms are 30 days, and the average customer takes about 40. The clock starts when the invoice goes out, so every day of invoicing delay is added on top.

Where the cash sits

An average order is 20 windows for $20,000. The company pays $8,000 for materials when the order is confirmed and adds $4,000 of labour on the line. The remaining $8,000 covers overhead and profit. Figure 3 follows the cash for one order.

The company is out of pocket on each order for 58 days. It takes about 1.6 orders a day, so about 96 orders are open with money spent on them at any time. Together they tie up about $1.06 million. The owner funds that amount from a credit line or from retained profit.

The same arithmetic gives a useful rule for this example. After an order is built it carries $12,000 of cost, and 1.6 orders a day reach that point. Each day removed between delivery and payment therefore frees about $19,000 of cash. Invoicing on the day of delivery, 5 days sooner, frees close to $100,000.

Five measures to track

Most accounting and ERP systems already hold the dates needed for these measures. If yours does not, a spreadsheet with one row per order and six dates is enough to start.

Table 3. Quote-to-cash measures and their values in the example

MeasureHow to calculate itExample
Quote turnaroundDays from the request to the quote being sent5 days
Order lead timeDays from the confirmed order to delivery14 days
On-time deliveryOrders delivered by the promised date ÷ orders delivered80%
Invoice lagDays from delivery to the invoice being sent5 days
Days sales outstanding (DSO)Unpaid invoices ÷ sales per day. Here $1.28 million ÷ $32,000.40 days

The promised date in the example is 15 days after the order. One order in five misses it, mostly because materials arrive late or the order waits for a batch. Production Planning and Scheduling for Manufacturing covers how to set dates the plant can keep.

Three common mistakes

  • Measuring only the factory. Output and scrap are tracked daily, while nobody knows how long a quote or an invoice takes.
  • Treating the invoice as paperwork. The invoice starts the customer's payment clock. A weekly invoicing run adds several days to every order.
  • Looking at each department alone. Each group meets its own target, and the order still waits at every handover. One person should own the total number of days.

Takeaway. Map the eight stages, put a date on each handover and add up the days. In the example an order takes 66 days to become cash, the factory accounts for about 5 of them, and about $1.06 million is tied up in open orders.
