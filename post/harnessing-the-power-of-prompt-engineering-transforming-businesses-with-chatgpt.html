Prompting AI Models for Business Work: What Still Works in 2026

Quak Foo LeeJun 11, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

A prompt is the instruction you give an AI model. This guide replaces three earlier articles on this site about prompt engineering, chain-of-thought prompting and prompting ChatGPT for business. Much of the 2023 advice has aged: today's models follow plain instructions far better, several now reason through a problem before answering, and some old tricks now make results worse. What still matters is being clear about the task, giving the model the right material, and checking the output.

The five parts of a good business prompt

Before and after

VersionPrompt
BeforeWrite a summary of this customer complaint.
AfterYou are helping our customer service lead. Summarize the complaint below for the weekly quality meeting.
Context: we make stainless steel tanks; the reader knows our products but not this customer.
Constraints: under 120 words; facts only, no apology wording; if the order number or date is missing, say so.
Output: three headings: What happened, What the customer wants, Open questions.
<complaint> …pasted email… </complaint>

The second prompt takes a minute longer to write and saves several rounds of editing. Putting the pasted material between simple tags, like the <complaint> markers above, helps the model separate your instructions from the text it is working on. Both OpenAI and Anthropic recommend this kind of structure for longer prompts.

What changed since 2023

  • "Think step by step" is often unnecessary. Chain-of-thought prompting, asking a model to reason in steps before answering, was a major finding in 2022 and helped older models with arithmetic and logic. Reasoning models now do this on their own before they answer. OpenAI's guidance for its reasoning models is to keep prompts simple and direct and to avoid telling them to think step by step, which can hurt rather than help. With a standard chat model, asking for the reasoning can still help on multi-step problems.
  • Role-play matters less than context. "You are a world-class marketer" adds little. Saying who the reader is and what they already know adds a lot.
  • Settings like temperature are rarely needed in chat. They matter when you build prompts into software; in a chat window, wording does the work.
  • Long documents are fine. Current models can read long reports and contracts in one go. Put the document first and the question last.

Habits that make prompts reliable

  1. Write the prompt once, then save it. A team library of tested prompts for recurring jobs (quote summaries, meeting notes, supplier emails) is worth more than clever one-off wording.
  2. Tell the model what to do when it is unsure. "If the information is not in the document, say so" reduces made-up answers.
  3. Ask for a format you can check. Tables, fixed headings and quoted sources are easier to verify than flowing prose.
  4. Test on real cases. Try a new prompt on five real examples, including a difficult one, before the team relies on it.
  5. Keep sensitive data in approved tools. Customer and employee information should only go into tools your business has approved for it.

When a better prompt is not the answer

If a task needs your company's own data, a sequence of steps across systems, or the same output hundreds of times, the answer is usually a workflow or an agent connected to your systems rather than a longer prompt. AI agents for small businesses explains where that line sits.
