The Theory of Constraints: Five Focusing Steps and What an Hour at the Bottleneck Is Worth

The theory of constraints (TOC), set out by Eliyahu Goldratt in his 1984 novel The Goal, starts from a simple observation: in any process, one step limits how much the whole process can deliver. Improving anything else does not raise output. This article, first published in 2021, has been rewritten with a worked example that puts a dollar value on the idea.

The idea in one picture

Speeding up cutting or packing in this line achieves nothing except more parts waiting in front of welding. An hour lost at welding is an hour lost for the whole business. An hour lost at packing costs nothing, because packing has spare capacity.

How to find the constraint

  • Look for the pile. Work waits in front of the constraint. The step with the largest and most persistent queue is usually it.
  • Compare load with capacity. For each step, divide the hours of work needed by the hours available. The highest ratio is the constraint.
  • Watch where expediting happens. If supervisors spend their days pushing jobs through one area, that is a strong signal.
  • Check what idles when one step stops. When the constraint stops, the whole output stops. When a non-constraint stops briefly, nothing downstream notices.
  • Be suspicious of averages. A step that is busy only 70% of the time on average can still be the constraint for a particular product mix or during peaks.

The five focusing steps

StepWhat it means
1. Identify the constraintFind the step with the least capacity for the work you have. Look for the biggest pile of work waiting in front of it.
2. Exploit itGet the most out of it with what you already have: no idle time at breaks, no defective parts reaching it, the right job next.
3. Subordinate everything elsePace the rest of the process to the constraint. Releasing more work into the line only builds inventory.
4. Elevate itIf more output is still needed, add capacity at the constraint: overtime, another machine, outsourcing part of its work.
5. RepeatOnce it is no longer the constraint, another step will be. Go back to step 1 and avoid letting old rules become the new limit.

Exploit, subordinate and elevate in practice

StepTypical actions
ExploitStagger breaks so the constraint keeps running; put the best operator there; inspect parts before the constraint so it never works on scrap; cut its set-up time; run the most profitable jobs per constraint hour first
SubordinateRelease work into the line only at the constraint's pace; let non-constraints have idle time; schedule maintenance on non-constraints during peaks, never on the constraint
ElevateOvertime or an extra shift at the constraint only; move some of its work to another machine or a subcontractor; buy capacity only after exploiting and subordinating

Worked example: what is an hour at the constraint worth?

Each unit earns $40 of contribution margin (price minus materials and other truly variable costs). Welding runs 15 hours a day over two shifts and stops for breaks and shift handovers. If the team staggers breaks so welding keeps running and adds 1 productive hour a day, the line makes 25 more units a day, worth $1,000 a day or about $240,000 over 240 working days, with no new equipment. The same hour gained at painting would be worth nothing. The figures are illustrative; the method is to value time at the constraint at the margin it produces.

Elevating: is overtime worth it?

Continuing the example: a Saturday shift of 7.5 hours at welding only, at 25 units an hour and $40 margin per unit, adds about $7,500 of throughput, against overtime costs of about $1,200 for the welders and a supervisor. Running the whole line on Saturday would cost far more and produce the same extra output, because only welding limits it. The figures are illustrative.

Throughput accounting in brief

TOC uses three measures to judge decisions. Throughput is sales minus truly variable costs, mostly materials. Investment is money tied up in inventory and equipment. Operating expense is everything else spent to run the business, including wages. A good decision raises throughput, or lowers investment or operating expense, without hurting the others. This view explains why cutting cost at a non-constraint rarely helps, while one extra hour at the constraint adds directly to profit.

Drum, buffer, rope

TOC's scheduling method follows from the five steps. The constraint is the drum that sets the pace. A small buffer of work is kept in front of it so it never starves. The rope ties the release of new work to the drum's pace, so the rest of the line does not flood the floor with work in progress.

TOC, Lean and Six Sigma

Theory of constraintsLeanSix Sigma
Main questionWhere is the limit?Where is the waste?Where does the variation come from?
Main measureThroughputLead time, flowDefects, capability
Best used whenOutput is capped by one stepWork waits and moves too muchA defect recurs and its cause is unclear

They work well together: TOC tells you where to focus, Lean removes the waste there, and Six Sigma solves the stubborn quality problems at that point.

Beyond the factory floor

The constraint is often not a machine. It can be one engineer who approves every drawing, a single estimator, a test lab, or a policy such as "all orders over $5,000 need the owner's approval". The same five steps apply. In some businesses the constraint is the market: demand, not capacity, limits sales, and the effort belongs in sales and marketing.

A service example

A small engineering firm finds that every project waits for one senior engineer to check and stamp drawings. The other engineers are often idle, waiting for comments. Applying the five steps: the senior engineer stops doing drafting and admin work that others can do (exploit), drawings are released for checking in a planned order rather than whenever they are finished (subordinate), a second engineer is trained and authorized to check simpler drawings (elevate), and the firm then looks for the next limit, which turns out to be sales. Project lead time falls without hiring anyone new.
