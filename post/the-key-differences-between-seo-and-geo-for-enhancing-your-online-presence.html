SEO vs GEO: How to Be Found in Google and in AI Answers

A buyer looking for a supplier used to type a few words into Google and scan ten blue links. Many now ask a full question and read an answer written by an AI: Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, ChatGPT, Perplexity or Copilot. The answer names a few sources. Being one of them is the new prize.

Search engine optimization (SEO) is the work of ranking in the list of links. Generative engine optimization (GEO) is the work of being quoted in the AI answer. This article explains how the two differ, what the evidence says works, and a short checklist a small business can follow without hiring an agency.

Two ways to be found

A search engine retrieves pages and ranks them. The reader chooses where to click. A generative engine retrieves pages too, then reads them, combines them and writes one answer, with its sources cited inside the text. The reader may never click at all.

Where the term GEO comes from

The name comes from a 2024 research paper by Aggarwal and colleagues at Princeton, IIT Delhi and other institutions, presented at the KDD data mining conference. The authors built a benchmark of about 10,000 queries and tested how changes to a page affected how visible it was in generated answers.

Their main finding: adding citations to sources, quotations from relevant experts and statistics raised a page's visibility in AI answers by up to 40 percent. Writing more fluently and plainly also helped. Keyword stuffing, the oldest SEO trick, did little or nothing. The effects varied by subject, so treat 40 percent as a best case from an experiment, not a promise.

What Google says

Google has been direct about its own AI features. Its Search Central documentation says that SEO best practices still apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode, and that there are no additional requirements or special optimizations needed to appear in them. To be shown as a supporting link, a page must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet. In May 2026 Google published a longer guide, "Optimizing your website for generative AI features on Google Search," which repeats the point: write helpful, people-first content and keep the site technically sound.

That settles the question for Google. Other AI engines, such as ChatGPT and Perplexity, use their own crawlers and may weigh signals differently. The practical answer is the same: a page that is easy to crawl, clearly written and backed by evidence does well in both.

A GEO checklist for a small business

  1. Make sure you can be crawled. Check robots.txt, your firewall and your hosting settings. Some security tools block AI search crawlers such as OAI-SearchBot (ChatGPT search) and PerplexityBot by default. Decide deliberately whether to allow them.
  2. Answer one question per page, early. Put the direct answer in the first two sentences, then the detail. AI engines lift short, self-contained statements.
  3. Back claims with evidence. Name your sources and link to them. Use numbers from your own work where you have them: "we cut order errors from 4 percent to 1 percent" is more citable than "we improve accuracy."
  4. Use plain headings and tables. Clear structure helps readers and machines find the part they need. Do not bend the writing around AI. Google advises against it.
  5. Show who wrote it and when. An author name, a date and a short bio make a page easier to trust and to cite.
  6. Keep your facts consistent everywhere. Your name, address, services and prices should match on your site, your Google Business Profile, LinkedIn and directories. AI answers draw on all of them.
  7. Publish what only you know. Worked examples, original data, real costs and lessons from projects. A rewrite of what is already online gives an AI no reason to cite you.
  8. Check the answers yourself. Once a month, ask your customers' top five questions in Google AI Mode, ChatGPT and Perplexity. Note who is cited. Keep the log, because the answers change.

What not to spend money on

  • "AI-only" rewrites of pages that already rank. Google says no special optimization is needed for its AI features.
  • Separate pages for every variant of a question. Thin, repetitive pages help neither SEO nor GEO.
  • Guaranteed AI citations. No one controls which sources an AI engine picks. Treat any guarantee as a warning sign.

An llms.txt file, a plain list of your key pages for AI tools, is cheap to add and does no harm. Google says it does not need one, so do not expect it to change much on its own.

Key points

  • SEO wins a place in the list of links. GEO wins a place inside the AI answer.
  • Both start from the same foundation: pages that can be crawled, indexed and understood.
  • Research found that citations, quotations and statistics raised visibility in AI answers by up to 40 percent. Keyword stuffing did not help.
  • Google says its AI features need no special optimization beyond good SEO.
  • For a small business, the best GEO is specific, evidence-backed writing about what you actually know.
