Business Process Optimization: Map, Measure and Redesign a Process

Quak Foo LeeApr 9, 2022Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Business process optimization means changing how work flows so that it takes less time, costs less and goes wrong less often, from the customer's point of view. This article replaces two earlier ones on this site, on process optimization and on streamlining workflows, with a single method and a worked example.

The method in five steps

  1. Pick one process and define its start and end, for example "order received" to "order shipped". Choose a process that customers notice or that costs real time.
  2. Map it as it really runs, with the people who do the work, step by step. A swim lane map shows each role and every hand-off; see process documentation that people use for an example.
  3. Measure it. For each step, record the working time and the waiting time. Add two or three measures for the whole process: elapsed time, error rate and cost per transaction.
  4. Redesign. For each step, ask: can we remove it, combine it with another, automate it, or move it so work flows without waiting? Remove before you automate; automating a needless step only makes it faster.
  5. Trial, standardize and review. Run the new process for a few weeks, write it down as the new standard, and check the measures monthly.

Running the mapping workshop

  • Invite one person from each role in the process and keep the group under eight.
  • Use sticky notes on a wall or a shared whiteboard; it is faster than drawing software and everyone can move things.
  • Walk one real, recent transaction from start to end, then ask what happens when things go wrong.
  • Note waits, re-keying, approvals and rework as you go; these are where the time hides.
  • Keep the discussion on the process, not on who made which mistake.

Worked example: order handling at a small distributor

The team mapped one typical order and timed each step. The times are illustrative.

StepElapsed timeAdds value for the customer?
Order arrives by email0.5 minNo
Re-keyed into a spreadsheet6 minNo
Credit check by owner240 minNo
Entered into accounting5 minNo
Printed and walked to warehouse120 minNo
Pick and pack25 minYes
Ship and invoice10 minYes

The order took 6.8 hours from arrival to shipping, but only 35 minutes of that was work the customer would pay for. The rest was re-keying, waiting for the owner to approve credit, and walking paper to the warehouse. The redesign removed the spreadsheet, connected the web form to accounting, replaced the owner's approval of every order with a credit rule so the owner only sees exceptions, and put the pick list on a warehouse screen.

Elapsed time fell from 6.8 hours to 0.9 hours, so same-day shipping became normal, and two error-prone typing steps disappeared. Nothing about picking or packing changed. Our operational efficiency guide shows how to put a dollar value on gains like this.

Common mistakes

  • Buying software before mapping the process.
  • Mapping the process as it should run instead of as it runs.
  • Optimizing one department and moving the problem to the next.
  • Not writing down the new process, so it drifts back.
