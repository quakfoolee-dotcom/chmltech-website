Toyota Case Study: How Lean Is Too Lean?

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Toyota's purchasing and production leadership in late 2021, after the company's September production cut. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

For most of 2021, Toyota was the automaker that had beaten the chip shortage. While rivals idled plants and parked unfinished cars waiting for semiconductors, Toyota kept its lines running, because after the 2011 earthquake it had asked suppliers to hold months of chips in reserve. Then, on August 19, 2021, Toyota announced that it would cut its global production for September by about 40%. The cause was not mainly chips. COVID outbreaks in Vietnam, Malaysia and Thailand had closed suppliers of other parts that Toyota had never thought to stockpile.

The company that taught the world just-in-time had to decide what its rule should now be. How much inventory should it hold, of which parts, held by whom, and at whose cost?

Company background

The Toyota Production System, developed from the 1950s by Taiichi Ohno and others, treats excess inventory as waste. Parts arrive just in time for use, problems are exposed rather than buried under stock, and suppliers deliver in small, frequent lots. The system made Toyota one of the most efficient and highest-quality carmakers in the world and was copied across industries under the name lean manufacturing.

Just-in-time has a known weakness: when a supplier stops, the line stops soon after. Toyota learned this painfully on March 11, 2011, when the Tohoku earthquake and tsunami struck northeastern Japan. One of the plants destroyed belonged to Renesas Electronics, a key maker of automotive microcontrollers. Toyota's production fell 78% year on year in April 2011. It took four months to return Japanese production to normal and six months overseas.

What Toyota changed after 2011

Exhibit 1. Toyota's changes after the 2011 earthquake

ChangeWhat it does
RESCUE supplier databaseMaps thousands of parts back through tier-two and lower suppliers, so Toyota knows which factory makes what, and where
Business continuity plan (BCP)Classifies parts by risk; for the most critical, requires suppliers to hold extra stock, reported as two to six months of chips depending on lead time
Semiconductor know-howToyota had run its own chip plant since 1989 and understood its chip supply chain in unusual depth
Faster escalationRoutines to detect a disruption and act on it within days

The approach paid off at first. In February 2021, Toyota's chief financial officer said the company had secured one to four months of stock for various semiconductors and did not expect a major impact on production. The consultancy AlixPartners estimated that the chip shortage cost the industry about $210 billion in lost sales in 2021. Toyota kept its U.S. lines running at close to 90% of capacity through June.

The situation in late 2021

The September cut showed the limit of the plan. Toyota had protected the parts it knew were risky. The new shortage came from ordinary parts, made by suppliers in Southeast Asia whose factories were closed by public health orders. Twenty-seven of Toyota's 28 assembly lines in Japan were affected for part of September.

The options

Option A: Keep the plan as it is

The BCP worked for chips; the Southeast Asia closures were a once-in-a-century pandemic event. Keep buffers for the critical list only and protect the efficiency of just-in-time.

Option B: Widen the buffer to more parts

Extend extra stock to more categories, including ordinary parts from concentrated regions. Higher carrying cost, borne by Toyota or its suppliers.

Option C: Diversify sources instead of stocking

Qualify second suppliers in other regions for parts made in one area. Slower and costly to set up, but it protects against regional events without holding stock.

Option D: Combine visibility, targeted buffers and dual sourcing

Use the RESCUE data to rank parts by impact and recovery time, and choose stock, a second source or nothing for each.

Discussion questions

  1. Is holding months of chips a departure from the Toyota Production System, or an application of it? What did Ohno's rule actually aim to eliminate?
  2. Use Exhibit 3. How would you decide which parts deserve a buffer? Which two or three measures would you use?
  3. Who should pay for the buffer stock, Toyota or the supplier? What happens to the relationship either way?
  4. The 2011 plan protected the risks Toyota knew about. How do you plan for the risk you have not identified?
  5. When should a company stop adding buffers? What is the cost of being too safe?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Toyota did not abandon just-in-time, and it did not return to its pre-2011 settings either. It kept the business continuity plan and the RESCUE database, and continued to hold targeted stocks of critical parts, while adjusting production plans repeatedly through late 2021 and 2022 as parts shortages came and went. Even with the cuts, Toyota outsold General Motors in the United States in 2021, the first time since 1931 that GM had not been the top-selling carmaker there, which analysts largely credited to Toyota's supply chain management.

The wider industry moved toward Toyota's approach. Carmakers that had cut inventories to the bone after the 2008 financial crisis began to map their lower-tier suppliers and to hold more stock of critical parts. In Europe, a group led by BMW and SAP founded Catena-X in 2021 to build a shared supplier database similar to RESCUE.

The lasting lesson is that lean and resilient are not opposites. Toyota's system removes stock that hides problems; it does not forbid stock held on purpose against a risk the company has measured.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

Hold stock on purpose, not by habit. Lean removes inventory that hides waste. A buffer sized against a known risk is a decision, not waste. The safety stock for manufacturers post shows how to size one from demand and lead-time variation.

Know your suppliers' suppliers. Most SMEs know their direct suppliers well and little beyond them. A simple list of where your critical parts are actually made shows where a single event could stop you.

Rank parts by impact, not by value. A cheap part with one source can stop a line as surely as an expensive one. Combine an ABC analysis by value with a ranking by supply risk.

Plans protect against known risks; flexibility protects against the rest. Second sources, alternate designs and fast escalation help when the disruption is one you did not foresee.
