Clear Thinking by Shane Parrish: Book Review, Lessons Learned and How to Apply Them

Clear Thinking: Turning Ordinary Moments into Extraordinary Results by Shane Parrish (Portfolio, October 2023, 288 pages) is a book about the decisions we do not notice we are making: the email answered in anger, the meeting where we go along with the room, the habit we never question. Parrish, a Canadian who worked at an intelligence agency before founding the Farnam Street blog and its Brain Food newsletter, argues that results are shaped less by a few big decisions than by how we handle these ordinary moments. This review summarises the book, sets out the lessons we took from it for running a small or mid-sized business, and gives a practical way to apply them.

The four defaults

Parrish's starting point is that much bad thinking is not thinking at all. We react on autopilot, driven by instincts that served our ancestors well. He names four of them:

DefaultWhat it doesWhat it looks like at work
EmotionActing on a feeling before thinkingReplying to a customer's angry email within minutes, in the same tone
EgoProtecting how we look and feel about ourselvesDefending a supplier you chose long after the evidence says change
SocialGoing along with the groupApproving a project in a meeting because nobody else objected
InertiaSticking with what we already doKeeping a manual process for years because it still more or less works

Defaults cannot be removed, but they can be anticipated. Parrish's practical advice is to change the environment rather than rely on willpower: work with people whose habits you want to copy, and build routines so that the automatic behaviour is the good one.

The four strengths

StrengthWhat it means
Self-accountabilityOwn your outcomes; no excuses, even when others are at fault
Self-knowledgeKnow your strengths, blind spots and the situations that trigger your defaults
Self-controlPause between the impulse and the action
Self-confidenceSecure enough to change your mind and to be seen not knowing

The thread through all four is creating a gap between what happens and how we respond. In that gap, reasoning has a chance to work. Parrish is clear that this is character, not technique: no decision tool helps someone who is not honest with themselves.

Managing weakness

Strength is not always enough. We are more reactive when tired, hungry, stressed or rushed, so Parrish recommends safeguards: rules decided in advance, checklists, cooling-off periods, and asking someone else to look at the problem. He also offers a simple way to handle mistakes: take responsibility, learn what caused the error, decide what you will do differently, and repair any damage done to others.

The decision process

The process is sensible rather than new, but two parts stand out. Defining the problem gets more time than most of us give it, on the grounds that a well-stated problem often points to its own answer. And the margin of safety, borrowed from engineering and value investing, asks you to plan for being wrong: keep reserves, avoid decisions that cannot be undone, and set in advance the signal that tells you to stop.

Parrish also recommends writing down your reasoning when you decide, because memory rewrites the past to flatter us. A short decision journal lets you judge the decision on what you knew at the time, not only on how it turned out.

What works, and what does not

  • Strengths: short chapters, memorable stories, and a framework that is easy to recall under pressure. The emphasis on ordinary moments rather than big strategic choices is useful and less common in decision books.
  • Weaknesses: regular readers of Farnam Street will recognise much of the material. Some readers find the advice more inspiring than step-by-step, and the final part on what to want in life is a change of register from the practical middle of the book.

Lessons learned

These are the lessons we took for owners and managers of small and mid-sized businesses.

  1. Most damage comes from unforced errors. In operations work we see more value lost to rushed replies, avoided conversations and unexamined habits than to wrong strategies. Avoiding the obvious mistakes is the first gain.
  2. Design the environment, do not rely on willpower. A rule such as "no pricing decisions on the call; we reply the next morning" works better than resolving to stay calm.
  3. Inertia is the expensive default in a small business. Spreadsheets, manual re-keying and "the way we've always done it" survive because nobody decides to keep them; nobody decides to change them either. A regular review of recurring processes breaks this.
  4. Meetings amplify the social default. Ask for written views before the meeting, and ask the most junior person to speak first.
  5. Write decisions down. A few lines at the time of a decision make later reviews honest and turn experience into learning.
  6. Leave a margin of safety. Cash reserves, a second supplier, a pilot before a full rollout and a clear stop signal all buy time to recover from being wrong.

How to implement it

Week 1: notice the defaults

For one week, note each time you acted before thinking: which default, what triggered it, and what it cost. Most people find two or three recurring triggers, such as late-afternoon email, a particular customer or a weekly meeting.

Week 2: set three safeguards

TriggerSafeguardExample rule
Angry or urgent messagesCooling-off periodDraft the reply, send it after a break or the next morning
Big purchases or hiresSecond opinionAny decision over a set amount is reviewed by one other person
Group decisionsIndependent views firstEveryone writes their view before discussion starts

Week 3: start a decision journal

FieldWhat to write
Decision and dateWhat you decided, in one sentence
ProblemThe problem you are solving, stated plainly
Options consideredAt least three, including doing nothing
Why this optionThe main reasons and what you expect to happen
How you feelTired, rushed, confident, under pressure?
Kill signalWhat result would make you stop or change course
Review dateWhen you will compare the outcome with this entry

Week 4: review and repeat

Review the journal entries that have reached their review date. Separate bad luck from bad judgment: a good decision can have a bad outcome, and the reverse. Keep the safeguards that helped, drop the ones that did not, and add one more.

Worked example: replacing a key supplier

A fabricator's main steel supplier has been late three times in a quarter. The owner's first impulse is to switch immediately (emotion), but he chose the supplier himself and is reluctant to admit it is not working (ego), and switching means new paperwork and relationships (inertia). Using the process from the book:

  • Define: the problem is not "a bad supplier" but late deliveries that delay two key customers' orders.
  • Explore: switch suppliers; add a second supplier for critical items; hold more stock of those items; agree a delivery-performance review with the current supplier.
  • Evaluate: a full switch is hard to reverse and carries quality risk; a second source for critical items reduces risk at a small cost.
  • Do it, with a margin: qualify a second supplier for the ten most critical items and hold two weeks of extra stock of them while the change beds in. Kill signal: if the current supplier is late again in the next 60 days, move half the volume.
  • Learn: the journal entry is reviewed after 90 days.

The example is illustrative; the point is that each default is named, and a reversible option with a stop signal is chosen over a dramatic one.

Who should read it

Owners, managers and anyone who makes frequent decisions under time pressure. It suits readers who want a framework they can remember rather than a textbook on cognitive bias. Readers who already follow Farnam Street closely may prefer to skim the first half and read Parts 3 and 4 carefully.

Verdict

Clear Thinking does not offer many new techniques, but it makes a convincing case that better results start with handling ordinary moments well, and it gives a simple vocabulary for doing so. Its practical value comes from the safeguards and the decision journal; used for a month, they are likely to change how you decide more than the book's stories alone.
