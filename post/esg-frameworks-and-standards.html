ESG Reporting Frameworks in 2026: A Guide for Canadian Small and Mid-Sized Businesses

Quak Foo LeeApr 3, 2022Updated Nov 13, 20266 min read

ESG (environmental, social and governance) reporting has gone through a large consolidation since this article was first written in 2022. Several of the frameworks it described have merged or disappeared, Canada has its own sustainability standards, and the rules on green marketing claims have changed twice. This updated guide is written for small and mid-sized Canadian businesses that are not required to publish a sustainability report but are increasingly asked for ESG information anyway.

Why a small business gets asked

Most mandatory sustainability reporting applies to large listed companies and banks. Smaller firms meet it indirectly, through four channels: customers who must report on their supply chains, lenders and investors who need climate information about their borrowers, European customers covered by EU rules, and the business's own marketing claims.

Three changes stand out. First, the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) issued IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 in June 2023, and the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) disbanded in October 2023 after its recommendations were fully built into IFRS S2. A company applying the ISSB standards meets the TCFD recommendations. Second, the SASB Standards are now maintained by the ISSB and supply the industry-specific metrics in IFRS S2. Third, the Climate Disclosure Standards Board, which this article used to list, was folded into the IFRS Foundation in 2022.

Where Canada stands

The Canadian Sustainability Standards Board finalized CSDS 1 (general requirements) and CSDS 2 (climate) on 18 December 2024. They follow IFRS S1 and S2 with some transition reliefs, such as more time for Scope 3 emissions, and are available for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2025. They are voluntary: on 23 April 2025 the Canadian Securities Administrators paused work on the rule that would have made climate disclosure mandatory for public companies, and that pause still stands. Federally regulated banks and insurers do face climate disclosure requirements under OSFI Guideline B-15, which is one reason lenders ask their business clients for emissions data.

In practice, voluntary does not mean irrelevant. Large Canadian companies and their banks are building reports on CSDS and ISSB lines, and the questions reach their suppliers as questionnaires.

If you sell to European customers

The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) was scaled back by the "Omnibus" package agreed in December 2025. It now applies to companies with more than 1,000 employees and over €450 million in turnover, plus large non-EU groups with significant EU business. Importantly for Canadian suppliers, it adds a value-chain cap: a reporting company may not demand more information from a business partner with fewer than 1,000 employees than is covered by the EU's voluntary standard for small and mid-sized enterprises, known as VSME. Its basic module covers topics such as energy use and greenhouse gas emissions, pollution, water, waste, workforce, health and safety, pay and training, and anti-corruption. If you prepare that data once, you can answer most EU customer requests.

Green claims and the Competition Act

Since June 2024 the Competition Act has expressly covered misleading environmental claims. Claims about a product's environmental benefits must be based on adequate and proper tests, and claims about the environmental benefits of a business or its activities must be substantiated. Bill C-15, which received royal assent on 26 March 2026, softened two points: business-level claims now need adequate and proper substantiation rather than an "internationally recognized methodology", and private parties can no longer take these claims directly to the Competition Tribunal, so enforcement rests with the Competition Bureau. The prohibition on false or misleading claims remains. Before you say "carbon neutral", "net zero" or "eco-friendly" on a website or label, keep the evidence on file.

A practical starting point for a small business

  1. Collect one core data set each year: fuel and electricity use, Scope 1 and 2 emissions calculated with the GHG Protocol, headcount, turnover, training hours, safety incidents, and your main policies (code of conduct, health and safety, anti-corruption, privacy).
  2. Map it to the requests you actually get. Most customer questionnaires and the EU VSME basic module ask for the same items in different layouts.
  3. Add Scope 3 only where it matters, such as purchased materials for a manufacturer or freight for a distributor, and say how you estimated it.
  4. Check every environmental claim in your marketing against the evidence you hold.
  5. Use a management system to keep it going. An ISO 14001 environmental management system, or the integrated ISO 9001, 14001 and 45001 approach, gives the data an owner and a yearly review.

Energy and emissions data often reveal savings too. Investments that cut fuel or electricity may also qualify for the clean technology tax credits extended in Budget 2026.
