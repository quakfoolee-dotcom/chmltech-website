Lululemon Case Study: The 2013 Yoga Pants Recall

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Christine Day, Lululemon's chief executive, in March 2013. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

In March 2013, store managers at Lululemon Athletica began reporting the same complaint from customers: some of the company's best-selling black yoga pants became see-through when the wearer bent over. The pants were made from Luon, Lululemon's own nylon and Lycra fabric, by the same manufacturer and with the same materials the company had used since 2004. Nothing in the standard laboratory tests had flagged a problem.

The affected styles were the core of the business: the black Wunder Under and Groove pants that had made the brand. Christine Day, the chief executive, had to decide quickly. Pulling every suspect pair would empty shelves for months and cut tens of millions of dollars from the year's revenue. Selling through, or quietly replacing stock, would protect the quarter and put the brand's reputation for quality at risk.

Company background

Chip Wilson founded Lululemon in Vancouver in 1998 to sell technical yoga wear, and the company created a category: premium athletic clothing worn far beyond the gym. It sold through its own stores, staffed by "educators" and tied to local yoga and running communities, and kept prices high, with few discounts. The company went public in 2007. Day, a former Starbucks executive, became chief executive in 2008 and led a period of rapid growth.

The situation in March 2013

The defect was hard to detect. The fabric looked normal on the table and passed tests for pilling, shrinkage, abrasion and colourfastness by an independent testing company. It became sheer only when stretched in four directions at once, as it is in a yoga pose. Day later told investors that the only way to test for it was to put the pants on and bend over. The cause was not yet known; it could be the fabric, the dyeing or the finishing.

The options

Option A: Pull all black Luon pants from the suspect shipments and say so publicly

Remove the stock from stores and the website, offer refunds or exchanges, and announce the problem before customers spread it. Expect a shortage of core styles for months.

Option B: Test and pull only confirmed defects

Inspect stock and remove only pants that fail a stretch test. Less revenue lost, but slower, and hard to do consistently in hundreds of stores.

Option C: Sell through and correct future production

Keep current stock on sale, handle complaints case by case, and fix the cause in the next batch. Protects the quarter, but if the problem becomes public, the company will look as if it hid it.

Discussion questions

  1. Lululemon's product passed every laboratory test it ran. What does that tell you about how test methods should be chosen?
  2. Use Exhibit 2. Is a $60 million hit to revenue a reasonable price for protecting the brand? How would you decide?
  3. How should the company talk to customers, investors and staff in the first 48 hours?
  4. Who is accountable for the defect: the supplier, the product team or the chief executive? Does the answer change what you do next?
  5. What quality controls would you put in place so the next problem is found before it reaches stores?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Day chose the full recall. On March 18, 2013, Lululemon announced that it was pulling the affected black Luon pants, about 17% of the women's pants in its stores, from shelves and its website, and offering customers refunds or exchanges. The company warned of a shortage of those styles in the near term and later estimated that the problem would reduce 2013 revenue by $57 million to $67 million. The share price fell about 5% on the news.

The recall itself was widely seen as the right call, but the handling drew ridicule. Day's remark that the only test was to bend over in the pants became a punchline, and reports that some store staff asked customers to demonstrate the problem made it worse; the company said that was not its policy. The chief product officer, Sheree Waterson, left the company. In June 2013 Day announced she would step down once a successor was found, and the shares fell about 16% in a day.

The year ended with a second crisis. In November 2013, founder Chip Wilson said in a Bloomberg television interview that some women's bodies were not suited to the pants. The backlash was immediate, he apologised, and in December he stepped down as chairman. Laurent Potdevin, president of TOMS Shoes, was named chief executive.

Lululemon recovered. It overhauled its quality checks and product leadership and grew into one of the largest athletic apparel companies in the world. The recall is now taught as a case in which the operational decision was right and the communication around it was not.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

Test the product the way the customer uses it. A test that does not reproduce real use gives false confidence. This is a core idea of quality management systems and of ISO 9001: verify against the requirement, not only against a standard method.

A change you did not make can still change your product. The supplier and the materials were the same, but the product changed. Incoming inspection and periodic re-testing catch drift that a supplier may not report.

Recall fast, and plan what you will say. Pulling the product protected the brand; careless words cost the chief executive her job. Prepare a short, factual message for customers, staff and investors before you need it.

Quality problems repeat until the system changes. Colour bleeding in 2012 was a warning. Track defects and complaints over time, not one incident at a time.
