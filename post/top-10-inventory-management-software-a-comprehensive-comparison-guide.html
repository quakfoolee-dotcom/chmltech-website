Inventory Management Software for Small Businesses in 2026: 10 Options Compared

Quak Foo LeeMar 10, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20266 min read

This comparison was first written in 2023. Since then one of the original ten has disappeared (Intuit discontinued TradeGecko, which had become QuickBooks Commerce, after 31 August 2023), several vendors have changed their prices more than once, and the choice for a small business now depends less on features and more on what kind of business you run. This rewrite groups the options by job, gives list prices checked in November 2026, and adds a worked example of the payback.

First, decide which job the software must do

Most poor choices come from buying for the wrong job: a manufacturer on a stock-tracking app, or a five-person distributor paying for an ERP. Start by placing your business in one of four groups.

A note on Katana for production scheduling

An earlier article on this site covered production scheduling with Katana. That article has been folded into this one. Katana, now marketed as Katana Cloud Inventory, remains a good fit for small manufacturers that sell online: it links sales orders to manufacturing orders, shows whether materials are available for each order, and lets you drag orders into a new sequence when priorities change. It is lighter than a full MRP. If you need finite capacity scheduling across work centres, routings with setup times, or detailed shop-floor costing, compare it with MRPeasy, and read ERP vs MRP before you decide.

What to check before you buy

  1. Your accounting link. Check that it posts cost of goods sold and inventory value correctly to QuickBooks, Xero or Sage, not just invoices.
  2. How you count and locate stock. Bins, lots, serial numbers and expiry dates are either built in or painful to add.
  3. Your sales channels. Shopify, Amazon, a B2B portal and EDI all need tested connectors.
  4. The pricing basis. Per user, per order, per location or flat rate. Model your volume in two years, not today.
  5. Reorder planning. At minimum, reorder points and lead times by item. Better tools also support ABC analysis and safety stock.
  6. Exit and data ownership. Make sure you can export items, transactions and history if you outgrow the tool.

Worked example: what does it pay back?

A distributor with 1,800 items and five office users runs its stock in spreadsheets. Average inventory at cost is $480,000. Holding stock costs about 25% a year once money tied up, space, insurance, damage and obsolescence are counted. With reorder points and better purchasing in an order-and-stock system, the business expects to cut average inventory by 15% without more stock-outs. The figures are illustrative.

Implementation help and data import$6,000

The subscription is $349 a month, or $4,188 a year. Cutting average inventory by $72,000 saves $18,000 a year in carrying cost, so the net annual benefit is $13,812 and the one-time cost pays back in about 10 months. Fewer stock-outs and less time spent counting are on top of that. The main risk is the 15% reduction: it only happens if someone owns the reorder settings and reviews them every month.
