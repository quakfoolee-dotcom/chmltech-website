From Mechanical Completion to Handover: The Commissioning Sequence

Quak Foo LeeNov 13, 20267 min read

A plant can be fully built and still be weeks from making product. The gap is commissioning: proving, system by system, that what was built works as designed, and only then putting process materials into it. Projects that rush this stage tend to pay for it at start-up, with leaks, trips, damaged equipment and, at worst, incidents.

This three-part series follows the 50,000 t/y plant from the FEED series from the end of construction to handover. This part sets out the sequence. Part 2 covers the pre-startup safety review, the last check before hazardous materials go in. Part 3 covers start-up, the punch list and the performance test.

Systemization: the unit of commissioning

A plant is not commissioned all at once. It is divided into systems, each a set of equipment that can be tested together, with clear boundaries on the P&IDs. Each system gets a test pack: the drawings marked up with its boundary, the test procedures, the check sheets and the punch list. Construction hands over by system, so commissioning can start on the first systems while others are still being built.

Utilities come first, because everything else needs them. Then the control and safety systems, because nothing should be operated without its protections working. Then the process systems, in the order the process runs.

Pre-commissioning in practice

Pre-commissioning is the unglamorous work that prevents most start-up problems. For the feed system it includes:

  • Flushing and cleaning. Construction debris left in pipes ends up in pump impellers, control valves and exchanger tubes. Temporary strainers protect the pumps for the first weeks.
  • Leak and pressure testing. Hydrostatic tests prove the strength of the piping at the time of construction. A leak test with nitrogen before start-up finds the flanges disturbed since.
  • Instrument loop checks. Each instrument is checked from the field device to the control room display, so that a high level in T-101 shows as a high level on the right screen and trips the right pump.
  • Rotating equipment. Motors are run uncoupled to check rotation and vibration, then coupled and aligned. Pumps are not run dry.

Commissioning: proving the systems together

With components proven, systems are run together on safe fluids. The feed system runs on water or the plant solvent, P-101 pushing through E-101 into R-101, with flow controlled and E-101 heated on steam. Control loops are tuned. Alarms are triggered from the field. Every safety instrumented function is tested end to end, from sensor to final element, against the cause and effect diagram. The functional safety standard IEC 61511 requires the safety system to be validated before the hazards it protects against are present.

Water runs also test the people. Operators run the plant on their procedures, find the steps that are wrong or missing, and fix them before the procedures matter.

Who leads, and when

The handover points in Figure 1 are also changes of responsibility. Construction leads until mechanical completion. A commissioning team, usually a mix of the engineering contractor, vendors and the owner's future operators, leads until the plant is ready for start-up. Operations leads once process materials are in. Projects that blur these lines tend to discover, during start-up, that nobody owned a particular check.

Key points

  • Commissioning proves the plant works before process materials go in, one system at a time.
  • Divide the plant into systems with clear boundaries and a test pack each; start with utilities and the control and safety systems.
  • Pre-commissioning (cleaning, testing, loop checks, run-in) prevents most start-up problems.
  • Test every safety function end to end before the hazard is present.
  • Be clear who leads each stage and what proves it is complete.
