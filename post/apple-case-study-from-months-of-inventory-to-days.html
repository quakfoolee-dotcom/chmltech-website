Apple Case Study: From Months of Inventory to Days

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Tim Cook, who joined Apple as senior vice president of worldwide operations in March 1998, and to Steve Jobs, who hired him. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

When Tim Cook arrived at Apple in March 1998, the company had just lost about $1 billion in a single year. Its products were confusing, its factories and warehouses were full, and computers were losing value on the shelves every week. Steve Jobs, back as interim chief executive since 1997, had already cut the product line to a handful of models. He had hired Cook, a supply chain manager from Compaq, to fix the operation behind them.

Cook's question was how far to go. Apple could tidy up its existing operations: fewer suppliers, tighter forecasting, a few warehouse closures. Or it could stop making computers itself, hand production to contract manufacturers, and run with almost no inventory at all, just as the company was about to launch the product meant to save it, the iMac.

Company background

Apple had grown in the 1980s on the Macintosh. In the 1990s it lost its way: a stream of overlapping models, a failed attempt to license the Mac operating system to clone makers, and a run of short-lived chief executives. In fiscal 1997 Apple lost $1.045 billion on sales of about $7 billion. It ran its own factories in California, Ireland and Singapore and kept stock in warehouses around the world. In 1995 it had been carrying about $1.8 billion in inventory.

When Jobs returned, he drew a two-by-two grid on a whiteboard: consumer and professional, desktop and portable. Apple would make one great product for each box and cancel the rest. Roughly 70% of the product line was cut. A short, simple range was the precondition for everything Cook did next.

The situation in 1998

In the personal computer business, inventory is a perishable good. Components drop in price every few months and new models make old ones hard to sell. Cook later said that computer inventory loses value at 1% to 2% a week, and that it should be managed like dairy products: once past its freshness date, it is a problem. Dell, the industry leader in operations, built computers to order and held only days of stock. Apple held about two months.

The cuts Cook had in mind carried real risks. With 24 key suppliers instead of 100, a single supplier's failure could stop production. Contract manufacturers would build Apple's products alongside other companies' and would need to be managed closely to protect quality and secrets. And a company with six days of stock has no cushion if demand is higher than expected, at the moment of its most important product launch in a decade.

The options

Option A: Tidy up the existing operation

Rationalise suppliers and forecasting, close some warehouses, keep the factories. Lower risk, but Apple would still carry weeks of stock in a business where stock loses value weekly.

Option B: Go lean in-house

Keep the factories but run them on a build-to-order model like Dell's, with suppliers holding components nearby. A large change in Apple's own plants, with capital still tied up in manufacturing.

Option C: Outsource manufacturing and run on days of inventory

Close factories and warehouses, concentrate spending with a small number of suppliers on tough terms, and move assembly to contract manufacturers. The largest gain in cash and cost, and the largest change in how Apple controls its products.

Discussion questions

  1. Why does inventory cost a computer company more than it costs a hardware store or a food wholesaler? What does that imply for how much to hold?
  2. Use Exhibit 1. What made it possible to cut inventory from about two months to six days in six months? Which change mattered most?
  3. What does Apple give up when it stops building its own products? How would you protect quality and confidentiality with a contract manufacturer?
  4. With only 24 key suppliers, how would you manage the risk that one of them fails?
  5. Could Cook's changes have worked without Jobs's decision to cut the product line? Why or why not?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Cook took the most aggressive option. Within months he reduced Apple's key suppliers from about 100 to 24, used the concentrated spending to negotiate better prices and terms, and persuaded many suppliers to locate near Apple's plants. He closed about half of the company's 19 warehouses. By September 1998 inventory was down from about a month to six days, and by September 1999 to about two days. The time to build a computer fell from four months to two. Over the following years Apple moved its assembly to contract manufacturers in Asia. Cook described inventory as "fundamentally evil."

The iMac launched in August 1998 and sold about 278,000 units in its first six weeks. Apple swung from the $1.045 billion loss of fiscal 1997 to a profit of $309 million in fiscal 1998.

The operating model Cook built became the base for everything that followed, including the iPod, the iPhone and the iPad. Apple used its purchasing scale to secure components years ahead, prepaying suppliers and even buying production equipment for them. More than a decade later it still carried less than a week of inventory while its revenue grew more than twenty-fold. Cook became chief operating officer in 2005 and chief executive in 2011.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

Simplify the product range before you optimise the supply chain. Apple could run on days of stock only because it sold a few products. Fewer variants make forecasting easier and every other improvement larger.

Know what your inventory costs you. If your stock loses value, ties up cash or risks becoming obsolete, put a number on it. The reorder point and EOQ and safety stock posts show how to set levels from demand and lead time rather than habit.

Concentrate spending to gain leverage, and manage the risk that comes with it. Fewer suppliers bring better terms and closer cooperation, but each one matters more. Keep a backup source for critical parts.

Outsource what others do better, but keep control of what makes you different. Apple handed over assembly and kept design, software and the relationship with suppliers.
