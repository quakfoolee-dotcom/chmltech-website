What a Pilot Plant Is For: The Questions It Must Answer

Quak Foo LeeNov 11, 20265 min read

A pilot plant exists to answer the questions the laboratory cannot. Its product is data. A pilot campaign that makes good material and leaves those questions open has not done its job.

Post 1 showed why size changes behaviour, using a reaction scaled from 1 L to 10,000 L. This post covers the middle step, the 100 L pilot reactor: what it should be designed to find out, how large it should be, and what to have in hand before it is shut down. Post 3 uses the results.

Start from the questions

The pilot plant sits between two lists. The first is the list of questions the lab work left open. The second is the list of numbers the commercial design needs. Each pilot run should move an item from the first list to the second.

Table 1. Eight questions a pilot plant commonly has to answer

QuestionWhat to measureDesign decision it feeds
How fast is heat released, and can any reactant build up unreacted?Heat flow and conversion against time during the additionAddition time, cooling system, relief design
Is the result sensitive to mixing?Yield and impurities at two or three agitator speedsAgitator type and power, feed point location
How well does the product filter, wash and dry?Filtration rate, cake moisture, drying curveFilter and dryer type and size
What do recycled streams do over many batches?Impurity levels in recycled solvent over consecutive runsPurge rate, solvent make-up, purification steps
Which materials resist the process fluids?Corrosion coupons and gasket samples exposed in the reactorMaterials of construction
Does commercial-grade raw material give the same product?Runs using the grade and supplier planned for the plantRaw material specifications in the design basis
What leaves as waste and emissions?Quantity and composition of every waste stream and ventTreatment systems and permit applications
Can operators run and clean it?Blockages, fouling, cleaning time, manual interventionsOperating procedures, staffing, cycle time

Not every project has all eight. A project should write down its own list before the pilot plant is designed, because the list decides which instruments, sample points and equipment the pilot plant needs.

How large should the pilot be?

The pilot should be large enough to show the effects of scale and small enough to change cheaply. The step that is safe to take depends on what is in the process.

Table 2. Typical size of one scale-up step

Type of operationTypical step in volumeWhy
Single-phase liquid or gas reactions with known kinetics100 to 1,000 timesBehaviour can be calculated with confidence
Liquid reactions limited by mixing or heat removal50 to 100 timesResults depend on vessel geometry
Gas-liquid and liquid-liquid reactions20 to 100 timesContact area between the phases is hard to predict
Solids: crystallization, filtration, drying10 to 50 timesParticle size and cake behaviour change with scale

These ranges are a guide and vary with how well the process is understood. In the example, each step is 100 times: 1 L to 100 L, then 100 L to 10,000 L. If the product had to be crystallized and filtered, that part of the process would justify a smaller step or a separate test on vendor equipment.

Run until the results repeat

One good run shows that the process can work. A design needs to know how it works on an ordinary day. That takes consecutive runs under the same conditions, with every input and output weighed.

In the example, ten consecutive runs gave yields between 85% and 89%, with a mean of 87.2%. The commercial design in Post 3 uses 87%. Using the best run, 89%, would understate raw material use and waste for every year of operation.

What to have before you shut the pilot down

Table 3. Data to collect before the pilot campaign ends

DataTest of completeness
Mass balanceEverything in and out weighed. Totals agree within about 2%.
Energy balanceHeat of reaction and heat transfer coefficient measured on the pilot reactor
Yield and qualityAt least five consecutive runs at the design conditions, using commercial-grade raw materials
Operating limitsRuns at the edges of temperature, addition rate and concentration, showing where quality is lost
Recycle effectsRecycled solvent used for enough runs to show whether impurities level off
Separation dataFiltration, washing and drying rates measured on representative material
MaterialsCorrosion rates from coupons exposed for the whole campaign
Waste and emissionsEach stream measured and analysed
Product samplesEnough material for customer approval

The first two rows matter most. A pilot plant without flow meters, load cells and temperature measurements on the jacket cannot produce a mass or energy balance, however well it runs.

Five common mistakes

  • Building the pilot to make samples. It is designed around the product and has too few instruments to give design data.
  • Using lab-grade raw materials. Trace impurities in commercial grades are a frequent cause of surprises at start-up.
  • Stopping after the first good run. The design is then based on the best result.
  • Running once through with fresh solvent. The plant will recycle, and impurities that build up never appear in the pilot.
  • Keeping no record of problems. Blockages, foaming and cleaning difficulties are design information and should be logged like any other result.

Takeaway. List the questions before designing the pilot plant, instrument it to close a mass and energy balance, and run it until the results repeat. In the example, ten runs give a design yield of 87%.
