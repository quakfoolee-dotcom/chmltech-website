Wide, Narrow or Very Narrow Aisles? Warehouse Density Against Access

Part 2 found that, laid out well, the U-shape, straight-through and L-shape pick at almost the same cost. This part keeps the U-shape and asks the next question every growing warehouse meets: when the racks are full, do you narrow the aisles to fit more pallets in, or lease more space?

Narrower aisles mean more pallet positions in the same building, but a different truck, more cost up front and less room for the people working in the aisles. The trade-off is density against access. This part runs three aisle widths through the same shift and prices the extra positions against Metro Vancouver rents.

Three aisle widths, three trucks

The aisle width is set by the truck that has to turn or work in it. Aisles for a standard counterbalance truck are usually about 12 ft (3.7 m); reach trucks and other narrow-aisle trucks work in about 9 to 10 ft (2.7 to 3 m); very-narrow-aisle (VNA) trucks fit aisles of about 6 ft (1.8 m) or less.

Table 1. The three set-ups

Set-upAisleTruckHow it stores a palletWhat else it needs
Wide aisles4 mCounterbalance truckDrives in, turns 90 degrees to face the rack, liftsNothing special; the same truck can also load trailers
Narrow aisles3 mReach truckTurns in the aisle in less room, reaches the forks outA trained operator; it is not built for trailers
Very narrow aisles2 mWire-guided turret truckDrives straight down the aisle and swivels its forks to either side; never turns in the aisleA superflat floor in the aisles, a guide wire or rail, VNA-tolerance racking, and rules that keep people out of an aisle while the truck is in it. A man-down turret truck can also fetch pallets from staging, but loses time each trip leaving and rejoining the guide wire

The same building, three ways

All three use the Part 2 U-shape: the same 72 by 52 m building, three receiving and two shipping doors, staging, packing, crew, 300 orders and 14 inbound trucks. The racks run across the building with front and back cross-aisles, and the busiest SKUs sit nearest packing. Only the aisle width changes, and with it the number of rack rows:

  • Wide aisles (4 m): 11 aisles, 660 pick faces. That is fewer than the 780 SKUs, so the 120 slowest SKUs share a face, two to a pallet position.
  • Narrow aisles (3 m): the Part 1 and 2 layout. 14 aisles, 840 pick faces: one per SKU with some to spare.
  • Very narrow aisles (2 m): 17 aisles, 1,020 pick faces. The turret truck and pickers never share an aisle: whoever arrives second waits at the aisle end.

All three keep the same clear height: a floor position and three beam levels. In a taller building VNA gains more, because turret trucks lift higher than counterbalance trucks.

  • Picking barely changes. The busy SKUs sit near packing in all three, so pickers need 18.0, 17.7 and 17.5 hours a day. Across ten other simulated days the order never changed, and wide aisles were never more than 0.85 picker-hours a day behind very narrow aisles. At this volume more faces even help a little: more of the busy SKUs fit in the front bays. From 400 orders a day, with more pickers waiting at aisle ends, very narrow aisles fall behind narrow aisles. In wide aisles the shared faces push the slowest SKUs to the back, which costs little because they are rarely picked.
  • Narrow aisles put away fastest. The reach truck needs 2.15 minutes a pallet. The counterbalance truck needs 2.39: it has to turn in the aisle to face the rack. Dock-to-stock is 33 minutes against 50.
  • Very narrow aisles cost time at the aisle ends. The turret truck is set to store a pallet 10 seconds faster than a reach truck, but each trip off the guide wire to staging costs time, and it cannot enter an aisle with a picker in it. It needs 2.35 minutes a pallet and waits 9.4 minutes in the shift. Pickers wait 21 minutes in total, against about 1 in the other set-ups.

The waiting grows with volume. At 400 orders a day, with more pickers in the aisles, the turret truck waits 15.5 minutes a shift; at 600, 39.7 minutes, while the other two stay under a minute, and pickers lose 85 minutes in total waiting to get into an aisle. The more people pick from the ground level of VNA aisles, the more they and the truck get in each other's way, which is why many VNA sites keep their fast movers in a separate wide-aisle pick zone.

To compare like with like, the extra positions are priced against leasing the same storage elsewhere. The wide-aisle rack zone holds one pallet position for every 8.6 sq ft. At C$25.99 a sq ft a year, one leased position costs

\[R = a \times r = 8.56\ \text{sq ft} \times \$25.99 \approx \$223\ \text{a position a year}\]

where \(a\) is the floor area per position and \(r\) the gross rent. That counts the rack floor only, with no second dock, staff or shuttle trucks, so it understates the real cost of a second building. The payback is then

\[\text{Payback} = \frac{\text{One-off cost}}{n \times R + \Delta L}\]

where \(n\) is the number of added positions and \(\Delta L\) the labour saved a year against wide aisles (picking plus put-away time at $40 an hour, 250 days a year). For narrow aisles the existing racks are reused; taking them down and re-erecting them is our assumption at 25% to 40% of their new price. For very narrow aisles all racking is new, to VNA tolerances. The payback is at 300 orders a day with one truck; from 400 orders every set-up needs a second truck, and for very narrow aisles that second truck is another turret truck at $142,000 to $284,000, not counted here.

  • Narrow aisles are the cheaper step. 720 more positions for $185 to $539 each, paid back in 0.8 to 2.3 years against leasing about 6,165 sq ft.
  • Very narrow aisles add twice the positions at a higher price each. 1,440 more positions at $479 to $1,314 each, because every position needs new VNA racking, and the turret truck and the floor come on top. Payback is 2.1 to 5.8 years.
  • The time saving is small. About $11,000 and $6,000 a year: the case for narrower aisles is the space, not the speed. Those are minutes of people already paid for the shift, so treat them as capacity rather than cash.
  • The payback is real only if you need the positions. Rent avoided is the whole saving. If the racks are not full most of the year, or slotting and clearing dead stock would free enough space (see cube utilization and slotting), narrower aisles buy capacity you pay for and do not use.

What the shift does not show

  • One truck per aisle. VNA trucks are made for one aisle width; if the only turret truck is down, nothing else can work those aisles.
  • Operators. Reach and turret trucks need more training, and experienced VNA operators are harder to find and keep.
  • Safety rules. VNA sites keep pedestrians out of an aisle while the truck works in it, usually with gates, sensors or a permit system. Narrow aisles shared by people and reach trucks need clear give-way rules.
  • Racks in Vancouver. Seismic design and anchoring raise rack costs above the published averages, and moving racks needs an engineer's sign-off.
  • Fire and sprinklers. More pallets and narrower flue spaces can change sprinkler requirements; check with your fire protection engineer before narrowing aisles.
  • Lease terms. Leasing more space is flexible; a VNA floor and guide wire stay with the building.

How to check your own building

  1. Measure how full the racks are, month by month for a year. Narrower aisles pay only for positions you would otherwise rent.
  2. Count the positions each width gives you, on a scale plan of your own building, with the clear height and the columns.
  3. Price the space you would lease instead, including additional rent, shuttling between buildings and a second set of staff.
  4. Get quotes, for racks (with seismic design), trucks, and, for VNA, a floor survey.
  5. Count the people in the aisles. If many picks are from the ground level of the racks, plan where pickers will work while the turret truck is in an aisle.

What the model leaves out

  • One shift with one fixed seed per day, single-order case picking, one pallet truck (two from 400 orders a day).
  • The same building height for all three; truck lift heights and speeds at height are not modelled.
  • Truck differences are fixed times, not results: the counterbalance truck takes 15 seconds longer to store a pallet, the turret truck 10 seconds less, plus 20 seconds a trip to leave and rejoin the guide wire.
  • At 350 orders a day, just below the switch to two trucks, all three set-ups leave some inbound pallets not put away by 15:00.
  • No replenishment of pick faces, no returns, no breakdowns.
  • Prices are published ranges in US dollars at one exchange rate, with no resale value for the old truck and no financing cost.

Key points

  • In this building, 3 m aisles hold 720 more pallet positions than 4 m aisles, and 2 m aisles 1,440 more.
  • Picking cost hardly depends on aisle width when the fast movers are near packing. Very narrow aisles add waiting at the aisle ends, which grows with volume.
  • Against Metro Vancouver rents, narrow aisles paid back in about 0.8 to 2.3 years and very narrow aisles in about 2.1 to 5.8 years, but only if the extra positions would otherwise be leased.
  • Check how full the racks really are before you pay for density.
