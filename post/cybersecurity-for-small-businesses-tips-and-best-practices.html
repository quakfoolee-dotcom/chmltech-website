Cybersecurity for Small Businesses in Canada: The 13 Baseline Controls and a 90-Day Plan

Quak Foo LeeMar 16, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

This article was first written in 2023 as a list of general tips. It has been rewritten around the Canadian framework that small businesses can actually follow and be certified against, with a 90-day starting plan.

Why small businesses are targeted

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (the Cyber Centre) judges cybercrime to be the threat most likely to affect small and medium organizations: ransomware, stolen passwords, and fraud such as fake invoices or requests to change a supplier's bank details. These attacks are not personal. They are automated and aimed at whoever has unpatched software, reused passwords or staff who have not seen the tricks before. AI tools have made phishing emails more convincing and voice impersonation cheaper, so the old advice to look for spelling mistakes no longer works.

The 13 baseline controls

The Cyber Centre's Baseline Cyber Security Controls for Small and Medium Organizations is written for organizations with fewer than 500 employees. It applies the 80/20 rule: a short list of lower-cost controls that gives most of the protection. They are grouped below.

A 90-day starting plan

WhenActionBaseline controls
Week 1Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, accounting, banking and remote access; remove old accountsControls 5 and 12
Week 2Set every computer and phone to update automatically; replace anything that no longer gets updatesControls 2 and 4
Week 3Back up key data daily, keep one copy offline or immutable, and test a restoreControl 7
Week 4Write a one-page incident plan: who to call, how to isolate a device, how to reach your bank and insurerControl 1
Month 2Run short phishing and payment-fraud training; agree a call-back rule for any change to bank detailsControl 6
Month 3Review cloud services and IT providers: who has admin rights, where data is kept, what they logControls 8 and 10

The first three weeks give the largest reduction in risk for the least money. Multi-factor authentication stops most attacks that start with a stolen password, automatic updates close the holes that ransomware uses, and a tested offline backup is what lets you recover without paying.

Certification: when a customer asks for proof

  • CyberSecure Canada is the federal certification program for small and medium organizations, run by Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada. It is voluntary and is based on the baseline controls. An accredited certification body audits you, and the certification mark is valid for two years. It is a useful answer when larger customers send security questionnaires.
  • The Canadian Program for Cyber Security Certification (CPCSC) is mandatory for some defence suppliers. Level 1, an annual self-assessment, was introduced on 14 April 2026 and started appearing in select National Defence contracts in summer 2026. Level 2 requires an external assessment every three years. If you supply, or want to supply, defence work, start with the Level 1 self-assessment.

If something goes wrong

Isolate the affected device from the network, but do not wipe it. Call your IT provider, your bank if money moved, and your insurer if you have cyber cover. Report the incident to the Cyber Centre and fraud to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. If personal information was involved and there is a real risk of significant harm to the people affected, organizations covered by PIPEDA must report the breach to the Privacy Commissioner of Canada and notify those people, and must keep a record of every breach for two years. Quebec and some sectors have their own rules.

Operational technology

For manufacturers and process plants, control systems need the same discipline: separate the plant network from the office network, remove default passwords on PLCs and HMIs, control remote access by vendors, and keep offline copies of control programs. Treat a change to control system access as a change under your management of change procedure.
