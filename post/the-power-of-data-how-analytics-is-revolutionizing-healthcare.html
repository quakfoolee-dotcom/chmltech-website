Healthcare Analytics and Patient Flow: Using Data to Improve Care Processes

Quak Foo LeeJan 18, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Health care produces large amounts of data, but the improvements that matter most usually come from using it to see how patients, staff and supplies move through a process. This article replaces two short 2023 articles on this site, one on healthcare analytics and one on digital process improvement in healthcare, and adds current Canadian data and a worked example.

The problem is often flow, not effort

The Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) reported in June 2026 that emergency departments had about 16.1 million visits in 2024–25, and that one in 10 patients admitted through emergency, around 180,000 people, waited more than 48 hours for an inpatient bed. CIHI's analysts stressed that the causes lie largely outside the emergency department: limited access to primary care, and shortages of long-term care and home care places that keep hospital beds occupied by patients ready to leave.

That is a classic process problem. A queue forms in front of the step with the least capacity, not where the staff are working hardest. Data helps by showing where the queue really starts: time stamps from arrival to triage, decision to admit, bed request, bed assigned and discharge make the bottleneck visible.

Five places analytics helps

AreaWhat to measureWhat it lets you change
Patient flowArrivals by hour, time to bed, discharge times, bed occupancyMatch staffing to demand; plan discharges earlier in the day
SchedulingNo-shows, cancellations, appointment length by typeReminders, overbooking rules, right-sized slots
Quality and safetyReadmissions, infections, falls, medication errorsFind the steps where harm starts
Supplies and pharmacyUsage by unit, expiry, stock-outsPar levels and reorder points instead of guesswork
Revenue and billingClaim rejections, days to paymentFix the intake step that causes most rejections

Worked example: no-shows in a clinic

A physiotherapy clinic books 60 appointments a day and loses 10% to no-shows. Its booking data shows most no-shows are first appointments booked more than two weeks ahead. The clinic adds a text reminder two days before and a waitlist for short-notice openings. If no-shows fall to 7%, it recovers about 432 appointments a year over 240 working days, worth about $38,880 at $90 a visit. The figures are illustrative; the method is to find the pattern in the data first, then change the step that causes it.

Making it work in a clinic or care organization

  1. Start with one flow and one measure, such as time from referral to first appointment.
  2. Use the data you already record. Scheduling and electronic medical record systems hold time stamps that are rarely analysed.
  3. Map the process with the staff who run it, including the hand-offs to other organizations.
  4. Protect privacy. Use de-identified or aggregated data for analysis, and follow your province's health privacy law and your custodian's policies.
  5. Review weekly, so the data drives small changes rather than an annual report.
