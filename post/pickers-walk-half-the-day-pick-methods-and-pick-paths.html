Pickers Walk Half the Day: Pick Methods and Pick Paths for SME Warehouses

Orders are going out late. The 3 pm carrier pickup leaves with half a dozen orders still on the pick list, overtime has become normal, and the first answer is always the same: hire another picker. Before you do, follow one of your pickers for an hour. Most of that hour is not spent picking. It is spent walking.

Part 1 of this series won back space in the example distributor's pallet-rack zone and moved the fast movers to the front. This part follows the pickers through the same warehouse. It compares the four common ways of picking orders and the two simple routes through the aisles, and shows how many picker-hours each one takes for the same day's work.

Where a picker's day goes

A pick trip has four parts: getting the list and the cart, walking between locations, picking the case off the pallet, and sorting or consolidating the result for packing. The order-picking literature has long reported that travel is the largest of these, often about half of a picker's time or more, which is why most of the ways to pick faster are ways to walk less.

The example picks 899 cases a day from the rack zone: 300 orders of about 3 lines each. The times assumed throughout are:

AssumptionValue
Walking speed with a cart0.9 m/s
Pick one case off a pallet20 s
Put a line into the right order's tote (batch, wave)4 s
Set up a trip: list, labels, cart60 s
Set up a zone trip30 s
Bring an order's totes together at packing (zone, wave)15 s
Productive picking hours per picker a day7 h

With single-order picking in the as-is layout, each order costs about 192 m of walking. The day takes 27.8 picker-hours, and 64% of that is walking. That is 4 pickers' worth of work for 899 cases.

The four pick methods

MethodHow it worksSuitsWatch out for
Single-orderOne picker takes one order from start to finish, then comes back for the nextFew orders a day, large orders, urgent one-offsWalks the whole warehouse for every order. Picker-hours rise in step with orders
BatchOne trip picks several orders at once onto a cart of totes, one tote per order, and each line goes straight into its order's toteMany small orders: e-commerce, parts and service ordersA put-to-the-wrong-tote error. Needs a scan or a clear tote label at each put
ZoneEach picker owns one area and picks only the lines in it. An order's totes meet at packingLarger buildings, several storage types, several pickers a shiftUneven zones leave one picker swamped and another idle. Consolidation at packing
WaveOrders are released in timed waves set by the carrier pickups. Each zone picker sweeps their zone once per waveFixed carrier cut-offs, steady daily volumeNeeds a WMS or a disciplined release routine. A late wave misses its truck

The methods combine. Wave picking, as modelled here, is zone picking with orders released in 3 waves a day, timed to the pickups. Each zone picker carries up to 40 lines a trip. Batch and zone trips here hold 8 orders.

Routing: return or S-shape

The route matters as much as the method. Two simple rules cover most small warehouses, and both can be printed on a pick list as a sorted location sequence.

In the example the answer flips with the layout. In the as-is warehouse, fast movers are scattered along the aisles, so the S-shape route is a little shorter for single-order picking (186 m against 192 m an order). After Part 1's re-slot, the busy pallets sit in the front bays, and the return route wins clearly: 92 m against 136 m. Slotting and routing have to be decided together.

Three things stand out.

  • Slotting comes first. Part 1's re-slot alone, with no change of method, cuts single-order picking from 27.8 to 18.5 picker-hours a day.
  • Batching halves it again. Picking 8 orders a trip brings the walk down to about 26 m an order and the day to 9.1 picker-hours. Two pickers can now do what took 4.
  • Zone and wave win on walking, not always on hours. Wave picking walks only about 5 m an order, but it pays for consolidation at packing. At 300 orders it saves about 1.1 picker-hours a day over batch picking, and it needs the zones staffed and balanced.

Volume changes the choice. At 150 orders a day, batch picking takes 4.5 picker-hours and fits one picker. At 600 orders, single-order picking would need 36.5 hours, or 6 pickers. Batch takes 17.5 hours and wave 15.4, because each wave's trips get denser as the orders grow.

Common mistakes

  • Hiring before measuring. Time five trips. If walking is half the trip, the fix is the route and the method, not another person.
  • Unsorted pick lists. A list in order-entry sequence sends the picker back and forth. Sort it by location along the route.
  • Batching without a put check. Faster picking is lost if the wrong case lands in the wrong tote.
  • Zones by floor area. Size zones by picks a day, or one picker carries the fast movers for everyone.
  • Changing the method before the slotting. The best route and method depend on where the fast movers sit.

Key points

  • Walking is the largest part of a picker's day. In the example it is 64% of single-order picking as-is.
  • Re-slot first, then batch: together they take the example from 27.8 to 9.1 picker-hours a day.
  • Choose the route with the layout. Return suits fast movers at the front; S-shape suits picks spread along the aisles.
  • Zone and wave picking cut walking most and suit higher volumes, fixed cut-offs and several pickers.
  • Track lines per picker-hour and orders shipped by the cut-off.
