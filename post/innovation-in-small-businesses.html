Innovation in a Small Business: Six Practices, Cheap Tests and an Idea Log

Innovation in a small business is rarely a laboratory breakthrough. It is a new service built on skills the team already has, a product variant customers keep asking for, or a change to a process that removes a daily frustration. What separates firms that keep finding these from firms that don't is not talent. It is a routine: a clear problem, ideas from more than one head, cheap tests, and a decision on every idea.

This article sets out six practices that make that routine work, a two-hour idea session, a test card, a worked example with numbers, and a calculator that shows why many small tests beat one big bet. It was first published in November 2025 under a title that credited the practices to McKinsey & Company. We found no McKinsey source for the list, so the practices are described here in our own words, and the article has moved to a shorter address.

1. Start from the customer's problem

Most weak ideas are answers to a question nobody asked. Write the problem as one sentence about a real customer: "Contractors cannot get a firm delivery date when they order" leads to better ideas than "improve the customer experience". Design thinking, a widely used method, follows the same order: understand the people you serve, define the problem, generate ideas, build a rough prototype and test it with them.

Five short customer interviews are usually enough to sharpen a problem statement. Ask about the last time the problem happened, what it cost them and what they did about it. Avoid asking whether they would like your idea; people are polite.

2. Make curiosity part of leadership

Teams copy what leaders do. A manager who asks why, listens and changes course when the evidence says so gives everyone permission to do the same. A useful habit is to ask, in every plan review, "What would have to be true for this to work, and how could we check that cheaply?" The first principles thinking article covers this way of questioning in more depth.

3. Mix perspectives

People who do the same job tend to see the same options. Bring sales, operations, finance and someone who talks to customers daily into the same session. In a small firm, inviting a trusted customer or supplier can be the most productive change of all.

How the session runs matters as much as who attends. Research on brainstorming has found for decades that people generate more ideas working alone first than talking in a group, mainly because in a group only one person can speak at a time while the others wait and forget. So have people write ideas silently first, then share and build on them.

4. Run small, cheap experiments

Most ideas fail, so the aim is to fail cheaply and learn quickly. Before a test starts, write down what you expect, how you will measure it and the result that counts as a pass. Setting the pass mark first stops a disappointing result being explained away afterwards.

Decision byThe leadership review at the end of week eight

Why many small tests beat one big bet

If one idea in five passes its test, a business that runs 12 cheap tests a year can expect two or three successes. Each success might be worth far more than all the tests together cost. The calculator shows the arithmetic with your own figures.

5. Make ideas visible

A rough sketch, a storyboard of the customer's experience or a cardboard model draws better feedback than a written description. People find it easier to criticise a picture constructively, and the picture improves faster. A whiteboard and sticky notes are enough.

6. Work in short cycles

Agree a short goal, work on it for two to four weeks, review what was learned and decide the next step. Short cycles keep momentum and limit the cost of a wrong turn. They also fit naturally with the plan-do-check-act cycle many firms already use; see OODA vs PDCA for how the two compare.

Worked example: a new service from existing skills

A 15-person engineering firm hears the same request from several clients: help preparing documents for a regulatory audit. The tempting move is to launch a new service line with a web page, a brochure and an advertising budget. Instead, the team writes the test card in Table 3 and offers the service to five existing clients at a pilot price of $4,500, against an intended price of $7,500.

Table 4. The three pilots and what changed

PilotHoursWhat was learned
142A third of the time went on chasing the client for documents; a request list is written for the next pilot
238The request list helps; a standard checklist of 40 items replaces notes made from scratch
334Checklist and templates in place; the remaining time is the review itself

Three of the five clients accepted, so the test passed. The pilots earned $13,500 against $10,830 of staff time at $95 an hour, roughly breaking even while the method was worked out. With the checklist and templates, the team expects 30 hours per engagement, a cost of $2,850 against the $7,500 price, or $4,650 contribution each. Ten engagements in the first year would add $75,000 of revenue and $46,500 of contribution, and the firm launches with a proven method, a realistic price and two client testimonials. The figures are illustrative.

Protecting time and keeping an idea log

Innovation competes with daily work and usually loses. Protect a small, regular slot: an hour a month for the leadership team to review ideas and tests, and a quarterly idea session with a mixed group. Keep every idea in one log and decide on each within a month.

Do not judge the routine on a single big win. Most of the value comes from many small, tested improvements, and tests that fail cheaply are a sign the process is working.

Common traps

  • Ideas without a problem. A session with no clear question produces a list that goes nowhere.
  • The loudest voice wins. Write ideas silently first and vote silently.
  • Testing too late. Months spent polishing an idea before any customer sees it.
  • No pass mark. Without one, every result looks "promising" and nothing is ever stopped.
  • No follow-through. People stop suggesting ideas once they see nothing happens to them. Tell each person what was decided about theirs.

Changing how people work also needs care; the change management toolkit covers the main models.

Takeaway. Start from one customer problem, collect ideas silently before discussing them, and test the best with a pass mark set in advance. Keep tests cheap and decide on every idea within a month. In the example, three pilots costing almost nothing turned a client request into a priced, proven service.

The engineering firm, its pilots and the calculator's starting values are illustrative examples with round-number inputs. They do not describe any real company.
