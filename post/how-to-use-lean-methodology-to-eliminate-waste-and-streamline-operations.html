Lean Methodology: The Eight Wastes, a Waste Walk and a Value Stream Map

Lean is a way of improving work by removing everything that does not add value for the customer, so that work flows with less waiting, effort and cost. It started in car manufacturing and is now used in offices, hospitals, warehouses and service businesses. This guide was first published in November 2025 and has been rewritten with a waste walk example and a value stream map. The earlier version quoted percentages we could not trace to sources; they have been replaced with an illustrative worked example.

The five Lean principles

PrincipleWhat it means
ValueDefine value from the customer's point of view: what they would pay for
Value streamMap every step that delivers that value, from order to delivery
FlowMake the value-adding steps run without interruption
PullMake only what the next step or the customer needs, when they need it
PerfectionKeep improving; there is always more waste to remove

The eight wastes

WasteMeaningExamples
TransportMoving materials or information more than neededParts carried between buildings; files emailed back and forth
InventoryMore stock or work in progress than neededRacks of parts waiting; a backlog of half-finished quotes
MotionPeople moving more than the work requiresWalking to fetch tools; searching shared drives
WaitingPeople or work idleWaiting for approval, material or a machine
OverproductionMaking more or earlier than neededBatches built ahead of orders
Over-processingMore work than the customer needsTighter tolerances than specified; reports nobody reads
DefectsErrors and reworkScrap, rework, wrong invoices
Unused talentNot using people's knowledge and ideasOperators never asked how to improve their work

Value-added or not: a three-question test

Before you can remove waste you have to see it, and most of it hides inside ordinary-looking work. A step adds value only if all three of these are true:

  1. The customer would pay for it if they could see it being done.
  2. It changes the product or service in a way the customer cares about: shape, content, information or condition.
  3. It is done right the first time. Fixing an error is never value-adding, however necessary.

Steps that fail the test fall into two groups. Some are pure waste and can go: re-keying data, waiting for an approval nobody uses, searching for tools. Others are necessary but not value-adding, such as regulatory records, safety checks and invoicing; the aim there is to make them as quick and simple as possible, not to remove them.

Step in an order processValue-added?What to do
Cutting and welding the customer's partYesProtect and improve
Entering the order a second time in another systemNo: wasteRemove with an integration
Waiting two days for a manager's sign-offNo: wasteReplace with a rule; sign off exceptions only
Recording material heat numbers for traceabilityNo, but requiredKeep; capture by scanning, not typing
Reworking a weld that failed inspectionNo: wasteFind and fix the cause

When teams time a process this way, the value-adding steps are usually a small share of the total elapsed time; the rest is waiting and hand-offs. Our process optimization guide has a worked example.

A waste walk

A waste walk (often called a gemba walk) means going to where the work happens, watching one product or transaction from start to finish, and noting every instance of the eight wastes. It takes an hour or two and needs no software. The findings below come from an illustrative walk in a small fabrication shop.

What we sawWasteCountermeasure
Parts waiting at paint for a full batchWaiting, inventoryPaint smaller mixed batches twice a day
Welders walk to the crib for consumablesMotionPoint-of-use bins at each weld bay
Travellers re-written by hand at each stepOver-processing, defectsOne printed traveller with barcode
Finished units wait for a supervisor's sign-offWaitingOperator self-check with a checklist; spot audits

A product takes about 12 working days to get through the shop, but only about 5.2 hours of that is work. Cutting the waiting, by smaller batches, a pull signal from assembly to paint, and removing the sign-off queue, shortens lead time far more than speeding up any single step.

Lean tools in brief

  • 5S: organize the workplace so everything has a place; see 5S in service businesses.
  • Kaizen events: short, focused improvement workshops; see how to run a Kaizen event.
  • Standard work: the current best way of doing a task, written down and followed.
  • Pull and kanban: signals that trigger production or replenishment only when needed.
  • Quick changeover (SMED): shorter set-ups make small batches affordable.
  • Root cause analysis: 5 whys and fishbone diagrams to fix causes rather than symptoms.

Getting started

  1. Pick one value stream that matters to customers.
  2. Do a waste walk with the people who run it.
  3. Map it, measuring waiting as well as work.
  4. Fix the biggest wait first, often a batch, an approval or a hand-off.
  5. Measure lead time, quality and on-time delivery before and after, and set realistic targets from your own baseline.

Lean outside the factory

The same thinking works in offices and service businesses, where the "product" is a quote, an invoice, a claim or a patient visit. The wastes look different but are just as common.

Office processTypical wasteLean fix
QuotingWaiting for pricing approval; re-keying data from emailPricing rules with authority limits; a quote template linked to the CRM
InvoicingErrors from manual entry; invoices waiting for month-endInvoice from the shipping record; invoice daily
PurchasingSeveral approvals for small items; chasing suppliersApproval limits by value; a reorder point for common items
Onboarding staffEquipment and access not ready on day oneA standard checklist started when the offer is accepted

Common reasons Lean efforts stall

  • Tools without purpose: 5S or kanban introduced because they are "Lean", not to solve a known problem.
  • No time for improvement: people are expected to improve on top of a full workload.
  • Using Lean to cut jobs: once people fear that ideas cost them their jobs, the ideas stop.
  • Not sustaining: improvements slip back without standard work and regular checks.

Leaders keep Lean alive by visiting the work regularly, asking questions rather than giving answers, and protecting time for improvement.
