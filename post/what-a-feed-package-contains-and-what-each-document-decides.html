What a FEED Package Contains, and What Each Document Decides

Quak Foo LeeNov 11, 20265 min read

FEED, or front-end engineering design, turns a selected concept into a defined plant. It ends with a package of documents that an owner can approve, a lender can review and a contractor can bid on.

Post 1 placed FEED as the third of five project stages. Post 2 covered the design basis that FEED starts from. This post goes through what the package contains, using the same $100M specialty chemical plant.

What FEED has to produce

A FEED package answers three questions.

  • What are we building? A scope defined well enough that two engineers would design the same plant from it.
  • What will it cost? A Class 3 estimate, typically accurate to between −20% and +30%.
  • How will we build it? A schedule, a contracting plan and a list of risks.

The documents are produced in a set order, because each one is calculated from the one before.

The documents and what each one decides

Table 1. Main FEED deliverables

ProcessEquipment list and datasheetsSize, duty and material of each itemVendors cannot quote, so costs are factored
ProcessUtility summaryPeak and normal demand for power, steam, cooling and gasesUndersized utilities found at start-up
SafetyHazard review (HAZID or HAZOP)Safeguards the design needsSafety changes made during construction
SafetyRelief and hazardous area studiesRelief device loads, electrical classificationWrong equipment ratings, permit delays
LayoutPlot plan and equipment layoutWhere everything sits and how it is reachedPiping and civil quantities unknown

A package for a small plant may combine some of these. None of the questions in the third column can be left unanswered.

PFD and P&ID: the two drawings to know

Owners are asked to review both drawings, and they serve different purposes.

Table 2. PFD and P&ID compared

Process flow diagram (PFD)Piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID)
PurposeShows how the process worksShows how the plant is built and controlled
ShowsMain equipment, main streams, key operating conditionsEvery line, valve, instrument, control loop and relief device
Typical count10 to 20 sheets80 to 150 sheets
Used forHeat and material balance, equipment sizingHazard review, cost estimate, construction, operation
Owner should checkCapacity, feeds and products match the design basisOperators can start, stop, isolate and maintain each item

The sheet counts are for a plant of the example's size. The P&IDs are the most important drawings in the package, because the hazard review, the estimate and the construction scope are all taken from them.

How the Class 3 estimate is built

A Class 3 estimate is built up from the documents and is no longer factored from a single number. Each line has a source in the package.

Table 3. Estimate build-up and the source of each line

Cost elementAmountShareWhere the number comes from
Equipment$30,000,00030%Vendor budget quotations against datasheets
Bulk materials$15,000,00015%Quantities taken from P&IDs and layout
Construction labour$22,500,00022.5%Hours estimated from quantities and local rates
Engineering and project management$12,500,00012.5%Planned hours by discipline
Owner's and other indirect costs$10,000,00010%Permits, spares, commissioning, owner's team
Contingency$10,000,00010%Set from the risk register
Total installed cost$100,000,000100%

Equipment is under a third of the total. The rest is the cost of installing it, connecting it and managing the work. An estimate that prices the equipment well and factors everything else has not reached Class 3. The methods are covered in Engineering CAPEX and OPEX Estimation for Process Plants.

What FEED does not include

FEED stops short of construction drawings. Piping isometrics, structural steel details, cable schedules and vendor fabrication drawings belong to detailed engineering, which follows approval. That work typically costs three to five times as much as FEED, so it should start only after the investment decision.

Eight questions to ask when you receive a FEED package

  1. Is the design basis signed, and are any holds still open?
  2. Does the heat and material balance close, and does it match the capacity in the design basis?
  3. Have the P&IDs been through a hazard review, and are the actions closed or listed?
  4. How much of the equipment cost is backed by vendor quotations?
  5. Does the estimate state its class, its accuracy range and its exclusions?
  6. Is contingency tied to named risks?
  7. Which items have the longest delivery, and when must they be ordered?
  8. What is outside the battery limits, and who pays for it?

A package that answers all eight is ready for an investment decision. A gap in any of them is a reason to extend FEED before committing the full budget.

Takeaway. A FEED package defines the scope, the cost and the plan. Judge it by whether each number in the estimate can be traced to a document in the package.
