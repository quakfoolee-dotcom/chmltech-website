Costs, interest rates and trade rules are all moving at once, and none of them is under a small firm's control. What is under its control is how much a shock hurts: how close the business runs to break-even, how quickly cash comes back, how much it wastes, and how exposed it is to a single supplier, customer or border.

This article looks at the pressures on Canadian businesses as of November 2026, shows how to stress test your own profit and loss, and works through an example of a small manufacturer hit by falling sales and rising costs at the same time. A calculator lets you run the test with your own figures. It replaces a post first published in April 2025, which described the outlook at that time, and has moved to a shorter address.

Efficiency and resilience are not the same thing

Efficiency is doing the work with less waste. Resilience is surviving a shock. The two usually support each other: less scrap, faster invoicing and fewer errors improve margins in good times and lower break-even in bad times. They pull apart in a few places, and those need a deliberate choice.

Stress test your profit and loss

A stress test asks one question: what happens to profit if sales fall and costs rise at the same time? It needs only three numbers from your accounts.

  • Sales for the last twelve months.
  • Variable costs, the costs that rise and fall with sales: materials, direct labour paid by the hour, energy, freight, commissions. Express them as a share of sales.
  • Fixed costs, the costs that stay the same: rent, salaries, insurance, software, interest.

Profit is sales × (1 − variable share) − fixed costs. Break-even sales is fixed costs ÷ (1 − variable share). The margin of safety is how far sales can fall before you reach break-even, as a share of today's sales.

Worked example: a metal fabricator

A 25-person metal fabricator in British Columbia has sales of $6.00M a year. Variable costs are 55% of sales: materials 35% (of which 60% is steel), direct labour 15% and energy and freight 5%. Fixed costs, including interest on a $1.5 million credit line, are $2.20M. Profit is $500k, and break-even is $4.89M: sales can fall 19% before the business loses money.

The owner tests a plausible bad year: sales down 10%, steel up 15%, energy and freight up 20%, and one percentage point more on the credit line ($15,000 a year). Steel and energy together add 4.15% of sales to variable cost, a 7.5% increase.

Table 3. The stress test

Today$6.00M55.0%$2.20M$500k$4.89M19%

After the three moves$5.40M55.5%$2.13M$265k$4.80M11%

A 10% fall in sales alone more than halves profit, because the fixed costs do not fall with it. Add the cost increases and the business slips just below break-even. Nothing in the stressed case is dramatic on its own; it is the combination that hurts.

The owner then tests three moves, chosen because none needs new equipment or lay-offs:

  1. A steel surcharge. New quotes carry a price adjustment clause tied to a published steel price index, and existing customers are told the surcharge applies from the next order. The owner assumes two-thirds of the steel increase is recovered, about 2.1% of sales.
  2. Less waste. Scrap, rework and overtime from rushed jobs come to about 4% of sales. Nesting software, a first-off check and a frozen weekly schedule cut that by 1.5% of sales. Lean methodology and OEE help find these losses.
  3. A fixed cost review. Unused software subscriptions, an under-used leased forklift and an insurance renewal tendered to three brokers save $80,000 a year.

Together they bring profit back to $265k in the stressed year and lower break-even to $4.80M. Figure 2 shows how each line moves.

Cash deserves its own mention. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash when customers pay late and credit costs more. Every day cut from the time between paying suppliers and being paid releases cash that no longer has to be borrowed; the quote-to-cash process shows where those days usually sit.

Common mistakes

  • Cutting across the board. A 10% cut to every budget removes capability along with waste. Cut what the stress test and the waste measures point to.
  • Cutting maintenance and training first. Both are easy to defer and expensive later, when breakdowns and errors rise.
  • Hoarding stock everywhere. Buffers on every item tie up cash that may be needed more. Protect the critical few.
  • Waiting for certainty. Rates, tariffs and prices will keep moving. A business that knows its break-even can act on each change as it comes.

For how to keep plans flexible as conditions change, see operational agility.

Takeaway. Know your break-even and margin of safety, then test them against a bad year. In the example, a 10% fall in sales with higher steel, energy and interest costs turned a $500k profit into a small loss; a surcharge, less waste and a fixed cost review restored about $265k without new equipment or lay-offs.

The fabricator and its figures are an illustrative example with round-number inputs. It does not describe any real company. The economic figures in Table 1 are as published in October and November 2026 and will change.
