U-Shape, Straight-Through or L-Shape? Choosing a Warehouse Flow Pattern

Part 1 showed that the same building and racks can need half the picker-hours with a better layout. Its redesign put the receiving and shipping doors on the same wall: a U-shape. That is one of three common flow patterns. The other two put receiving and shipping on opposite walls (straight-through, also called I-flow) or on two walls that meet at a corner (L-shape).

Which one is best is usually argued from rules of thumb. This part runs the same Vancouver distributor, racks, orders and trucks through all three patterns, side by side, and measures what changes.

The three patterns

Table 1. Three ways to arrange the flow (the last two columns are common rules of thumb)

PatternWhere the docks areHow goods moveUsually said to suitWatch out for
U-shapeReceiving and shipping on the same wall, side by sideIn, back into storage, and out again near where they came inMost SME warehouses; buildings with one good yard sideInbound and outbound trucks and staging close together
Straight-through (I-flow)Receiving on one wall, shipping on the opposite wallOne way across the buildingHigh volumes, cross-docking, separate inbound and outbound sites or security zonesTwo yards; fast movers can sit near shipping or near receiving, not both
L-shapeReceiving and shipping on two walls that meet at a cornerIn on one side, a turn, out on the nextBuildings or sites where only two adjacent sides have yard roomTwo yards whose traffic meets at the corner

The same building, three ways

All three keep the building (72 by 52 m, about 40,300 sq ft), the same racks (840 pallet positions a level), three receiving and two shipping doors, the same crew and the same receiving-heavy day as Part 1: 300 orders and 14 inbound trucks. Each one follows the good practice from Part 1: aisles open at both ends, busiest SKUs nearest packing, packing next to shipping, separate staging lanes.

  • U-shape: the Part 1 redesign. 14 aisles of 30 m across the building, receiving doors at the west end of the south wall and shipping doors at the east end.
  • Straight-through: receiving on the west wall, shipping and packing on the east wall, 10 aisles of 42 m running the same way as the flow (the same number of pallet positions, arranged along the building).
  • L-shape: the U-shape's racks, with receiving on the south wall toward the south-east corner, shipping on the east wall, and packing in the corner between them.

  • Picking hardly changes. In every pattern the busiest SKUs sit in short aisle ends next to packing, so pickers walk 69 to 72 m an order. Across ten other simulated days the three patterns were within 0.5% to 2.8% of each other in picker-hours. The L-shape needed the fewest on 7 days and the U-shape on 3; straight-through never did, though it was never more than 0.35 hours a day behind the U-shape.
  • Put-away is where the patterns differ, and it is about the receiving doors. The forklift carries most pallets to the fast movers, which sit next to packing. In the L-shape the receiving doors are close to that corner, so a put-away takes 1.91 minutes. In the U-shape they are at the far end of the wall (2.15 minutes), and in straight-through they are across the building (2.24 minutes). Dock-to-stock follows: 14, 33 and 38 minutes on average.
  • It is the door position, not the letter. To check, the same U-shape was run with its receiving doors moved toward the middle of the wall, next to packing. Its put-away fell to 1.95 minutes and dock-to-stock to 15 minutes, the same as the L-shape. The flow pattern decides which walls the doors can go on; what pays is putting receiving close to where the fast movers live.
  • Straight-through separates the docks, not the traffic. Receiving and packing are on opposite sides, but the forklift still carries fast-mover pallets to the east end, where the pickers work: 51 give-way stops against 41 in the U-shape and 33 in the L-shape, all small amounts of time.

Volume does not change the picture. At 150 orders a day the three need 9.0, 9.1, 8.9 picker-hours; at 600, 35.0, 35.3, 34.8.

What the shift does not show

A one-shift simulation of picking and put-away is only part of the decision. These usually weigh as much, and often decide it:

  • The site. A U-shape needs one yard; straight-through and L-shape need two. Many sites only have room for trucks on one side.
  • Dock capacity. One wall limits the number of doors. Very high truck counts, or a large share of cross-docking (see the dock door bottleneck), are where straight-through earns its reputation for high volume.
  • Separation. Food, pharmaceuticals, customs-bonded goods or very different inbound and outbound carriers can call for receiving and shipping on separate sides.
  • Growth. A U-shape can extend away from its dock wall without moving a door. Straight-through can extend sideways along both dock walls. An L-shape keeps two walls free.
  • Yard and dock staff. Two yards can mean two gates, two sets of dock staff, or people walking across the building.

If this is trueStart with
Only one side of the site has room for trucksU-shape
You pick by the case or piece and a few fast movers drive most picks and put-awaysAny pattern, with receiving doors placed close to where the fast movers sit
Inbound and outbound truck counts need more doors than one wall holdsStraight-through
A large share of goods is cross-docked, or inbound and outbound must be kept apartStraight-through
Yard room is only on two adjacent sidesL-shape, with receiving toward the corner where packing and the fast movers are

How to check your own building

  1. Mark where trucks can go. Draw the site with every side that has, or could have, yard room.
  2. Count doors by the hour. Take your busiest day's truck arrivals and departures and see whether one wall's doors can cover them.
  3. Find the fast movers' path. Rank SKUs by picks a day (ABC analysis) and ask whether they can sit close to both receiving and packing.
  4. Measure the put-away trip. Time a few pallets from the receiving door to where your fast movers sit. If it is the longest trip in the building, the receiving doors are in the wrong place, whatever the pattern.
  5. Test before you move racks. A plan to scale and ten traced orders, as in Part 1, show most of the difference.

What the model leaves out

  • One shift with one fixed seed per day, single-order case picking, one forklift (two from 400 orders a day).
  • No cross-docking, no replenishment of pick faces, no returns.
  • The same building for all three. A real choice usually comes with a different building shape or site.
  • The yards are drawn to fit the plan; real truck aprons are deeper.
  • Each pattern is one reasonable layout of it. A different rack block or door position would give different numbers, as the moved-door U-shape shows.

Key points

  • Laid out well, the three patterns pick at almost the same cost in this building: within 2.8% of each other on every simulated day.
  • Put-away and dock-to-stock depend on how close the receiving doors are to the fast movers. Here the L-shape, with receiving near its corner, and a U-shape with receiving moved to the middle both cut put-away time by about 10% and dock-to-stock by more than half.
  • Straight-through separates the docks but not the people and forklifts inside.
  • Site, dock capacity, separation and growth usually decide the pattern before productivity does.
