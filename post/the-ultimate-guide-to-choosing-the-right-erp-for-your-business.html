Choosing and Implementing an ERP for a Small or Mid-Sized Business: A Practical Guide

An enterprise resource planning (ERP) system brings finance, sales, purchasing, inventory and often production into one database. For a growing small or mid-sized business it replaces a tangle of accounting software, spreadsheets and re-keyed orders. It is also one of the largest and riskiest projects such a business takes on. This guide combines two earlier Chmltech articles, on choosing an ERP and on implementation pitfalls, into one practical method.

Signs you need an ERP

  • The same order or item is typed into two or more systems.
  • Nobody can say with confidence what is in stock or what an order really earned.
  • Month-end closing takes more than a week because data must be collected from several places.
  • Growth is limited by administration rather than demand.
  • Your accounting package cannot handle inventory, multiple locations, production or currencies well.

If only one of these applies, a better accounting package or a single add-on may be enough. An ERP pays off when several apply at once.

Step 1: Start with your processes, not the software

Map how work actually flows today: quote to cash, procure to pay, plan to produce, and record to report. Mark where data is re-keyed, where people wait, and where errors start. From the maps, write requirements in your own words, such as "allocate stock to orders by promised date" rather than "advanced inventory". Rank them as must-have, important and nice-to-have. This step usually takes a few weeks and is the best protection against the most common cause of overruns: discovering missing functions after the contract is signed.

Step 2: Shortlist three and run scripted demos

Use your requirements to shortlist three systems and, just as importantly, three implementation partners. Then send each the same demo script built from your own processes and sample data, so that you compare like with like instead of watching each vendor's best features. Score each demo on the same criteria straight after it ends.

In this illustrative example, Option A wins narrowly on a weighted score of 3.70 even though Option B is cheaper and Option C fits the processes best. The weights make the trade-off explicit, so the decision does not rest on the most persuasive presenter. Always call two reference customers of a similar size and industry for each finalist, and ask what went wrong.

Step 3: Count the five-year cost, not the licence

Licence prices are the most visible cost and rarely the largest. The worked example below is for a 25-user distributor on a cloud ERP with per-user pricing. The numbers are illustrative; use quotes for your own case.

ItemCost
Implementation partner (configuration, setup, project management)$45,000
Data migration and cleansing$8,000
Integrations (e-commerce, shipping, banking)$10,000
Training$6,000
Internal staff time: 600 hours at $55$33,000

Licences: 15 full users at $110/month and 10 light users at $8/month$20,760 a year

Five-year total cost$235,800

Licences make up only 44% of the five-year cost here. Implementation services, data migration and the time of your own staff make up the rest, and staff time is the item most often left out. A useful check is that implementation services commonly cost one to two times the annual licence fee for a small project. The Productivity Mega Deduction confirmed in Budget 2026 lets most businesses write off depreciable software and hardware in the year they are put in use, and the BDC LIFT program can finance technology projects.

Step 4: Implement in phases, with an owner

Name an internal project lead with the authority to make decisions and protected time to do the work. Hold a fit-gap review where the partner shows how the standard system handles each requirement; change your process where the standard way is good enough and keep customizations to the few that give you a real advantage. Test with real scenarios from your own business, not the partner's examples, and train people on those same scenarios. Most failures are not about the software. They come from vague scope, customization, poor data and too little attention to the people who will use the system every day.

Common pitfalls and how to avoid them

PitfallWhat happensHow to avoid it
Choosing before mapping processesRequirements are vague, demos impress, gaps appear after signingMap the main processes first and turn them into demo scripts
Too much customizationUpgrades break and costs rise every yearChange the process before changing the software; keep a short list of must-have customizations
Dirty dataWrong stock, prices and balances on day oneClean and de-duplicate customers, items and open balances before migration
No internal project leadDecisions stall and the partner fills the gap at your expenseName a project lead with authority and protected time
Too little training and change managementPeople keep spreadsheets alongside the new systemTrain on your own scenarios; appoint super-users in each team
Underestimated scopeExtra modules and integrations surface lateInclude integrations, reports and data migration in the quote; hold a contingency
Big-bang go-live with no fallbackShipping or invoicing stopsRehearse the cut-over, go live at a quiet time, keep the old system read-only

After go-live

Plan for a stabilization period of four to eight weeks with extra support, then review the project after 90 days against the problems that started it: order errors, stock accuracy, days to close the month, and time spent on administration. Most of the benefits come in the year after go-live, as people stop using workarounds and start using the reports.
