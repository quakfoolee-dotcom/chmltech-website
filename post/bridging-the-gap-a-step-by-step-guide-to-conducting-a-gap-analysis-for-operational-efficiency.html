Gap Analysis: A Step-by-Step Guide with a Worked Example

Quak Foo LeeApr 8, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

A gap analysis compares where a process, team or business is today with where it needs to be, and lists the differences with their causes and a plan to close them. It is one of the most practical tools in improvement work: simple enough to do in a few workshops, structured enough to turn opinions into a prioritized plan. This guide, first written in 2023, has been rewritten with a figure, a worked example table and the common uses in small and mid-sized businesses.

Where gap analysis is used

  • Process improvement: current versus target performance for a process such as quoting, order entry or month-end close.
  • Digital adoption: the systems and data you have versus what you need, often the core of a digital adoption plan.
  • Standards and certification: your quality system against ISO 9001, or your security controls against a framework.
  • Skills: the capabilities the team has versus those the plan requires.
  • Strategy: the capabilities and market position needed to reach a goal versus today's.

Six steps

1. Scope

Be specific: "order handling from receipt to shipment" or "our quality system against ISO 9001:2026", not "operations". A narrow scope gives a usable result in weeks.

2. Define the future state

Describe what good looks like in measurable terms: quote turnaround of two days, inventory accuracy of 95%, all orders entered once. Where possible, base targets on customer needs, a standard or a credible benchmark rather than aspiration.

3. Map the current state

Walk the process with the people who do it, measure it and collect evidence. Resist the temptation to describe the current state from memory in a meeting room.

4. List the gaps and their causes

For each difference, ask why it exists. A gap described only as a symptom ("quotes are slow") leads to the wrong fix; one described with its cause ("we wait for supplier prices on every quote") points to the right one.

5. Prioritize

Score each gap on the value of closing it and the effort and risk involved. Close high-value, low-effort gaps first; plan the large ones as projects.

6. Plan, act and review

Give each action an owner, a date and a measure, and review progress monthly. Repeat the gap analysis after six to twelve months to see what moved.

Gap analysis and digital adoption

A digital adoption plan is essentially a gap analysis for systems and data: which processes are manual, which systems do not connect, where data is re-keyed, and what technology and changes would close those gaps. The same discipline applies: define the future state in business terms first (faster quotes, fewer errors, better visibility), and only then choose the software. Starting from a product rather than a gap is the most common reason digital projects disappoint.

Tips for a useful result

  • Involve people from each step of the process; gaps often sit in the hand-offs between teams.
  • Use evidence: timings, error counts, samples of documents, not impressions.
  • Keep the output to a few pages; a 60-page report rarely gets acted on.
  • Separate quick fixes from projects so momentum starts while bigger changes are planned.
  • Re-run it: a gap analysis is a snapshot, and its value comes from repeating it.
