AI and Jobs in Canada: What the Data Shows and What Employers Should Do

Quak Foo LeeApr 23, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

Will AI take jobs or create them? The honest answer in 2026 is both, unevenly, and more slowly in some places than the headlines suggest. This article, first written in 2023 as a list of AI opportunities, has been rewritten around current Canadian and global data and what it means for small and mid-sized employers and their staff.

The global picture

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025, based on a survey of more than 1,000 large employers, projects that between 2025 and 2030 about 170 million jobs will be created and 92 million displaced worldwide, a net gain of 78 million, with technology, the green transition, demographics and economic pressures all contributing. It also projects that 59 out of every 100 workers will need some reskilling or upskilling by 2030, and that 11 of them are unlikely to receive it. The fastest-growing roles include big data, fintech and AI specialists; the fastest-declining include postal, banking and data-entry clerks.

Which Canadian jobs are exposed

Statistics Canada has estimated how exposed Canadian jobs are to AI, separating jobs where AI is likely to complement workers from those where it may replace tasks. Using 2021 census data, it found 29% of workers in jobs highly exposed to and highly complementary with AI, 31% in highly exposed but less complementary jobs, and 40% in jobs not highly exposed. Highly educated workers were more likely to be exposed, but also more likely to be in jobs AI could help. Statistics Canada stresses that high exposure does not necessarily mean a higher risk of job loss.

How Canadians actually use AI at work

Statistics Canada's first survey of AI use by workers, from March 2026, found that 53.8% of workers in high-exposure, high-complementarity occupations used generative AI tools at work, compared with 45.9% in high-exposure, low-complementarity occupations and 14.2% in low-exposure occupations. Use was lowest in accommodation and food services, agriculture, and transportation and warehousing. Most users applied the tools to some tasks rather than most of their work.

What this means for a small or mid-sized employer

  • Tasks change before jobs do. Expect drafting, summarizing, searching, data entry and first-level analysis to be done with AI help, while judgement, relationships and hands-on work stay with people.
  • The gap is skills, not headcount. Staff who learn to use AI tools well will be noticeably more productive than those who do not, in the same role.
  • Clerical roles change most. Plan with the people in them: many can move to customer, quality, planning or analysis work that the business struggled to staff.
  • New roles appear in small forms. Someone who owns your AI tools, data quality and automation, often part time, is the small-business version of the "AI specialist" in the global reports.

A practical plan

  1. List the tasks, not the jobs, in each role, and mark which AI could draft, speed up or check.
  2. Set a simple AI use policy: approved tools, what data may go into them, and when a person must review output.
  3. Train everyone in the basics, using your own documents and tasks, then go deeper with those who use it most.
  4. Redesign one process at a time with the people who do it, and measure time and quality before and after.
  5. Use the time saved deliberately: on customers, quality and growth, and say so to staff, so AI is seen as help rather than a threat.

How AI is changing work in four sectors

Professional servicesResearch, first drafts, document review, meeting notesClient judgement, accountability, relationships

Questions staff ask, and honest answers

  • "Will AI replace my job?" In most small businesses, AI changes parts of jobs. Where a role is mainly routine data handling, it will change most, and the business should plan that change with the person.
  • "Can I use AI tools for my work?" Yes, within the company's policy on approved tools and data.
  • "Who is responsible if the AI is wrong?" The person and the business that use it, which is why output is checked before it reaches customers.
  • "Will I get training?" It should be part of the plan; employers that train staff get far more value from the tools.

For individuals

The safest career strategy is to become the person in your role who uses AI well: knows what to ask, checks the output, and spends the saved time on the parts of the job that need judgement and relationships. Skills that the WEF report highlights as growing, such as analytical thinking, resilience, technology literacy and leadership, are exactly the ones that make AI use effective.
