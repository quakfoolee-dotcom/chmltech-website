About the books

The Art of the Deal

Donald J. Trump with Tony Schwartz

Random House, 1987

Publisher's page

The Art of War

Sun Tzu (translated by John Minford)

Penguin Classics

Publisher's page

This review compares two very different books that business readers often set side by side: The Art of the Deal (1987), a memoir of New York real estate deal-making by Donald J. Trump with journalist Tony Schwartz, and The Art of War, the ancient Chinese text on strategy attributed to Sun Tzu. One is about asserting yourself to win; the other about winning without having to fight. This article was first published in April 2025 and has been rewritten with side-by-side tables, a figure and worked examples. It looks at both books as business advice only, not at the authors' later careers.

The Art of the Deal in brief

The book opens with a diary of a busy week of calls and meetings, then tells the stories behind the author's best-known projects. Its practical core is a short list of principles he calls the elements of the deal:

PrincipleWhat it meansSmall-business version
Think bigSet ambitious goals; big ideas attract attention and partnersBid for the larger contract you can actually deliver
Protect the downsidePlan for the worst case; the upside takes care of itselfFixed-price quotes only with a contingency and clear scope
Maximize your optionsNever rely on one deal or one planKeep a second customer or supplier in play
Know your marketTrust first-hand observation over consultants' reportsTalk to customers yourself before launching
Use your leverageKnow what the other side wants or needs from youKnow what you offer that competitors cannot
Enhance your locationImprove what you have to raise its valueUpgrade the product or service rather than discounting
Get the word outPublicity has value; be visibleCase studies, reviews and local press
Fight backRespond firmly when treated unfairlyEnforce contract terms and payment dates
Deliver the goodsPromotion fails if the product does not live up to itUnder-promise and over-deliver
Contain the costsSpend what is needed, no moreReview every recurring cost once a year
Have funEnjoy the game; energy mattersChoose work you will keep doing well

The Art of War in brief

Sun Tzu's thirteen chapters cover planning, the cost of war, attack, position, energy, manoeuvre, terrain and the use of spies. The text starts with five factors to assess before any campaign: the moral cause that unites people with their leader, the season and conditions, the terrain, the quality of the commander, and organisation and discipline. Its most useful principles for business are these:

PrincipleWhat it meansSmall-business version
Win without fightingThe best result is achieved without a costly battleWin the customer through fit and service, not a price war
Know yourself and the other sideVictory comes from knowing both your strengths and theirsHonest strengths-and-weaknesses review against two rivals
Prepare before you actBattles are won in the planning, before they are foughtPrepare figures and options before every negotiation
Avoid strength, strike weaknessDo not attack where the other side is strongestServe the niche a large competitor neglects
Adapt like waterChange approach as conditions changeReview plans monthly, not yearly
Speed mattersLong campaigns drain resourcesShort pilots rather than open-ended projects
Use informationGood intelligence prevents expensive mistakesTrack market prices, tenders and customer feedback

DeliverDeliver the goodsDiscipline and method decide outcomes

When each book's advice fits

The Art of the Deal's style works best when you hold a strong hand in a one-off transaction. The Art of War is more useful when you are the smaller party, or when the relationship will continue after the deal, because it values avoiding fights that leave both sides weaker.

Lessons for small businesses

  1. Preparation wins. Both books put most of the work before the meeting: knowing your numbers, the other side's needs and your alternatives.
  2. Protect the downside first. Avoid commitments that could sink the business if things go wrong; take the bigger risks only once the worst case is survivable.
  3. Your alternatives are your leverage. A second customer, supplier or plan is worth more in a negotiation than any tactic.
  4. Do not fight where the other side is strongest. A small firm rarely wins a price war with a large one; it wins on service, speed, specialism or location.
  5. Be visible, but deliver. Promotion brings opportunities; only delivery keeps them. Exaggerated claims can also breach Canada's Competition Act rules on false or misleading representations.
  6. Keep conflict for when it is needed. Stand firm on contract terms and payment, but treat most customers, suppliers and staff as long-term partners, not opponents.

A negotiation preparation sheet using both books

QuestionWhat to write down
What do we want?Ideal result, acceptable result, walk-away point
What are our alternatives?Other customers, suppliers or plans if this deal fails (maximize your options)
What does the other side need?Their pressures, deadlines and alternatives (know the other side; leverage)
Where are we strong, where weak?Honest view of both sides (know yourself)
What could go wrong?Worst case and how we limit it (protect the downside)
Where and when?Timing and setting that favour us (choose the terrain)
What will we promise?Only what we can deliver (deliver the goods)

Worked example: a large customer asks for a price cut

A machine shop earns 30% of its revenue from one distributor, which asks for a 10% price reduction at contract renewal. The owner's first instinct is to refuse outright or to give in to keep the volume. Working through the preparation sheet:

  • Alternatives: two other prospects could take some of the capacity within six months; that is the walk-away option, but not an immediate one.
  • The other side's needs: the distributor values short lead times and has had quality problems with a cheaper competitor.
  • Position: the shop is strong on speed and quality, weak on price for long production runs.
  • The offer: no cut on standard parts, a 5% reduction on high-volume parts in exchange for a two-year agreement and quarterly forecasts that let the shop schedule more efficiently.
  • Downside: the owner starts actively winning the two prospects so that one customer never again holds 30% of revenue.

The result uses the Art of the Deal's leverage and options and the Art of War's advice to avoid the head-on fight. The figures are illustrative.

Worked example from daily life: renewing a rental lease

A tenant's landlord asks them to agree to a rent increase above the provincial limit. Before replying, the tenant checks the rules (in British Columbia, rent can rise only once every 12 months, with three months' written notice, and within the yearly limit set by the Residential Tenancy Branch, 2.3% for 2026; a tenant does not have to agree to more), looks at comparable listings nearby, and notes their own strengths: always paid on time, looked after the unit, would save the landlord a vacancy and the cost of finding a new tenant. They reply politely, decline the above-limit increase, and offer to stay on for the long term, which saves the landlord a vacancy, in return for no more than the allowed increase. There is no confrontation, the tenant knows the terrain, and the landlord gets something they value. The approach is illustrative; check your own province's tenancy rules.

Criticisms of each

  • The Art of the Deal reflects one industry in one decade, and its stories are told from the author's side only. Its comfort with exaggeration and hard-edged tactics does not suit businesses that depend on repeat customers and trust.
  • The Art of War is written for war. Taken literally, its emphasis on deception and treating others as enemies is the wrong model for customers, staff and most suppliers. Its value lies in the principles of preparation, position and economy of effort, not the military framing.

Verdict

Read together, the books cover two halves of strategy. The Art of the Deal is about ambition, leverage and making things happen; The Art of War is about judgment, position and knowing when not to act. For most small businesses, the Art of War's patience and preparation make the better default, with the Art of the Deal's options and leverage applied in the moments that need them, and with both books' shared lesson in mind: protect the downside and deliver what you promise.
