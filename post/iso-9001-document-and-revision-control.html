Document and Revision Control Under ISO 9001: Keeping the Right Version in Use

Quak Foo LeeNov 9, 20268 min read

A machinist builds a batch of parts to revision C of a drawing. The customer changed the design two weeks ago, and revision D is sitting in the engineer's inbox. The parts are scrapped, the delivery is late and nobody did anything unusual. That is what a failure of document control looks like: ordinary people, doing their jobs carefully, from the wrong information.

Document control has a reputation as the dullest part of ISO 9001, a matter of headers, signatures and revision letters. Its purpose is simple and important: everyone who does the work uses the right, current information, and you can show what was done afterwards. This article explains what the standard requires, how to set up revision control that people follow, and how to keep it light enough for a small business.

Documents and records: two different jobs

ISO 9001 uses one term, documented information, for both, but it asks for two different kinds of control. The 2015 edition expressed the difference with two verbs, and the summaries of the 2026 edition report no substantive change to clause 7.

Documents ("maintain")Records ("retain")
What they areInstructions for what to do: procedures, work instructions, drawings, specifications, forms, the quality policyEvidence of what was done: inspection results, calibration certificates, training records, audit reports, signed-off forms
Do they change?Yes. They are revised as the work changes.No. A record is a fact about the past and must be protected from alteration.
Main riskSomeone uses an out-of-date versionA record is lost, unreadable, altered or discarded too soon
Main controlApproval, revision control, withdrawal of obsolete versionsStorage, protection, retention period, disposal

A blank inspection form is a document. The same form filled in and signed is a record. Keeping that distinction clear removes most of the confusion about what needs a revision number.

What clause 7.5 requires

The requirements fit on one page. The summary below follows the 2015 wording; check the exact text in your copy of ISO 9001:2026.

RequirementIn practice
Keep the documented information the standard requires, and whatever else you decide is needed for the system to work (7.5.1)You choose what to write down, based on your size, the complexity of your processes and the competence of your people
Identify and describe it (7.5.2)Title, reference number, revision, date, owner
Use a suitable format and medium (7.5.2)Language people read, a file type they can open, paper or electronic as suits the workplace
Review and approve it for suitability and adequacy (7.5.2)Someone competent checks it before release, and the approval is visible
Make it available and suitable for use where and when needed (7.5.3)The current version is at the point of use, not only in the office
Protect it (7.5.3)Against loss, improper use, loss of confidentiality and loss of integrity
Control distribution, access, retrieval and use (7.5.3)Who can see it, who can change it, and how people find it
Control storage and preservation, including legibility (7.5.3)Files that still open, paper that is still readable
Control changes, for example by version control (7.5.3)Revision identifiers, a record of what changed, withdrawal of old versions
Control retention and disposition (7.5.3)How long records are kept, and how they are disposed of
Identify and control documents of external origin (7.5.3)Customer drawings, standards, regulations, supplier data sheets
Protect records from unintended alteration (7.5.3)Read-only storage, signed PDFs, audit trails

Notice what is absent: there is no requirement for a document control procedure, a master list in a particular format, wet-ink signatures or a numbering scheme. You design the method. The auditor checks that it works.

The life of a controlled document

Every controlled document has an owner, usually the manager of the process it describes. The owner decides when it needs changing, makes sure it is reviewed by people who know the work, and arranges approval. Anyone may propose a change; only the owner and the approver may make one.

A revision scheme people understand

Choose one scheme and use it everywhere. Two are common.

SchemeExampleSuits
Letters for released revisionsRev A, B, C … with drafts shown as A1, A2 before releaseDrawings and specifications, especially where customers use the same convention
NumbersRev 1, 2, 3 … with drafts as 1.1, 1.2Procedures, work instructions and forms

Whatever the scheme, three rules keep it reliable.

  1. Every released change raises the revision. No "minor" edits to a released document without a new revision, or two different documents will exist with the same identifier.
  2. The revision appears on every page, or on every sheet of a drawing, with the effective date.
  3. Each document carries a short revision history: revision, date, what changed and why, and who approved it. "Updated" is not a description. "Torque for M8 bolts changed from 22 to 25 N·m per customer ECN 1142" is.

Marking the changed text with a bar in the margin or a highlight in the first release of a new revision helps people see what is different, which is the first thing they need to know.

One master copy

Most document control failures come from copies. A procedure is printed, emailed, saved to a desktop or photographed on a phone, and from then on the copy no longer changes when the master does. The control is to have one master location for current documents, make it easy to reach from where people work, and treat everything else as uncontrolled.

  • Electronic master: a single shared library with version history and permissions, where most people can read and only owners can edit. Link to documents rather than attaching them to emails.
  • Paper at the point of use: if instructions must be on paper at a machine or in a vehicle, keep a list of where each controlled copy is, replace them all on revision and collect the old ones. Mark printouts that are not on the list "uncontrolled when printed", with the print date.
  • Obsolete versions: remove them from use. If you keep them for reference, mark them clearly as superseded and store them apart from current documents.

Documents from outside

Customer drawings and specifications, industry standards, regulations and supplier data sheets are documents you did not write but must work to. The standard requires you to identify and control them. Keep a register listing each one, its current revision or edition, where the master copy is, who is responsible for watching for updates, and how often they check. Customer drawings deserve the most care, because a revision issued by the customer must reach the shop floor before the next job starts. A drawing revision mix-up is one of the example risks in our article on risk-based thinking.

Records: how long, and how protected

For records, set a retention period by type and write it down. The period should reflect what customers require by contract, what regulation requires and what you need to answer a complaint or a warranty claim. Examples for a small manufacturer might look like this; the right periods for you depend on your contracts and the law that applies to you.

RecordExample retentionReason
Inspection and test resultsProduct life plus warranty period, or as the customer requiresEvidence of conformity if a claim arises
Calibration recordsAt least as long as the inspection results that relied on the instrumentTo show measurements were valid
Training and competence recordsDuration of employment plus a set periodEvidence that people were competent
Internal audits, management reviews, corrective actionsAt least one full certification cycleCertification audits look back across the cycle
Supplier evaluationsWhile the supplier is approved, plus a set periodEvidence of supplier control

Protect records from change. Store completed forms as read-only files or signed PDFs, keep the audit trail switched on in electronic systems, and back them up. When the retention period ends, dispose of them deliberately, and securely if they contain personal or customer information.

A minimum setup for a small business

  1. One shared folder or library for current controlled documents, with version history on and edit rights limited to owners.
  2. A master list: reference, title, current revision, effective date, owner. A spreadsheet is enough.
  3. A standard header or footer on every document showing reference, revision, date and approver.
  4. A revision history table at the end of each document.
  5. A separate archive folder for superseded revisions, read-only.
  6. A register of external documents with a named person for each.
  7. A one-page retention schedule for records.

This can be set up in a few days with tools you already have. Specialized document management software helps as volume grows, particularly for approval workflows and electronic signatures, but it is not a requirement.

What an auditor will check

Auditors rarely start in the document library. They start at the workstation. Expect them to:

  • pick up the instruction or drawing in use and compare its revision with the master list;
  • look for unofficial copies, handwritten notes and photographs of instructions;
  • ask an operator how they would know if the instruction had changed;
  • trace a recent change from the request, through approval, to the revised document in use;
  • check that a customer's latest drawing revision has reached production;
  • ask for a record from two years ago and see how long it takes to find.

Common failures

  • Silent edits: a released document changed without raising the revision.
  • Copies everywhere: printouts and email attachments that outlive the master.
  • Customer revisions stuck in an inbox: no defined route from the customer to the shop floor.
  • Over-documentation: procedures for tasks that trained people do reliably, which then go out of date because nobody reads them.
  • Records that cannot be found: kept, but in personal folders and inboxes.

AI tools can help with document control: drafting revisions, comparing versions and checking cross-references. Their output must still be reviewed and approved like any other draft. See ISO 9001 and AI for the controls.

Key points

  • Documents tell people what to do and are revised. Records show what was done and are protected from change.
  • Clause 7.5 sets outcomes, not a method: the right version where it is needed, approved, protected, and traceable through its changes.
  • One master location, one owner per document, and a revision raised on every released change prevent most failures.
  • Control documents from customers and other outside sources as carefully as your own.
