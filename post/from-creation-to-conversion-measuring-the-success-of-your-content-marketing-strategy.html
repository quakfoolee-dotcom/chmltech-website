How to Measure Content Marketing: From Visits to Enquiries

Quak Foo LeeMar 10, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20265 min read

Content marketing (articles, guides, case studies, videos, templates) only earns its cost if it brings the right people to your business and helps some of them become customers. Measuring it does not need expensive tools. It needs a clear idea of what each piece is for, a few measures that match that purpose, and a monthly habit of looking at them. This article, first written in 2023, has been rewritten for Google Analytics 4 and for a search landscape where AI answers increasingly sit between your content and your readers.

Start with the job each piece of content does

A guide written to attract engineers searching for a calculation method should be judged on search traffic and how long people stay. A case study written to reassure buyers late in a decision should be judged on whether it is viewed before enquiries and proposals. Judging both by page views produces the wrong decisions. Before measuring, label each piece by its main job: attract, engage, convert or support sales.

Five levels of measurement

LevelQuestionMeasuresWhere to find them
ReachAre the right people finding it?Search impressions and clicks, sessions from search, new usersSearch Console, GA4
EngagementDo they read it?Engaged sessions, engagement rate, scroll to 75%, average engagement timeGA4
ActionDo they take the next step?Downloads, newsletter sign-ups, contact forms, calls (key events)GA4
Business resultDoes it lead to revenue?Qualified leads, proposals and wins that touched the contentCRM
ReputationDoes it build authority?Links from other sites, mentions, citations in AI answers, sharesSearch Console, alerts, manual checks

Most small businesses stop at reach. The most useful insight usually sits at the action level: which pieces lead visitors to do something. In GA4, mark the actions that matter, such as a contact form submission or a template download, as key events so they can be counted by page. Our GA4 guide shows how.

Worked example: a quarterly content review

An engineering consultancy reviews five pieces of content over a quarter. The figures are illustrative.

Three conclusions follow. The two technical guides do the most work: they bring visitors who read and act. The case study has few visits but converts at a high rate, so it should be linked from the guides and sent to prospects, not judged on traffic. The productivity list attracts visitors who leave quickly and never act; it should be improved with specific, expert content or retired. The news piece did its job briefly and needs no further effort.

A content scorecard you can keep monthly

  1. Organic clicks from Search Console, for the site and for your top 20 pages.
  2. Engaged sessions and engagement rate by landing page in GA4.
  3. Key events by page, and key events per 1,000 visits.
  4. Enquiries that mention or follow content, recorded in the CRM with a simple source field.
  5. New links and mentions, checked monthly.

Put these on one sheet, compare with last month and the same month last year, and write one sentence on what you will change. That sentence is the point of the exercise.

Improving content that already ranks

The fastest gains usually come from pages that already get impressions but few clicks or actions, not from new articles. In Search Console, sort pages by impressions and look for those with a low click-through rate or an average position between 5 and 20. For each, check whether the title and description match what searchers want, whether the page answers the question in the first paragraph, whether the content is current, and whether there is a clear next step such as a related guide, a template or a contact link. Updating five such pages a quarter often does more than publishing ten new ones.

Who should own the numbers

In a small business, one person should own the content scorecard, even if writing is shared. Their job is not to produce reports but to bring one recommendation to the monthly marketing or management meeting: which piece to improve, which to promote, which to retire, and what to write next based on the questions customers asked that month.

Measuring in the age of AI answers

Search engines and AI assistants now answer many questions directly, so some readers get value from your content without visiting. Two practical responses: track impressions as well as clicks, since rising impressions with falling clicks often means your content is being summarized; and check from time to time whether AI assistants cite your pages for the questions you care about. Content that contains your own data, worked examples and experience is the kind most likely to be cited and the hardest to replace with a summary. See SEO vs GEO for more.

Attribution without overthinking it

Business-to-business buyers often read several pieces over weeks before getting in touch, and analytics tools only see part of that. Do not let imperfect attribution stop you. Ask new enquiries how they found you, add a "how did you hear about us" field to forms, and look at the pages a contact viewed before converting where your tools allow it. Patterns across a year of enquiries are more reliable than any single attribution model.

Common mistakes

  • Counting page views and social likes as success.
  • Not marking key events, so you cannot see which content leads to action.
  • Judging a sales-support piece by its traffic.
  • Publishing more instead of improving the pieces that already rank.
  • Reviewing numbers without deciding anything.
