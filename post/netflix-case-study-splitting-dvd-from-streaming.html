Netflix Case Study: Splitting DVD from Streaming

This case is part of the Chmltech Business case series. It is written for discussion, in the style of a business school teaching case: it stops at the decision and leaves the outcome to a closed section at the end, so you can decide first. The decision belongs to Reed Hastings, Netflix's co-founder and chief executive, in the spring and summer of 2011. It is drawn from public reporting and company statements listed under Sources; it is not based on interviews or private documents.

The decision

In mid-2011 Netflix looked unstoppable. Its old rival Blockbuster had gone bankrupt the previous autumn. Its share price had climbed past $300. Most of its customers paid $9.99 a month for a plan that combined unlimited streaming with one DVD by mail at a time. Reed Hastings believed the DVD business, profitable as it was, would shrink, and that the company's future was streaming alone. Streaming needed much more money for film and TV licences, and the content owners were raising their prices sharply.

Hastings had to decide how fast to move. Netflix could keep the combined plan and let customers drift to streaming in their own time. Or it could separate the two services and price them separately now, so that each paid its own way and the company could put its money into streaming. Separate prices meant that a customer who wanted both would pay much more.

Company background

Hastings and Marc Randolph founded Netflix in 1997 as a DVD-by-mail rental service. Its subscription model, with no late fees and a queue of films delivered in red envelopes, beat Blockbuster's stores and fees. In 2007 Netflix added streaming at no extra charge for its DVD subscribers. Streaming grew rapidly as broadband spread and Netflix's app appeared on game consoles, TVs and phones, and in 2010 the company launched its first streaming-only plan and expanded to Canada.

The two businesses had very different economics. DVD rental relied on owning physical discs, distribution centres and postage, a business with a known cost but no growth. Streaming relied on licensing content from studios for a fixed fee over a period, which became more expensive every time a deal was renewed, but cost almost nothing to deliver to one more customer.

Netflix's management saw that the combined plan hid the real cost of each service and slowed the shift to streaming. Content costs were rising fast; a major licence with Starz, which supplied many recent films, was up for renewal. Hastings had also seen how hard it was for a company to give up a business that still made money: AOL with dial-up, Blockbuster with stores. He wanted Netflix to move before the change was forced on it.

The risk was customer anger. A 60% price increase for the most loyal customers, with no new benefit, would be the largest in the company's history. Netflix had few streaming competitors of its size in 2011, but customers could cancel with a click.

The options

Option A: Keep the combined plan for now

Raise prices modestly over time and let DVD decline naturally. Smoother for customers, but streaming growth stays tied to an old business and Netflix may be too slow when rivals arrive.

Option B: Separate prices, one company

Price streaming and DVD separately at $7.99 each, under one brand and one website. Clear economics and a strong push to streaming, at the cost of a 60% increase for combined customers.

Option C: Separate prices and separate companies

Split the DVD service into its own brand with its own website, account and management, so each can be run for its own market. Maximum focus on streaming, but customers who want both must manage two accounts.

Option D: Exit DVD outright

Announce a closing date for the DVD service and move all effort to streaming. Clear, but it gives up a profitable business and millions of customers who still want discs.

Discussion questions

  1. Was Hastings right that the DVD business would shrink? If so, why did moving early cause so much damage?
  2. Use Exhibit 1. How would you have introduced the new prices to soften the reaction? What would you have offered existing customers?
  3. What is the difference between Option B and Option C for the customer? For the company?
  4. When should a company stop protecting a profitable product that it believes is declining?
  5. How much customer loss would you have accepted to reach a streaming-only future a year or two earlier?

What happened next

Decide what you would do before you open this section.

Show what the company did and how it turned out

Hastings went further than most observers expected. On July 12, 2011, Netflix announced that it would separate the plans, raising the price for customers who wanted both from $9.99 to $15.98 a month, about 60% more. The reaction was furious, on Netflix's own blog, Facebook page and in the press. On September 18, Hastings published an apology for how the change had been explained, and in the same post announced that the DVD service would become a separate company called Qwikster, with its own website and account. Customers who wanted both services would have to use two sites.

The second announcement made things worse. On October 10, just over three weeks later, Netflix cancelled Qwikster, though it kept the new prices. In the third quarter Netflix lost about 800,000 U.S. subscribers, ending September with 23.8 million, its first decline in years and more than the 600,000 it had predicted. The share price fell from a peak near $305 in July to the $60s and $70s by late 2011, a loss of more than three-quarters of its value. Hastings later said Netflix had moved too fast.

The recovery came quicker than the reaction suggested. Netflix added about 600,000 U.S. subscribers in the fourth quarter, ending 2011 with 24.4 million. It then put its money into streaming and, from 2013, its own programming, starting with House of Cards. The DVD service continued, shrinking, until Netflix closed it on September 29, 2023. By the end of 2024 Netflix had more than 300 million paid memberships worldwide.

In hindsight, the strategy was right and the execution was wrong. Separating the prices pushed customers toward the future Netflix was betting on. Doing it suddenly, with a large increase, no new benefit and then a confusing split, turned loyal customers into critics.

Lessons for small and mid-sized businesses

Be willing to replace your own product before a competitor does. Netflix chose to shrink a profitable business to grow the one with a future. Most SMEs have a legacy product or service that will decline; plan the move rather than waiting for it.

A right strategy can still fail on execution. The size of a price change, how it is explained and what customers get in return matter as much as the logic behind it. Test the message with a few customers first. The change management principles apply to customers as much as to staff.

Do not make customers do the work of your reorganisation. Qwikster asked customers to manage two accounts so that Netflix could run two businesses. Organise internally however you like; keep it simple for the customer.

Correct mistakes quickly. Netflix reversed Qwikster in three weeks, which limited the damage. Admitting a wrong call early costs less than defending it.
