Communication on the Shop Floor: Tiered Meetings, Shift Handovers and Visual Management

Quak Foo LeeApr 2, 2023Updated Nov 13, 20264 min read

In a manufacturing plant, most communication problems show up as something physical: a wrong part made because a drawing changed and the old one was still at the machine, a breakdown nobody told the next shift about, or a defect that repeats for days because the people who saw it never reached the people who could fix it. This article, first written in 2023 as a list of general tips, has been rewritten around the practices that solve those problems on the shop floor.

Tiered daily meetings

Short, standing meetings at fixed times, in layers, move information up and decisions down within the same day. Each tier uses a simple board with the same few measures, and any problem the tier cannot solve goes up to the next one.

Shift handovers

Information lost between shifts causes a large share of repeat problems. A written or digital handover, completed by the outgoing lead and read with the incoming lead at the machine or line, covers the same six areas every time:

AreaWhat to pass on
SafetyIncidents, near misses, hazards left in place
ProductionOutput against plan; job in progress and where it stopped
QualityDefects found, holds, first-off checks pending
EquipmentBreakdowns, temporary fixes, maintenance requested
MaterialsShortages, wrong material, deliveries expected
PeopleAbsences, training, overtime approved

Keep the form to one page. The conversation matters as much as the form; a two-minute walk of the line together catches what writing misses.

Visual management

The best communication on the shop floor often needs no words. Boards showing hourly output against target, red and green status on each machine, andon lights or calls for help, marked floor locations and current drawings at the point of use let anyone see the state of the work at a glance. The rule: if a supervisor has to ask how the line is doing, the board is not working.

Instructions that people can follow

  • Work instructions with photos at the workstation, controlled so the old version is removed when a new one is issued. See process documentation that people use.
  • Plain language, with key steps translated where the workforce speaks several languages.
  • Change notices for engineering changes, explained at the tier 1 meeting before the first affected job starts.
  • Teach-back: for critical tasks, ask the operator to explain the step back rather than asking "any questions?"

Listening upward

Operators see problems first. Make it easy and safe to raise them: a problem board at each area, a promise that every item gets an answer within a week, and recognition for ideas that are used. When people see their suggestions acted on, they keep making them; when suggestions disappear, they stop.

Worked example: cutting repeat defects with a handover and tier 1 meeting

A plastics moulder runs three shifts and sees the same short-shot defect reappear after almost every shift change, because setting adjustments made by one shift are not passed on. The plant introduces a one-page handover covering process settings, defects and holds, and a ten-minute tier 1 meeting at each shift start. Within two months, repeat defects of the same type fall by more than half, and the tier 2 meeting starts fixing causes rather than symptoms. The figures are illustrative.

Digital tools, used carefully

Tablets at the line, digital boards, shift logs and team messaging apps can make all of this faster, especially across shifts and sites. Keep the paper version's simplicity: the same measures every day, one place to look, and no expectation that operators read messages while running a machine. Our scorecards and meeting rhythm article covers the management side of the same system.

What to put on a tier 1 board

RowExample content
SafetyDays since last incident; today's hazard or reminder
QualityDefects yesterday by type; any holds
DeliveryOutput by hour against plan; jobs due today
CostScrap and downtime minutes yesterday
PeopleAttendance, training, recognition
ProblemsOpen items with owner and due date; items escalated to tier 2

Common mistakes

  • Meetings that run long because they turn into problem-solving sessions; solve offline and report back.
  • Boards updated by supervisors only, so operators do not own them.
  • Escalations that never get an answer, which quickly teaches people to stop raising issues.
  • Too many measures; five or six that matter are enough.
  • Skipping meetings on busy days, which are the days communication matters most.
