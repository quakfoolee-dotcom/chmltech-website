Mis-Picks, Mis-Ships and the Real Cost of an Error

The customer ordered 12 cases of the 3/4-inch fitting and received 12 cases of the 1/2-inch. Now there is a phone call, a return label, a second shipment, a credit note, a carton coming back to be received and restocked, and a customer wondering whether to order from you next time. Picking that line took about twenty seconds. Fixing it takes most of an hour and a good part of the margin on the order.

Parts 1 to 4 made the warehouse faster and its records right. This part looks at the errors that still get through: what they cost, where they come from, and which checks pay for themselves in the example warehouse.

An error costs more the later it is found

Published estimates put the cost of one mis-pick that reaches the customer at about $22 to $100, depending on the product, the freight and the customer. Operations that do not use barcodes commonly see error rates of 1 to 2%. The example uses $75 for an error that reaches the customer, built up as follows:

Cost of one error at the customerAssumed
Return freight$15
Reship freight$15
Handling$20
Service credit$10
Lost customers$15
Total$75

The lost-customer line is an expected value: a small share of customers who receive a wrong order buy less afterwards, and that lost margin is spread over every error.

The example's errors

The distributor picks 225,000 lines a year for 75,000 orders. With paper pick lists, 0.60% of lines are picked wrong: the wrong item (0.27% of lines), the wrong quantity (0.24%) or the right item in the wrong order's tote after batch picking (0.09%). That is about 1,350 errors a year, and with no check before shipping every one reaches a customer.

\[\text{Cost of errors a year} = 1{,}350 \times \$75 \approx \$101{,}250\]

Order picking accuracy is about 98.20% of orders. The WERC benchmark puts best-in-class order picking accuracy at 99.68% or better in its 2025 report, and earlier reports gave a median of about 99.3%, so the example sits in the bottom group.

The checks, in order

Check 1: compare at packing. The packer compares each case with the packing slip before taping the carton. It costs about 20 seconds an order and needs nothing new. A careful visual check catches perhaps half to two-thirds of errors: in the example about 756 a year, which leaves 594 going out.

Check 2: scan at the pick face. A handheld scanner confirms the location and the item at each pick, and the tote at each put. A wrong item or a wrong tote is refused at the shelf, where fixing it costs a few seconds. Quantity errors are harder: the picker scans once and keys the quantity, so many still get through. In the example, scanning stops about 975 errors at the shelf, and orders picked right rise to 99.50%. It needs handhelds and a mobile licence, about $7,200 up front and $3,000 a year.

Check 3: scan every case at packing. Replace the visual check with a scan of each case against the order. It catches almost every remaining wrong item and most wrong quantities, and at about 4 seconds a line it takes less time than the visual check for a typical order. Errors reaching customers fall to about 33 a year, and orders shipped right reach 99.96%.

Check 4: weigh the parcel. A check scale compares each packed parcel with its expected weight. It catches most remaining quantity errors, but by now there are very few to catch: about 26 a year. The scale and the 6 seconds an order cost more than they save, and the total rises from $25,000 to $29,000. A check scale pays where errors are frequent or costly, for example many small, similar items or expensive goods. In this warehouse, after scanning, it does not.

Figure 2 is the example's packing line. Each order leaves the pick zone on the left in a tote, the plastic bin the picker fills. At the pack station it is packed into a parcel, which passes the check scale (when it is on) and goes into the truck on the right. Red marks an order with an error. Every active checkpoint lights green as it checks an order, and red when it catches one, which then goes back along the re-pick lane. The check scale stops each parcel for a moment and shows its weight against the expected weight. An error that gets past every checkpoint goes to the customer, and a little later its return comes back. Errors are shown ten times more often than they happen so that you can see them. The figure steps through the fixes on its own, about twenty seconds each, until you pick one.

Scanning at the pick face and at packing takes the example from about $101,000 a year in errors to about $25,000 in errors and checks together, a saving of about $77,000 a year. The handhelds pay for themselves within months. Accuracy is the other half of the story: the customer now gets the right order 99.96% of the time.

Fix the causes too

Checks catch errors. The cause log tells you why they happened. Most mis-picks come from a short list:

  • Look-alike items side by side. Two sizes of the same fitting in neighbouring slots. Separate them when re-slotting (Part 1).
  • Poor labels. Faded, missing or ambiguous location labels. Label every location and every pick face clearly, with a barcode.
  • Wrong records. The system sends the picker to a location holding something else. That is phantom stock (Part 4).
  • Units of measure. A case of 12 against a pack of 6. Show the unit on the pick list and the scanner screen.
  • Pressure at the cut-off. Errors cluster in the last hour before the truck. Wave release (Part 2) spreads the load.

Common mistakes

  • Not knowing the cost of an error. Without it, every check looks either essential or unaffordable.
  • Checking everything twice. Each check costs time on every order. Add checks where errors are, and stop when the next one costs more than it saves.
  • Allowing scan overrides. A scanner that lets the picker skip a "wrong item" warning catches nothing.
  • Blaming people for system errors. Most mis-picks trace back to slotting, labels and records.
  • Measuring at the dock only. Count returns and customer complaints too, or escaped errors stay invisible.

Key points

  • An error costs about ten times more at each later stage: cents at the shelf, dollars at packing, $75 at the customer here.
  • With paper picking and no checks, the example sends about 1,350 errors a year to customers, costing about $101,000.
  • Scanning at the pick face and at packing cuts that to about 33 errors and saves about $77,000 a year, including the cost of the checks.
  • Stop adding checks when the next one costs more than the errors it catches: here, the check scale.
  • Log the cause of every error, and fix slotting, labels and records as well as checking.
