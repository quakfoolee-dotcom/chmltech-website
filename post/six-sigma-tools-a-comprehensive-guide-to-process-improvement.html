Six Sigma Tools by DMAIC Phase, with DPMO and Cpk Worked Examples

Six Sigma is a data-driven method for reducing defects and variation in a process. Its projects follow five phases, DMAIC: define, measure, analyze, improve and control. This guide, first published in 2023, has been rewritten to show which tools belong to which phase, with worked examples of the two calculations people most often get wrong.

Roles and belts

RoleTypical trainingWhat they do
Champion or sponsorHalf-day to two-day overviewSelects projects, provides resources, removes obstacles
Black BeltAbout four weeks spread over several months, plus projectsLeads complex projects full or part time; coaches Green Belts
Green BeltAbout one to two weeks, plus a projectLeads projects in their own area alongside their normal job
Yellow BeltOne to two daysTakes part in projects; collects data; applies basic tools

Small businesses rarely need Black Belts on staff. One or two trained Green Belts, with an outside coach for harder projects, is usually enough.

The DMAIC phases

What each phase should deliver

PhaseDeliverables at the review
DefineSigned charter, SIPOC, customer requirements, project scope and timeline
MeasureProcess map, data collection plan, measurement system check, baseline performance and capability
AnalyzeRoot causes confirmed with data, not opinion; size of each cause's effect
ImproveSolution chosen and piloted; before-and-after results; risks reviewed with FMEA
ControlControl plan, updated procedures, training done, control chart in use, handover to the process owner

Tools by phase

PhaseCommon toolsPurpose
DefineProject charter, SIPOC, voice of the customer, CTQ treeAgree what the problem is and what good looks like
MeasureProcess map, data collection plan, measurement system analysis (gauge R&R), baseline capabilityKnow where you start, and trust the numbers
AnalyzePareto chart, fishbone diagram, 5 whys, scatter plots, hypothesis tests, regressionSeparate the few real causes from the many possible ones
ImproveBrainstorming, design of experiments, FMEA, pilot runs, mistake-proofingFind the change that fixes the cause, and test it safely
ControlControl charts, control plan, standard work, visual managementKeep the process where you put it

A small business rarely needs all of them. A process map, a Pareto chart, a fishbone diagram and a control chart cover most projects. The statistical tools matter when the cause is not obvious and a wrong guess would be expensive.

A Pareto chart in practice

The invoicing team sorts last month's 36 errors by type. The table shows the count and cumulative share of each.

Error typeCountCumulative share
Wrong price1439%
Wrong quantity964%
Missing PO number681%
Wrong tax code492%
Wrong address3100%

The first two types account for 64% of errors. The project focuses there first: price errors come from an out-of-date price list, quantity errors from manual entry of handwritten orders.

The fishbone diagram

A fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram lists possible causes of a problem under headings, often the six Ms: methods, machines, materials, measurement, manpower (people) and mother nature (environment). It is a structured brainstorm. Its output is a list of suspects to test with data in the Analyze phase, not a conclusion.

Worked example 1: defects per million opportunities

An accounts team issues 2,400 invoices a month. Each invoice has 5 things that can go wrong (customer, items, prices, tax, terms). Last month, 36 errors were found. Defects per million opportunities (DPMO) is 36 ÷ (2,400 × 5) × 1,000,000 = 3,000. Using the standard conversion with a 1.5 sigma shift, that is about a 4.2 sigma process. "Six sigma" corresponds to 3.4 DPMO, so there is room to improve, but for an invoicing process 4.2 sigma may be good enough; the goal is set by what errors cost, not by the name of the method.

Worked example 2: process capability, Cp and Cpk

A machined shaft must be 9.70 to 10.30 mm. Measurements show an average of 10.08 mm with a standard deviation of 0.06 mm.

  • Cp, which compares the tolerance width with the spread of the process, is (10.30 − 9.70) ÷ (6 × 0.06) = 1.67.
  • Cpk, which also accounts for how well the process is centred, is the smaller of (10.30 − 10.08) and (10.08 − 9.70), divided by 3 × 0.06 = 1.22.

The process has enough precision (Cp 1.67) but sits off-centre, which lowers Cpk to 1.22. Re-centring it on 10.00 mm would raise Cpk to 1.67 with no other change. Many customers require a Cpk of at least 1.33. Capability figures are only meaningful if the process is stable, which a control chart confirms first.

Control charts

A control chart plots a measure over time with a centre line and upper and lower control limits, usually set three standard deviations from the mean, calculated from the process itself. Points inside the limits with no pattern show a stable process; the variation is common-cause and only a process change will reduce it. A point outside the limits, or a run of several points on one side of the centre line, signals a special cause worth investigating. Control limits are not specification limits: a stable process can still make parts out of specification, which is what capability indices reveal.

When not to use Six Sigma

  • The cause is obvious and the fix is cheap; just fix it.
  • The problem is flow and waiting, not defects; use Lean.
  • There is no reliable data and no way to collect it in a reasonable time.
  • The process runs too rarely to measure, such as an annual event.

Lean and Six Sigma together

Lean removes waste and waiting; Six Sigma removes defects and variation. Most improvement programs now use both: Lean tools to make work flow, and Six Sigma tools when a quality problem needs data to solve. Our Lean Six Sigma training covers both.
