Do We Need a WMS Yet? When Spreadsheets Stop Scaling

Every warehouse starts on paper. Someone prints the orders, walks the floor, ticks the lines and hands the sheets to the office, where they are keyed into a spreadsheet or the accounting package at the end of the day. With two people and a few hundred SKUs it works, because the people know where everything is.

Then the business grows. More SKUs, more locations, more people, a second shift, an e-commerce channel. The question arrives in every growing distributor sooner or later: do we need a warehouse management system yet? Vendors will say yes. The accountant looks at the subscription and says not yet. This part puts numbers on the question with the same distributor as Parts 1 to 6, at any volume from 50 to 1,200 orders a day.

What a WMS actually does

A WMS is not mainly a stock list. The ERP or accounting system already knows how many of each item the company owns. A WMS knows where each one is, down to the location, and it tells people what to do next: where to put this pallet, which orders to pick together, which route to walk, which location to count today. Every move is confirmed with a scan, so the record changes the moment the stock does.

The options an SME usually weighs:

The price ranges come from published guides: cloud WMS subscriptions typically run $100 to $500 a user a month, often with a monthly base fee, and small single-site operations commonly spend $25,000 to $75,000 in the first year including implementation, training and hardware. Enterprise systems with an integrator start in the hundreds of thousands. The model spreads implementation over five years and scanners over four.

Where the money goes without one

Software is the easy cost to see. The costs of running without it are spread across the floor and the office, and most of them grow faster than the business:

  • Searching. When locations live in people's heads, every new SKU, location and person adds searching. The model adds 50% to paper's floor time an order for every 1,000 orders a day of volume, against 3% for a WMS that directs every move.
  • Keying. Every pick list, receipt and adjustment is typed in again by someone in the office, and someone answers the phone when a customer asks whether an item is in stock.
  • Counting. Without location records, the only way to fix the books is a wall-to-wall count with the warehouse shut (Part 4).
  • Errors. Without a scan at pick and pack, about 0.75% of orders reach the customer wrong, at $75 each (Part 5).
  • Wrong records. With 92% of locations right, some orders hit a location that is empty or short: a search, a short shipment or a rush order.

The break-even volume

Figure 1 is the answer in one picture. Below about 60 orders a day, paper and a spreadsheet is the cheapest way to run the example's warehouse: the team is small enough to know where everything is, and any software costs more than it saves. From there to about 135, the ERP's own inventory module with scanners wins: most of the benefit comes from the scan, which kills keying and most errors, and it costs little if the ERP is already paid for. Above about 135 orders a day, a cloud WMS is cheapest, and the gap widens with every order.

The enterprise WMS never wins in the SME range. Its cost an order only falls below the cloud WMS at about 2,440 orders a day, eight times the example's volume. Its extra capabilities are real, but an SME pays for them long before it can use them.

Between about 100 and 250 orders a day the ERP module and the cloud WMS are close: at 150 orders a day the gap is $600 a year. In that band, choose on fit, not on price (see the questions below).

Pickers with paper carry their lists back to the office, where they wait to be keyed in at the end of the shift; pickers with scanners send each pick to the system as it happens, shown as a cyan spark. A WMS pays for itself at this volume. The cloud WMS costs about $52,000 a year in subscriptions and devices, against about $1,200 for paper. It saves $129,000 a year overall, mostly in floor and office labour, with 1 fewer floor people and fewer errors and wrong records. The one-time cost of $68,000 includes implementation, scanners and a six-week dip in floor pace after go-live, and is repaid about 9 months after signing, counting 3 months to go live.

The ERP module is the cheaper first step. It gets most of the way for less: $572,000 a year, and paid back in about 7 months. For a business below about 135 orders a day, or one still cleaning up its processes, it is a sound start. Its limits show as volume grows: no wave planning, weaker put-away logic and slower records.

The enterprise WMS costs more than paper here. At $695,000 a year it is dearer than running on paper, and its $354,000 one-time cost takes 9 years to repay.

The model is conservative about growth. A business on paper at 300 orders a day needs 8 floor people; at 900 it needs 28, against 20 with a WMS. Hiring and training those extra people, and the space they need, are not counted.

Signs you have outgrown spreadsheets

SignWhy it matters
Only one or two people know where things areThe warehouse stops when they are away, and it cannot grow past them
Stock records below about 95% rightStockouts, rush orders and customers told "in stock" when it is not (Part 4)
Errors reaching customers above 0.5% of ordersAt $75 each, it adds up quickly (Part 5)
Someone spends hours a day keying in paperworkPaying twice for every transaction, and a day behind
The warehouse shuts for a yearly countLost shipping days and a record that is wrong again within weeks
Lot, expiry or serial numbers must be trackedRecalls and first-expired-first-out are not practical on paper
A second channel or site, or 3PL clientsStock must be split, reserved and billed by client and location
Large customers ask for advance ship notices and labelsEDI and compliant labels need system data at the dock

Three or more of these together usually mean the warehouse has already paid for a WMS in hidden costs.

ERP module or WMS: the questions to ask

  • Does the ERP module direct the work, or only record it? Directed put-away, batch or wave picking and system-chosen cycle counts are where most of a WMS's labour saving comes from.
  • Does it handle your units? Cases, inners and eaches; pallets with mixed items; catch weight.
  • Lot, expiry and serial numbers? At receiving, at picking and on the shipping documents.
  • Shipping integration? Rates, labels and tracking from the carriers you use, without retyping.
  • Scanners on the floor? Real offline-capable handhelds, not a web page on a phone that times out in the back aisles.
  • What happens at 3 times the volume? Users, licences, performance and the price at that size.

A roadmap that works for SMEs

StepWhat to do
1. Fix the process firstSpace and slotting, pick methods, the dock, counting, errors and labour (Parts 1 to 6). A WMS on a messy process automates the mess
2. Clean the dataLabel every location. Clean the item master: dimensions, weights, units, barcodes
3. Write requirements by workflowReceiving, put-away, picking, packing, shipping, counting, returns. What must happen, and who does it
4. Scripted demosShortlist three to five systems and make each demonstrate your workflows with your items and orders
5. Check the linksERP or accounting, shipping, e-commerce and EDI. Integration is where projects go over budget
6. Go live in a quiet monthJanuary to March for most distributors. Never in the run-up to the peak (Part 6)
7. Support and measureVendor on site for the first weeks. Track the same measures before and after

Common mistakes

  • Buying for the business you hope to be in ten years. An enterprise system for a 300-order warehouse costs more than paper.
  • Waiting until it hurts. In the example, staying on paper past 135 orders a day costs more every month.
  • Skipping the data cleanup. Wrong dimensions and missing barcodes stop a go-live faster than any software bug.
  • Customising before using. Run the standard workflows for three months, then change only what the floor really needs.
  • Counting only the subscription. Implementation, scanners, training and the go-live dip often cost more than the first year's fees.

Key points

  • A WMS's value is knowing where every item is, and directing the work, not keeping a stock list.
  • In the example, paper is cheapest below about 60 orders a day, the ERP module with scanners up to about 135, and a cloud WMS above that.
  • At 300 orders a day, a cloud WMS saves $129,000 a year against paper, and pays back about 9 months after signing.
  • An enterprise WMS does not pay at SME volumes. In the example it costs more than paper.
  • Fix the process and the data first, choose on your own workflows, and go live in a quiet month.
