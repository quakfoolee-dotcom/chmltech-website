Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 22 · 7 min

What 5S really is (and what it is not)

5S is a structured method for organizing a workplace so that the right things are in the right place, in good condition, and stay that way. The name comes from five Japanese words, usually translated into English as Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain. It grew out of Japanese manufacturing practice and became one of the foundations of Lean operations.

The most common misunderstanding is that 5S is a clean-up campaign. A weekend of tidying produces a nice photo and then decays within a month. Real 5S is a system for making abnormal conditions obvious, so that problems are seen and fixed before they turn into defects, delays or injuries.

The five stages at a glance

StageCore questionTypical output
SortWhat do we actually need here?Red-tag area, fewer items
Set in OrderWhere should each needed item live?Labelled locations, shadow boards
ShineIs everything clean and working?Cleaning-as-inspection routines
StandardizeHow do we make the good state the normal state?Checklists, visual standards
SustainHow do we keep it going?Audits, leader routines, habits

Many organizations add a sixth S, Safety, and call it 6S. In this course we treat safety as a thread that runs through every stage rather than a separate step at the end. Removing clutter removes trip hazards. Labelled locations keep chemicals and sharp tools where they belong. Cleaning reveals leaks and frayed cords.

A quick example

Imagine a 40-person fabrication shop. Welders keep spare tips, gloves and clamps in a shared cabinet at the end of the bay. Every shift, people walk back and forth, borrow from each other and occasionally use a worn ground clamp because the good one cannot be found. After 5S, each welding cell has a small labelled board with its own clamps and consumables, a minimum stock line, and a two-minute end-of-shift check. Nothing about the welding changed, but the hunting, the borrowing and the unsafe substitutions largely disappear.

What 5S is not

  • Not a one-time event or a housekeeping blitz.
  • Not a job for the cleaning contractor.
  • Not only for factories: clinics, warehouses, labs and offices all benefit.
  • Not about making everything look identical; it is about making everything easy to find, use and check.

Through this course you will move through each stage in order, run a baseline audit, and build a visual management system and a sustain routine for your own area.

Key takeaways

  • 5S is a system for making abnormal conditions visible, not a clean-up event.
  • The five stages are Sort, Set in Order, Shine, Standardize and Sustain.
  • Safety runs through every stage, whether or not you call it a sixth S.

Apply it at work

Walk one area you are responsible for and take five photos of things that would confuse a new employee or slow someone down. Write one sentence per photo about which S would address it.

Check your understanding: A plant manager organizes a Saturday clean-up and the area looks great on Monday. Six weeks later it looks the same as before. What is the most likely reason?

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 22 · 6 min

How 5S attacks waste

Lean thinking classifies non-value-added activity into categories of waste. The most widely used list is eight wastes, often remembered with the acronym DOWNTIME. 5S does not fix every waste on its own, but it is one of the fastest ways to reduce several of them at once.

WasteExample in an SMEHow 5S helps
DefectsWrong part picked from a mixed binSet in Order separates and labels parts
OverproductionBatches made "just in case"Visual limits on WIP space expose it
WaitingOperator waits for a gauge someone borrowedDedicated, labelled tool locations
Non-utilized talentSkilled tech spends time hunting for paperworkOrganized information at point of use
TransportationSupplies stored across the buildingRelocate by frequency of use
InventoryThree years of obsolete spare partsSort and red-tag removal
MotionBending, reaching, walking to a shared cabinetPoint-of-use storage, ergonomic heights
Extra processingRe-printing forms because the current version is unclearStandardized, controlled documents

Searching is the hidden cost

The waste most people underestimate is searching. A few minutes per person per day looks trivial, but it repeats every day for every person. Consider a distributor's warehouse with 12 staff who each lose 15 minutes a day looking for scanners, tape guns, pallet labels or the right paperwork. That is 3 hours a day across the team. Over 240 working days it is 720 hours a year, roughly the working time of a part-time employee, spent producing nothing. Later in this course you will use a calculator to put a dollar figure on this for your own area.

Waste and safety are linked

Many wastes create risk as well as cost. Excess inventory gets stacked high or in aisles. Unnecessary motion leads to strain injuries. Searching leads to improvising with the wrong tool. When you remove the waste, you usually remove the hazard too, which is why safety committees and improvement teams should work on 5S together rather than separately.

Using the waste lens on a walk

  • Watch one task from start to finish, not the whole area at once.
  • Note every time the person stops, walks, searches or asks someone.
  • Tag each observation with a waste type and a possible S that would address it.

Key takeaways

  • 5S directly reduces motion, transportation, inventory, waiting and defect waste.
  • Searching time looks small per person but adds up to hundreds of hours a year.
  • Removing waste usually removes hazards as well.

Apply it at work

Observe one person doing a routine task for 20 minutes. Count every search, walk or interruption and tag each with one of the eight wastes.

Check your understanding: A clinic's nurses regularly walk to a storeroom down the hall for the same three supplies several times per shift. Which waste is this primarily, and which S addresses it?

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 22 · 7 min

Safety as the thread through every S

A well-run 5S program is one of the most practical safety tools a small business has. It does not replace your occupational health and safety program, hazard assessments or legal obligations under provincial OHS rules, but it makes many hazards visible and keeps controls in place day after day.

Safety in each stage

StageSafety contribution
SortRemoves clutter, trip hazards, damaged tools and expired or unlabelled chemicals
Set in OrderKeeps aisles and emergency exits clear; puts heavy items at waist height; segregates incompatible materials
ShineCleaning reveals leaks, frayed cords, cracked guards and worn PPE
StandardizeMakes the safe condition the documented standard, with visual cues for PPE zones and lockout points
SustainRegular audits and leader walks catch drift before it becomes an incident

Visual safety controls that fit 5S

  • Floor marking for walkways, forklift lanes and keep-clear zones in front of electrical panels, eyewash stations and fire extinguishers.
  • Labelled homes for PPE at the entrance to the zone where it is required.
  • Clearly marked locations for spill kits and first aid supplies, with a minimum-stock indicator.
  • Lockout points tagged and shown on a simple equipment map.

Worked example

A small food packaging plant runs its first Sort in the maintenance room. The team finds three unlabelled spray bottles, a ladder with a cracked rail, and cartons stacked in front of the electrical panel. None of these were on anyone's list of known hazards, because they had become part of the background. The team disposes of the unknown chemicals through the proper channel, removes the ladder from service, and marks a keep-clear box in front of the panel. Then they add these three checks to the area's weekly 5S checklist so the problems do not return.

Working with your safety program

Involve your joint health and safety committee or safety representative from the start. Ask them which hazards appear most often in inspections and build those into your 5S standards. When a 5S audit finds a safety issue, route it into your existing hazard reporting process so it is tracked and closed, rather than living only on a 5S scoresheet.

Rule of thumb: if a 5S change creates a new hazard, such as storing heavy bins overhead to free floor space, it is not an improvement.

Key takeaways

  • Each S has a direct safety contribution, from removing hazards to keeping controls in place.
  • Visual controls such as keep-clear zones and PPE homes make the safe condition obvious.
  • Link 5S findings into your existing hazard reporting so safety issues are tracked and closed.

Apply it at work

Ask your safety representative for the three most common findings from recent workplace inspections. Write down which S would prevent each from recurring.

Check your understanding: During a Set in Order exercise, a team proposes moving heavy spare motors to a high shelf to free floor space. What is the best response?

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 22 · 8 min

Running a baseline 5S site assessment

Before changing anything, measure where you are. A baseline assessment gives you a starting score, photos to compare against later, and a list of priorities. It also builds credibility: when people see an honest assessment, they are more likely to trust the program.

Define the area

Pick a bounded area with a clear owner, such as one production cell, a stockroom, a lab bench or an intake desk. A whole building is too big for a first assessment. Draw a simple sketch of the area and its boundaries.

Score against clear criteria

Use a 1 to 5 scale for each S, plus safety and visual management. Write a short description of what each score looks like so two different auditors give similar scores.

ScoreSort example
1Unneeded items everywhere; no one knows what is needed
2Some clearing done, but large amounts of unneeded material remain
3Most unneeded items removed; a red-tag process exists
4Only needed items present; red-tag reviews happen on schedule
5Team prevents unneeded items from arriving in the first place

How to run the walk

  1. Walk the area with two or three people, including someone who works there and someone who does not.
  2. Take wide and close-up photos from fixed positions you can repeat later.
  3. Score each dimension independently, then compare and agree a score with evidence.
  4. Record the top three issues per dimension, including every safety concern.
  5. Share results with the team the same week, framed as a starting point, not a judgment.

Worked example

An engineering office runs a baseline on its shared plotting and document area. Scores: Sort 2, Set in Order 2, Shine 3, Standardize 1, Sustain 1, Safety 3, Visual management 1. The standout issues are stacks of superseded drawings, no labelled homes for supplies, and an extension cord running across the walkway to the plotter. The team decides to start with Sort and the cord, because both are quick and visible.

Common baseline mistakes

  • Scoring generously to avoid embarrassment, which leaves no room to show progress.
  • Skipping photos, which makes before-and-after comparison impossible.
  • Auditing without the people who work in the area, which creates resistance.

The lab after this lesson lets you run a self-assessment across seven dimensions and see your profile as a radar chart.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a bounded area that has a clear owner.
  • Written score descriptions make audits consistent between auditors.
  • Photos from fixed positions are the simplest proof of progress.

Apply it at work

Choose one area and run a 30-minute baseline walk with a colleague. Score each S plus safety and visual management from 1 to 5 and take photos from three fixed positions.

Check your understanding: Two supervisors score the same stockroom 2 and 4 for Set in Order. What is the most effective fix for future audits?

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 22 · 7 min

Sort: deciding what belongs

Sort is the first and most important S. You cannot organize clutter; you can only rearrange it. The goal is simple: keep only what is needed, in the quantity needed, in the area where it is needed.

The three decisions

For every item in the area, the team makes one of three decisions:

  • Keep at point of use: used frequently in this area for the work done here.
  • Store elsewhere or relocate: needed, but rarely, or needed by a different area.
  • Red-tag and remove: broken, obsolete, duplicate, unknown or not needed at all.

Use frequency as the main test

How often usedTypical decision
Every hour or every shiftKeep within arm's reach at the workstation
Daily or weeklyKeep in the area, in a labelled nearby location
Monthly or quarterlyStore in a central or secondary location
Less than once a yearQuestion whether to keep it at all
Never, or unknownRed-tag

Quantity matters too

Sort is not only about which items, but how many. If a packing station uses one tape gun at a time, it does not need four. If a lab uses one box of gloves per week, a case of twenty boxes at the bench is excess. Set a sensible quantity and move the rest to a supply location with a reorder point.

Safety is a sort criterion

Some items should be removed regardless of how often they are used: damaged ladders, tools with cracked handles, unlabelled containers, expired PPE, and chemicals without a safety data sheet on file. Treat these as immediate removals and route them through your hazard process rather than waiting for the red-tag review.

Worked example

A machine shop's tool crib holds 140 drill bits. The team checks job records and finds the cell regularly uses 18 sizes. They keep two of each of those 18 sizes at the machine, move one spare set to the crib, and red-tag 60 bits that are duplicates, worn or for a machine the shop no longer owns. Setup time drops because the operator no longer sorts through mixed bits, and a few worn bits that were causing poor holes leave the floor.

Watch out for

  • "We might need it someday." Ask: when was it last used, and what would it cost to get another if we do?
  • Personal stashes hidden in drawers, which undermine the shared standard.
  • Throwing out items that belong to another department. Red-tagging gives owners a chance to claim them.

Key takeaways

  • Sort comes first: you cannot organize clutter, only move it.
  • Frequency of use drives the keep, relocate or remove decision.
  • Damaged or unidentified items are removed immediately for safety reasons.

Apply it at work

Pick one drawer, shelf or cabinet in your area. Sort every item into keep, relocate or remove, and note how often each kept item is actually used.

Check your understanding: A packing station has five tape guns but only one packer works there per shift. What is the best Sort decision?

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 22 · 7 min

Running a red-tag event

A red-tag event is a structured, time-boxed Sort. It gives the team permission to challenge every item, while protecting items that might belong to someone else or have real value. Done well, it is often the most energizing day of a 5S program.

Prepare

  1. Agree the area, the date and who will take part. Include people who work there.
  2. Set up a red-tag holding area, clearly marked and away from walkways and exits.
  3. Prepare red tags or a simple log with: item, location found, reason, date, who tagged it, and proposed disposition.
  4. Agree who can approve disposal of capital items, inventory and anything with resale or scrap value.
  5. Brief everyone on safe lifting, PPE, and how to handle unknown chemicals. Unknown substances are reported, not moved.

Run

Work through the area systematically, shelf by shelf. For each item ask: is it needed, is it needed here, and is it needed in this quantity? If the answer to any is no, tag it and move it to the holding area. Photograph the area before and after.

Review and dispose

DispositionWhen to use it
Return to areaOwner shows it is used regularly
RelocateNeeded by another area or used rarely
Sell or recycleHas value but no use here
Dispose safelyBroken, obsolete or hazardous, using proper channels

Hold tagged items for a fixed period, often one to four weeks, so owners can claim them. Then make decisions and clear the holding area completely. A holding area that becomes permanent is just a new pile of clutter.

Worked example

A regional distributor runs a half-day red-tag event in its shipping area. The team tags 85 items, including obsolete label rolls, a broken pallet jack, two monitors and boxes of forms for a carrier the company stopped using. Over two weeks, 9 items are claimed and returned, 20 are relocated to the main storeroom, the pallet jack goes for repair assessment, and the rest are recycled or discarded. The shipping area gains a full bay of floor space.

Tips

  • Respect the finance rules for inventory write-offs and fixed assets.
  • Thank people for what they tagged, not just for what was thrown out.
  • Record recurring categories of tagged items; they point to purchasing or process problems.

The lab after this lesson lets you practise Sort decisions on a set of typical workplace items.

Key takeaways

  • A red-tag event is a time-boxed Sort with a clear holding area and disposal rules.
  • Hold tagged items for a fixed period, then clear the holding area completely.
  • Unknown chemicals are reported and handled through proper channels, not moved casually.

Apply it at work

Plan a 2-hour red-tag event for one area: choose the date, participants, holding area location, tag format and who approves disposal of valuable items.

Check your understanding: Three months after a red-tag event, the holding area is still full of tagged items. What does this indicate?

Set in Order: a place for everything

Once only needed items remain, Set in Order gives each one a defined home so that anyone can find it, use it and return it quickly. The standard often quoted is that a person new to the area should be able to find any item within about 30 seconds.

Principles for deciding locations

  • Frequency: the more often used, the closer to the point of use.
  • Sequence: arrange items in the order the work uses them.
  • Ergonomics: heavy and frequent items between knee and shoulder height; avoid twisting and overhead reaches.
  • Safety: keep aisles, exits and emergency equipment clear; segregate incompatible chemicals; store sharp tools guarded.
  • Visibility: it should be obvious when something is missing or in the wrong place.

Address every location

Give locations a simple address system, such as area, rack, shelf and position. Label both the location and the item, so returning an item is as easy as finding it. In a warehouse that might be "B-03-2"; in a clinic supply room it might be a shelf label with the item name and a photo.

Worked example

A medical clinic's intake desk handles forms, labels, a card reader, sanitizer and a phone. Staff currently store forms in three drawers and the label printer is under the counter on the far side. After observing two shifts, the team arranges the desk in task sequence: greeting and sanitizer at the front, forms in labelled vertical slots on the left, label printer on the right next to the specimen tray. Each item has a marked outline or labelled slot. The supervisor times a new staff member finding six items: all within 20 seconds.

ItemUse frequencyNew location
Intake formsEvery patientLabelled slots, left of keyboard
Label printerEvery patientRight side, beside specimen tray
Spare tonerMonthlySupply room, shelf C-2
SanitizerEvery patientFront of desk, outlined spot

Common pitfalls

  • Labelling everything before testing the layout. Trial the layout with temporary tape for a week first.
  • Designing locations without the people who do the work.
  • Creating homes that are hard to return items to, so people stop using them.

Key takeaways

  • Every needed item gets a defined, labelled home.
  • Locate by frequency, sequence, ergonomics and safety.
  • Test layouts temporarily with the users before making them permanent.

Apply it at work

Choose one workstation. List its ten most-used items, how often each is used, and where each should live using the frequency and sequence principles. Trial the new layout with tape for one week.

Check your understanding: A team is deciding where to store a heavy fixture used several times per shift. Which location best follows Set in Order principles?

Visual tools for Set in Order: shadow boards, labels and floor marking

Set in Order becomes powerful when locations are visual. A visual location tells you at a glance what belongs there, whether it is present, and how many there should be. This is the start of a visual management system, which you will build out in the next module.

Common visual tools

ToolBest used forWhat it shows
Shadow boardHand tools, gauges, cleaning toolsMissing tool is instantly visible
Labels with photosBins, shelves, drawersWhat belongs, even for new or multilingual staff
Floor markingWalkways, WIP, carts, keep-clear zonesWhere things go and where they must never go
Min/max linesConsumables, suppliesWhen to reorder, when there is too much
Colour codingTools by cell, bins by status, cleaning tools by zoneOwnership or status at a glance

Floor marking conventions

Choose a colour scheme for your site, document it, and use it consistently. A common approach uses yellow for aisles and traffic lanes, white for equipment and fixtures, red for defects or red-tag areas, and red-and-white striped areas for zones that must be kept clear, such as in front of electrical panels and fire equipment. Black and yellow stripes often mark physical hazards. The exact scheme matters less than consistency and training.

Worked example

A maintenance team builds a shadow board for its shared tool wall. Each tool's outline is painted in a contrasting colour and labelled. When a tool is checked out, the borrower hangs a small tag with their name in the outline. At end of shift, the lead glances at the board: two outlines empty, both tagged, one empty and untagged. The untagged spanner is found under a machine guard within minutes, before it can cause a jam or an injury on start-up.

Design tips

  • Make labels readable from where people stand, not only up close.
  • Use photos or icons where language or literacy is a barrier.
  • Use durable materials that survive cleaning, heat or chemicals in that area.
  • Keep it simple; too many colours and signs become noise.
  • Never let floor marking or signs obscure existing safety signage.

Quick check

A visual location is working when someone who has never been in the area can answer three questions within seconds: what goes here, is it here, and how many should there be?

Key takeaways

  • Visual locations show what belongs, whether it is present, and how many.
  • Pick one floor-marking colour scheme, document it and use it consistently.
  • Shadow boards make missing tools visible before they cause problems.

Apply it at work

Design a shadow board or labelled location for one set of shared tools or supplies. Sketch it, include min/max quantities where relevant, and review it with the people who use it.

Check your understanding: What is the main purpose of a shadow board?

Module 2 · Lesson 9 of 22 · 7 min

Layout and motion: spaghetti diagrams and point of use

Set in Order is not only about shelves and labels. The arrangement of the whole area determines how far people walk and how often they search. A simple spaghetti diagram makes that movement visible and gives you a baseline to improve against.

How to draw a spaghetti diagram

  1. Sketch the area to rough scale on paper: benches, machines, racks, doors.
  2. Follow one person doing a normal task or cycle, ideally for 30 to 60 minutes.
  3. Draw a continuous line tracing every movement they make.
  4. Note why each trip happened: fetch a tool, find information, ask a question, get material.
  5. Estimate distance using known dimensions, such as bay widths or floor tiles.

Reading the diagram

Dense knots of lines show where work concentrates. Long repeated lines show where items or information are stored too far away. Lines that cross aisles or forklift lanes show safety exposure as well as waste.

Worked example

A small cabinet shop traces its assembler for one hour. The line shows 14 trips to a shared hardware rack at the far wall and 5 trips to the supervisor's desk to check drawings. Estimated walking: about 600 metres per hour. The team moves the most-used hardware into a labelled bin rack at the assembly bench and posts the current job drawing in a holder at the station. A repeat trace a week later shows 3 trips to the far rack and none to the desk.

MeasureBeforeAfter
Trips to hardware rack per hour143
Trips to supervisor for drawings50
Estimated walking per hour600 m150 m
Crossings of forklift lane81

Point of use, with limits

Point-of-use storage reduces motion, but it can be overdone. If every station holds a full supply of everything, you create inventory waste and cluttered benches. Keep frequently used items at the station in small quantities, and replenish from a central location using a simple signal such as an empty bin or a min line.

Information is an item too

Many trips are for information, not things: the current drawing, the work order, the label spec, the answer to a question. Treat information like a tool and give it a home at the point of use, whether that is a document holder, a screen, or a well-organized shared folder.

The calculator lab after this lesson lets you estimate the yearly cost of searching and walking in your own area.

Key takeaways

  • Spaghetti diagrams make walking and searching visible and measurable.
  • Move high-use items and information to the point of use in small quantities.
  • Reducing trips across traffic lanes reduces both waste and safety exposure.

Apply it at work

Draw a spaghetti diagram for one person in your area for 30 minutes. Note the reason for each trip and identify the two changes that would remove the most walking.

Check your understanding: A spaghetti diagram shows an operator crossing a forklift lane 10 times an hour to reach a supply rack. What is the strongest improvement?

Shine: cleaning as inspection

Shine is the third S, and it is where many programs underperform. Teams treat it as janitorial work. In 5S, Shine means cleaning so that you inspect as you go. The person cleaning a machine, bench or room is the person most likely to notice a leak, a loose bolt, a crack or a missing guard.

Why the operator should clean

  • They know what normal looks, sounds and smells like.
  • They notice early changes that an outside cleaner would not.
  • Ownership of the condition of their area builds pride and accountability.

Cleaning contractors still have a role for floors, washrooms and general areas. But equipment, tools and workstations should be cleaned and checked by the people who use them.

What to look for while cleaning

Look forExamplePossible consequence
LeaksOil under a hydraulic pressSlip hazard, failing seal
Wear and damageFrayed power cord on a grinderShock or fire risk
Loose or missing partsMissing guard fastenerExposure to moving parts
ContaminationDust inside a control cabinetOverheating, faults
Abnormal sounds or heatBearing running hotBreakdown

Tagging abnormalities

Give the team a simple way to flag what they find. Many sites use a coloured tag on the equipment plus an entry in a log or the maintenance system. A tag stays on until the issue is fixed, so problems are visible to everyone. Safety-critical findings, such as a damaged guard, should trigger immediate lockout or removal from service according to your safety procedures, not just a tag.

Worked example

A plastics moulder introduces a five-minute end-of-shift Shine at each press. The operator wipes down the platen area, checks the hydraulic lines, and empties the regrind tray. In the first month, operators tag 11 issues, including two weeping hose fittings and a worn safety-gate switch. Maintenance replaces the fittings before they fail and repairs the switch, which had been intermittently not detecting the gate.

Making Shine routine

  • Break the area into zones and assign each zone an owner.
  • Keep cleaning tools at the point of use, on their own small shadow board.
  • Keep it short: a few minutes daily beats an hour monthly.

Key takeaways

  • Shine means cleaning so you inspect; the user of the equipment is the best inspector.
  • Flag abnormalities with a visible tag and a tracked log entry.
  • Safety-critical findings trigger immediate action under your safety procedures.

Apply it at work

Eliminating the sources of dirt and mess

If an area needs heavy cleaning every day, the real problem is usually a source of contamination that nobody has fixed. Mature 5S teams ask not only "how do we clean this?" but "why does it get dirty, and can we stop it?"

Common sources

  • Leaking fittings, seals and valves.
  • Chips, dust or overspray escaping from a process.
  • Packaging waste created at the wrong place.
  • Spills from poorly designed transfer or dispensing steps.
  • Paper and supplies accumulating because there is no defined flow out of the area.

Use simple root cause tools

The 5 Whys method works well here. Start from the mess and ask why repeatedly until you reach a cause you can act on.

Why?Answer
Why is there coolant on the floor at Lathe 3?It splashes over the chip tray.
Why does it splash over?The splash guard is shorter than on the other lathes.
Why is it shorter?It was replaced with a generic part after damage.
Why a generic part?The correct one was not stocked and the machine was needed.
Why not stocked?Splash guards are not on the critical spares list.

The fix is to install the correct guard and add it to the spares list, not to mop more often. The team also removes a daily slip hazard.

Write cleaning standards

For the cleaning that remains, write a short standard per zone: what to clean, how, with what, how often, and what to check. Keep it to one page, ideally with photos showing the expected condition.

ZoneTaskFrequencyCheck
Packing benchClear offcuts, wipe surfaceEnd of shiftTape gun and knife on board
Floor around benchSweepEnd of shiftNo strapping or film on floor
Label printerClean print headWeeklyLabels scan first time

Hazardous cleaning

Some cleaning involves chemicals, energized equipment or confined spaces. Those tasks need their own procedures, training, PPE and, where applicable, lockout. A 5S cleaning standard should reference those procedures, not replace them.

Key takeaways

  • Recurring mess points to a source that should be fixed, not just cleaned.
  • Use the 5 Whys to trace contamination back to an actionable cause.
  • Write short, zone-based cleaning standards that reference safety procedures where needed.

Apply it at work

Building a visual management system

Visual management is the practice of making the status of work, equipment and the workplace visible at a glance, so that anyone can tell whether things are normal or abnormal without asking or searching. 5S creates the physical foundation; visual management builds on it.

Levels of visual management

LevelWhat it doesExample
Visual indicatorShares informationLocation labels, signs
Visual signalGrabs attention when something changesAndon light, empty-bin signal
Visual controlLimits what can happenMarked WIP space that holds only three carts
Visual guaranteeMakes errors hard or impossibleFixture that only accepts the part in the correct orientation

Aim to move up the levels over time, especially for safety-critical conditions.

What to make visible

  • Workplace condition: labelled locations, shadow boards, floor marking.
  • Equipment condition: gauge green zones, lubrication points, abnormality tags.
  • Material flow: min/max levels, kanban cards, WIP limits.
  • Performance: a team board showing today's plan versus actual, quality issues and actions.
  • Safety: PPE zones, keep-clear areas, days since last incident or open hazard reports.

Worked example: a team board

A distribution centre's pick team sets up a whiteboard near the start-up area. It shows hourly orders planned versus picked, mispicks found at packing, open safety observations, and a short action list with owners and dates. The supervisor runs a 10-minute stand-up at the board at the start of each shift. Within weeks, the team notices that mispicks cluster on one aisle where similar parts sit side by side, and fixes it by separating and relabelling the locations.

Design principles

  • Show the standard and the actual side by side so gaps are obvious.
  • Keep boards close to the work and update them by hand where possible; people pay attention to what they write.
  • Every red mark should lead to an action, an owner and a date.
  • Remove information nobody uses. A cluttered board is a 5S problem too.

Gauge marking: a small example with big value

Marking the normal operating range on a pressure or temperature gauge with a green band lets anyone see in a second whether a reading is normal. Combine it with a rule: if the needle is outside the band, stop and call the lead. That turns a dial into a visual control.

Key takeaways

  • Visual management makes normal versus abnormal obvious without asking.
  • Progress from indicators to signals, controls and guarantees over time.
  • Every abnormal mark on a board needs an action, an owner and a date.

Apply it at work

Pick one process in your area and design a simple visual board showing standard versus actual, issues and actions. Trial it for two weeks with a short daily stand-up.

Standardize: making the good state the normal state

After Sort, Set in Order and Shine, the area is in good shape. Standardize captures that condition so it becomes the expected, repeatable state rather than a one-off achievement. In practice, Standardize turns the first three S's into routines with clear owners.

Standardize tools

ToolPurpose
Area standard photoA posted photo of the area in its correct state, used as the reference
5S checklistShort daily or weekly checks with a named owner
One-point lessonA single-page visual explaining one task or standard
Responsibility mapShows who owns each zone, piece of equipment and task
Site standardsCommon rules for colours, labels and floor marking across all areas

Writing a good 5S checklist

  • Keep it to 8 to 12 items that can be checked in under five minutes.
  • Write each item as a yes or no condition you can see: "All tools on shadow board or tagged out", not "Area is tidy".
  • Include at least two safety items, such as exits clear and spill kit stocked.
  • Note who checks it and how often, and what to do when an item fails.

One-point lessons

A one-point lesson is a single page that teaches one thing: how to clean a nozzle, how to read a gauge band, where to store solvent rags. It uses photos and very few words. Operators often write them, which builds ownership and captures real know-how. Post them where the task happens.

Worked example

An engineering firm's document control area writes a one-page standard with a photo of the correct layout, a seven-item end-of-day checklist and a zone map naming owners. Items include "No drawings left on the plotter tray", "Superseded prints in recycling bin", "Paper stock above min line" and "Cable cover in place across walkway". The checklist rotates among three team members weekly, and the office manager reviews it every Friday.

Standardize across areas

When several areas run 5S, agree site-wide conventions early: one label format, one colour code for floor marking, one tag process for abnormalities. Different standards in every department confuse people who move between areas, and that confusion is itself a safety risk.

A standard is the current best known way. Expect to improve it, and update the document when you do.

Key takeaways

  • Standardize turns Sort, Set in Order and Shine into routines with owners.
  • Good checklist items are visible yes-or-no conditions, including safety checks.
  • Agree site-wide conventions so people moving between areas are not confused.

Apply it at work

Write an 8 to 10 item 5S checklist for one area, with at least two safety items, an owner and a frequency. Use it for two weeks and revise any item that people interpret differently.

Office and digital 5S: shared drives, email and desks

5S works just as well in offices and on screens, and the waste is often larger because it is invisible. Searching a shared drive for the latest version of a quote, digging through an inbox for an approval, or recreating a file that already exists all consume time without anyone noticing.

The five stages applied digitally

StageShared driveEmail
SortArchive or delete duplicates, old drafts and orphaned folders, within your record-retention rulesUnsubscribe from unused lists; archive closed threads

A practical naming convention

A good convention sorts correctly and tells you what a file is without opening it. One common pattern is YYYY-MM-DD_Client_Project_DocumentType_v02. Dates in year-month-day order sort chronologically. Keep one controlled "current" version and move superseded versions to an archive subfolder.

Worked example

A 15-person engineering office has a shared drive with over 40 top-level folders, several named after people who have left. Staff routinely send drawings from the wrong revision.   • Clear desks of paper that is filed or scanned elsewhere.
  • Cable management under desks removes trip hazards.
  • Keep walkways and exits free of boxes and stored files.

Digital Sort is an opportunity to reduce risk. Personal information that you no longer need is a liability under Canadian privacy expectations such as PIPEDA. Follow your retention policy: keep what you must, dispose of what you may, and restrict access to sensitive folders. Do not delete records you are required to keep; when unsure, ask whoever owns your records policy.

Key takeaways

  • Digital clutter causes searching, rework and version errors just like physical clutter.
  • A shared folder structure and a sortable file naming convention are the core of digital Set in Order.
  • Digital Sort must follow record-retention and privacy rules.

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 22 · 7 min

Sustain: why 5S decays and how to stop it

Sustain is the hardest S. Every workplace has a natural pull back toward disorder: rush orders, new staff, staff turnover, a busy season. Without a deliberate system, a well-organized area slides back within weeks. Sustain is that system.

Why 5S decays

  • It was treated as a project with an end date.
  • Nobody owns each zone after the improvement team moves on.
  • Leaders stop looking, so people conclude it no longer matters.
  • Standards are unclear or impractical, so people work around them.
  • Problems found by audits are not fixed, so audits feel pointless.

The building blocks of Sustain

ElementWhat it looks like
OwnershipEvery zone has a named owner on the responsibility map
Routine checksDaily or weekly checklist completed by the owner
AuditsScheduled audits by someone outside the area, scored against the same criteria as the baseline
Leader routinesSupervisors and managers walk the area and ask about the standard
Closed-loop actionsEvery finding has an owner and a date, and is closed
RecognitionProgress and good ideas are noticed and shared

Audit rhythm

A practical rhythm for a small business is a daily owner check, a monthly audit of each area by a peer from another area, and a quarterly management review of scores and trends. Rotating auditors across areas spreads good practice and keeps scoring honest.

Worked example

A food distributor's three warehouse zones started 5S strongly but saw scores slip from around 4 back to 2 after peak season. In the review, the team realises that seasonal staff were never trained on the standards and that the monthly audit had been skipped twice. They add a 15-minute 5S orientation to onboarding, put audit dates in the calendar with a named auditor, and post each zone's score trend on the team board. Scores recover over the following two months.

Safety and Sustain

Decay often shows up first as safety drift: a pallet parked in a keep-clear zone, a guard left off after cleaning, an extinguisher blocked by boxes. Include safety items in every audit and treat a safety failure as a priority finding with a short closing deadline.

Key takeaways

  • Sustain is a deliberate system, because every workplace drifts back toward disorder.
  • Ownership, routine checks, audits, leader routines and closed-loop actions keep 5S alive.
  • Safety drift is often the first sign that Sustain is weakening.

Apply it at work

Map the Sustain elements for one area: who owns it, what is checked daily, who audits monthly, and how findings are closed. Fill any gap you find.

Module 4 · Lesson 16 of 22 · 7 min

Leadership routines and gemba walks

People pay attention to what leaders pay attention to. If managers only ask about output and never about the condition of the workplace, 5S will fade. A short, regular leader routine is one of the most effective Sustain tools available, and it costs very little.

What a gemba walk is

Gemba is a Japanese term often translated as "the real place", meaning where the work happens. A gemba walk is a leader going to the work, observing, and asking questions to understand and support, not to inspect and blame.

A simple 5S leader walk

  1. Pick one area and spend 10 to 15 minutes there.
  2. Look at the posted standard photo and compare it to what you see.
  3. Check the 5S checklist: is it completed, and are failed items acted on?
  4. Look specifically for safety items: exits, keep-clear zones, PPE, guards.
  5. Ask the people working there two or three open questions.
  6. Agree at most one or two follow-ups and record them with owners and dates.

Good questions to ask

  • "What slowed you down today?"
  • "What is hard to keep in its place, and why?"
  • "Is there anything here that makes the job less safe?"
  • "What would you change about this standard?"

Layered audits

Many organizations use a layered approach in which different levels of the organization check the same area at different frequencies.

LayerWhoFrequency
1Area owner or team leadDaily
2SupervisorWeekly
3Manager or ownerMonthly

Worked example

The owner of a 30-person machine shop commits to a 15-minute walk every Tuesday and Thursday morning, rotating through five areas. On one walk, an operator mentions that the shared torque wrench is often missing. Rather than reminding people to return it, the owner approves a second wrench for the other cell and a shadow board slot for each. The operator sees the issue fixed within a week, and suggestions from that cell increase.

Avoid these traps

  • Walking only when something has gone wrong.
  • Pointing out problems without helping remove the obstacles behind them.
  • Making promises on the walk that are never followed up.

Key takeaways

  • Leaders signal what matters by what they regularly look at and ask about.
  • A short gemba walk compares reality with the standard and asks open questions.
  • Following up quickly on what you hear is what makes walks credible.

Apply it at work

Schedule two 15-minute 5S walks per week for the next month. Use the walk steps in this lesson and keep a simple log of follow-ups and closure dates.

Engaging people and managing resistance

5S changes how people work in their own space, and that can feel personal. A machinist's toolbox or a nurse's preferred supply drawer represents years of habit. Engagement is not a nice extra; it decides whether 5S sticks.

Common sources of resistance

What you hearWhat may be behind itHelpful response
"We don't have time for this."Real workload pressureStart small, protect time, show time saved
"We did this before and it died."Past initiative fatigueAcknowledge it; explain what is different this time, especially Sustain
"Don't touch my tools."Loss of control, fear of not having what they needLet users design their own layouts within the standard
"This is just housekeeping."Seen as cosmeticLink 5S to their frustrations, safety and quality

Use a change model

The ADKAR model is a useful checklist for each person affected: Awareness of why the change is needed, Desire to take part, Knowledge of how, Ability to do it in practice, and Reinforcement to keep it going. If someone is stuck, identify which element is missing. Someone who agrees with 5S but keeps reverting may lack the Ability, for example because the new location is impractical, or the Reinforcement, because nobody notices either way.

Practical engagement tactics

  • Start in an area with a willing team and visible pain points.
  • Let the people who do the work make the Sort and Set in Order decisions.
  • Show before-and-after photos and measured results, such as reduced walking.
  • Recognize contributions publicly and specifically.
  • Include safety representatives and frame 5S as protecting people.
  • Give each team a small budget for labels, boards and bins so they can act quickly.

Worked example

In a clinic, nurses resist moving supplies into a standard layout because each has a personal system. The manager asks two nurses to lead a trial in one exam room, with input from colleagues. They keep the items people use most in the same spot in every room. After a month, nurses covering other rooms report they no longer search for supplies. The other rooms adopt the layout at the nurses' own request.

Resistance often contains useful information. Listen for the practical problem inside the complaint, then fix it.

Key takeaways

  • Resistance to 5S is often about control and workload, and often contains useful information.
  • ADKAR helps pinpoint what a person is missing: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability or reinforcement.
  • Let the people who do the work design their own areas within the site standard.

Apply it at work

Identify one person or team likely to resist 5S in your area. Use ADKAR to guess which element is missing and plan one specific action to address it.

Measuring 5S: scores, trends and business results

What gets measured gets attention, but measuring the wrong thing can push people to game the system. A good 5S measurement approach combines audit scores with a few operational and safety measures that show whether 5S is making work better.

Three layers of measures

LayerExamplesPurpose
Condition5S audit score per area, checklist completion rateIs the standard being maintained?
OperationalSearch time, walking distance, changeover time, mispicksIs 5S reducing waste?
SafetyOpen hazard reports, near-miss reports, findings closed on timeIs the workplace getting safer?

Scoring well

  • Use the same criteria as your baseline so trends are meaningful.
  • Score each dimension, not only an overall total, so you can see where to focus.
  • Track trends over months rather than reacting to a single score.
  • Count overdue findings as a measure in their own right.

Worked example

A fabrication shop audits four areas monthly on a 1 to 5 scale for each of seven dimensions, giving a maximum of 35. The welding bay scores 18 at baseline, 24 after three months and 27 after six. Over the same period, the team measures average time to start a new job on the welding cell. They take five samples before and after, and find setup time has dropped from about 25 minutes to about 15, mostly from no longer searching for clamps and consumables. Two long-standing trip hazards were removed and have not reappeared in audits.

The five stages are the same everywhere, but what they look like changes with the setting. Adapting the method to the work, rather than copying a factory template, is what makes 5S succeed outside manufacturing.

Health care specifics

In clinics, Sort includes checking expiry dates and following infection prevention rules for what can be kept in patient areas. Connecting 5S to Lean, TPM and continuous improvement

5S is often called the foundation of Lean because many other methods depend on a stable, visible workplace. It is hard to run standard work, quick changeovers or kanban in an area where tools and materials are never in the same place twice.

How 5S supports other methods

MethodWhat it needs from 5S
Standard workFixed locations so the sequence of steps is repeatable
SMED (quick changeover)Tools, fixtures and information staged and ready before the changeover
Kanban and pullLabelled locations with clear min/max quantities
TPM (Total Productive Maintenance)Operator cleaning and inspection, which is Shine
Error proofingA visible standard so abnormal conditions stand out
KaizenA visible workplace where problems surface quickly

Use PDCA for each 5S change

Treat 5S changes as small experiments using Plan-Do-Check-Act:

  1. Plan: define the problem, such as 20 minutes a shift lost looking for gauges, and the proposed change.
  2. Do: try it in one place, using temporary labels or tape.
  3. Check: measure the result and ask users what works and what does not.
  4. Act: make it standard if it worked, adjust if partly, or try something else.

Worked example

A packaging line changes over between product sizes four times a week, each taking about 70 minutes. A changeover study shows much of the time goes to finding change parts and the right setting sheet. The team uses 5S to create a labelled, colour-coded cart for each product size with all change parts and the setting sheet, staged before the line stops. In the first month, changeovers average about 45 minutes. The cart becomes the foundation for further SMED work on the internal steps.

Where 5S fits in a roadmap

  • Start with 5S in a pilot area to build habits and credibility.
  • Add visual management and daily stand-ups as the area stabilizes.
  • Introduce standard work and changeover reduction once locations are stable.
  • Use the improved stability to tackle quality and flow problems with tools such as DMAIC.

5S does not need to be perfect before other methods start, but a basic level of order is a prerequisite for most of them.

Key takeaways

  • Standard work, SMED, kanban and TPM all depend on a stable, organized workplace.
  • Treat each 5S change as a small PDCA experiment.
  • Use 5S early in an improvement roadmap to build habits and credibility.

Apply it at work

Pick one recurring task, such as a changeover or a job setup, and list every item and piece of information it needs. Plan a PDCA trial to stage them in advance.

Warning signs to watch

  • Items stored "temporarily" in red-tag or keep-clear areas.

Worked example

Picture a small manufacturer that hires a contractor to install shadow boards, signs and floor marking across the plant in two weeks. It looks impressive.   1. Can a newcomer find any common item in about 30 seconds?
  2. Can anyone see at a glance whether the area is in its normal state?
  3. Are audit findings being closed on time?

If the answer to any is no, you know which part of the system to strengthen.
