Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 16 · 6 min

What ISO 9001 is and why SMEs adopt it

ISO 9001 is the international standard for quality management systems (QMS). It sets out requirements for how an organization plans, runs, checks and improves its work so it consistently meets customer and regulatory requirements and improves over time. It applies to any organization, of any size, in any sector, whether it makes products or provides services.

Why SMEs adopt it

  • Customer requirements: many manufacturers, distributors, government buyers and large companies require or prefer ISO 9001-certified suppliers.
  • Consistency: clear processes reduce errors, rework and dependence on a few experienced people.
  • Growth: a documented, working system makes it easier to train new staff, add shifts or sites, and win larger contracts.
  • Improvement: audits, measures and corrective action create a habit of finding and fixing problems.

Certification versus conformity

An organization can build its QMS to ISO 9001 without being certified. Certification means an independent certification body (also called a registrar) has audited the system and confirmed it meets the standard. Certification is what customers usually ask for.

A useful system, not paperwork

ISO 9001 has a reputation for binders of procedures nobody reads. The current standard is far more flexible: it asks for documented information where it is needed, and focuses on results, risk and the process approach. A good QMS describes how the business already works well, fixes the gaps and is used every day. Built that way, it pays for itself.

The current edition is ISO 9001:2015, amended in 2024 to ask organizations to consider whether climate change is relevant to their context. A revised edition is being developed; check which edition applies and any transition period when you plan certification. This course covers the concepts that carry across editions. It is an introduction, not a substitute for the standard itself, which you can buy from ISO or national standards bodies.

Key takeaways

  • ISO 9001 sets requirements for a quality management system for any organization.
  • Certification is an independent audit against the standard, often required by customers.
  • Build a useful system that reflects how you work, not paperwork.

List the customers or tenders that have asked for ISO 9001, and the three quality problems that cost your business the most today.

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 16 · 7 min

The seven quality management principles

ISO 9001 is built on seven quality management principles, described in the companion standard ISO 9000. They explain the thinking behind the requirements.

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Customer focusUnderstand and meet customer needs, and aim to exceed expectations
LeadershipLeaders set direction and create conditions for people to achieve quality objectives
Engagement of peopleCompetent, empowered people at all levels contribute to quality
Process approachManage activities as connected processes that produce consistent results
ImprovementContinual improvement is an ongoing objective
Evidence-based decision makingDecisions are based on data and analysis
Relationship managementManage relationships with suppliers and partners for mutual benefit

Why they matter

When a requirement seems unclear, the principles explain its purpose. For example, the requirement to monitor customer satisfaction follows from customer focus; the requirement for management review follows from leadership and evidence-based decisions.

Familiar ideas

The principles overlap closely with Lean, Six Sigma and operational excellence: customer focus, process thinking, data-driven decisions and continual improvement. A company already practising these has a head start on ISO 9001, and ISO 9001 gives those practices a structure that holds them in place.

Key takeaways

  • Seven principles underpin ISO 9001: customer focus, leadership, engagement, process approach, improvement, evidence-based decisions, relationship management.
  • The principles explain the purpose behind requirements.
  • They overlap closely with Lean and operational excellence.

Apply it at work

Rate your business from 1 to 5 on each of the seven principles and note one example of each in practice today.

How the standard is structured: PDCA and the process approach

ISO 9001 follows a common structure used by many ISO management system standards, which makes it easier to combine with others such as ISO 14001 (environment) and ISO 45001 (health and safety).

ClauseTopicPDCA
4Context of the organizationPlan
5LeadershipPlan
6Planning: risks, opportunities, objectivesPlan
7Support: resources, competence, communication, documentsDo
8Operation: customer requirements, design, purchasing, production, release, nonconformityDo
9Performance evaluation: monitoring, internal audit, management reviewCheck
10Improvement: nonconformity, corrective action, continual improvementAct

Clauses 1 to 3 cover scope, references and terms; the requirements start at clause 4.

Plan-Do-Check-Act

PDCA is a cycle for managing any process: plan what you will do and how, do it, check the results against the plan, and act to improve. ISO 9001 applies it to the whole system and to each process.

The process approach

Rather than managing departments in isolation, identify the processes that deliver value to customers, such as quote to order, design, purchasing, production, delivery and service, and how they connect. For each process, define inputs, outputs, owner, resources, controls, measures and risks. A simple process map showing the main processes and their interactions is a useful starting point for any QMS.

Risk-based thinking

Throughout the standard, organizations are asked to consider what could go wrong or right and to act in proportion. This replaces the old idea of a separate "preventive action" procedure. The lab after this lesson asks you to place everyday activities in the PDCA cycle.

Key takeaways

  • Clauses 4 to 10 contain the requirements and follow Plan-Do-Check-Act.
  • Manage connected processes with owners, inputs, outputs, controls and measures.
  • Apply risk-based thinking throughout, in proportion to the risk.

Apply it at work

Draw a one-page map of your main processes from customer enquiry to delivery and service, showing how they connect. Name an owner for each.

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 16 · 7 min

Context, interested parties and scope

Clause 4 asks the organization to understand its situation before designing its QMS.

Context

Identify the internal and external issues relevant to your purpose and ability to achieve quality results. Internal issues include your capabilities, people, culture and systems; external issues include markets, competition, regulation, technology, economic conditions and, since the 2024 amendment, whether climate change is relevant. A SWOT analysis is a common, practical way to record them.

Interested parties

List the interested parties relevant to the QMS, such as customers, regulators, suppliers, employees, owners and the community, and their relevant requirements. A simple table works well:

Interested partyRelevant requirements
CustomersProduct specifications, on-time delivery, certificates of conformity, responsiveness
RegulatorsProduct safety, labelling, pressure equipment or food safety rules as applicable
EmployeesClear instructions, training, safe workplace
SuppliersClear purchase orders and specifications, fair payment

Scope

Define the boundaries of the QMS: which products, services, sites and activities it covers. The scope appears on the certificate, so word it carefully, for example "Design, fabrication and installation of stainless steel process piping systems at the Burnaby facility". If a requirement cannot apply to you, for example design and development for a company that only makes to customer drawings, state it and justify why in the scope.

Keep it current

Review context and interested parties at least yearly, usually in management review. They feed the risks and objectives in the next clauses.

Key takeaways

  • Record internal and external issues, including whether climate change is relevant.
  • List interested parties and their relevant requirements.
  • Define and justify the scope, which appears on the certificate.

Apply it at work

Write a draft scope statement for your QMS and a table of five interested parties with their relevant requirements.

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 16 · 7 min

Leadership, quality policy and objectives

ISO 9001 places clear responsibility on top management. Quality cannot be delegated entirely to a quality manager.

What top management must do

  • Take accountability for the effectiveness of the QMS.
  • Set a quality policy and quality objectives that fit the business strategy.
  • Integrate QMS requirements into business processes rather than running them in parallel.
  • Provide resources, communicate the importance of quality and support people.
  • Promote the process approach, risk-based thinking, customer focus and improvement.
  • Assign roles, responsibilities and authorities.

The quality policy

A short statement of the organization's commitment to quality: meeting requirements, customer focus and continual improvement, in words that fit the business. It should be communicated, understood and available. Avoid generic wording that could belong to any company; staff should recognize their own business in it.

Quality objectives

Objectives turn the policy into measurable targets. Good objectives are specific, measurable, owned and time-bound, with a plan for achieving them:

ObjectiveMeasureTargetOwner
Improve on-time deliveryOrder lines delivered by promised date95% by year-endOperations manager
Reduce customer complaintsComplaints per 1,000 ordersBelow 3 by Q4Quality lead
Reduce internal scrapScrap cost as % of material costBelow 2%Production supervisor

Review progress at management review and use the same measures in regular management meetings, so quality objectives are part of running the business, not a separate exercise.

Key takeaways

  • Top management is accountable for the QMS and must integrate it into the business.
  • Write a short, specific quality policy staff recognize.
  • Set measurable, owned quality objectives with plans and review them regularly.

Apply it at work

Draft a quality policy of no more than five sentences and three measurable quality objectives with owners and targets.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 16 · 7 min

Risks and opportunities

Clause 6.1 asks the organization to determine the risks and opportunities that could affect its ability to deliver conforming products and services and to satisfy customers, and to plan actions in proportion.

A practical approach

  1. Start from context and interested parties (clause 4) and your process map.
  2. For each main process, ask what could go wrong (risks) and what could be done better (opportunities).
  3. Rate each risk for likelihood and impact on a simple scale, such as 1 to 5.
  4. Decide actions for the higher risks: prevent, reduce, transfer, monitor or accept.
  5. Assign owners and dates, and check that actions worked.

Examples

ProcessRisk or opportunityAction
PurchasingSingle supplier for a critical componentQualify a second source
ProductionOnly one person can set up the key machineCross-train two operators; write setup standard work
Quote to orderCustomer specifications missed at order entryContract review checklist
SalesOpportunity: certification opens a new market segmentTarget three prospects in that segment

Keep it proportionate

ISO 9001 does not require a formal risk management method or a risk register, though many SMEs find a simple register the easiest way to show their thinking. Focus on risks that matter to quality and customers; a long list of trivial risks adds paperwork without value. The lab after this lesson asks you to place risks on a likelihood and impact grid.

Key takeaways

  • Identify risks and opportunities by process, starting from context.
  • Rate likelihood and impact and act in proportion.
  • A simple register is enough; focus on risks that matter to quality and customers.

Apply it at work

For your three main processes, list two risks and one opportunity each, rate the risks and choose actions for the highest two.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 16 · 7 min

Support: resources, competence, awareness and communication

Clause 7 covers what people and processes need to work well.

Resources

  • People and infrastructure: enough staff, suitable buildings, equipment, IT and transport.
  • Work environment: conditions needed for quality, such as cleanliness, temperature or lighting where they matter.
  • Monitoring and measuring resources: gauges and instruments fit for purpose and, where measurement traceability is required, calibrated against recognized standards, identified with their status and protected from damage.
  • Organizational knowledge: knowledge needed to run processes, captured so it is not lost when people leave.

Competence

Determine the competence needed for each role that affects quality, ensure people are competent through education, training or experience, act to close gaps and keep evidence. A simple training matrix showing roles, required skills and who is qualified is a common and practical tool.

Awareness

People should know the quality policy, the objectives relevant to them, how their work contributes, and the consequences of not following requirements. A quick test: ask an operator what quality means in their job and what to do if something goes wrong.

Communication

Decide what to communicate about the QMS, when, with whom and how: team huddles, notice boards, toolbox talks, newsletters, customer and supplier communication.

Key takeaways

  • Provide people, infrastructure, environment and calibrated measuring equipment.
  • Define competence by role, close gaps and keep evidence, for example in a training matrix.
  • Make sure people know the policy, their objectives and their contribution.

Apply it at work

Build a simple training matrix for one department: roles across the top, key skills down the side, and who is qualified for each.

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 16 · 7 min

Documented information: keep it lean

Clause 7.5 covers documented information: the documents you maintain to describe how things are done, and the records you retain as evidence of what was done.

What the standard requires

ISO 9001 explicitly requires some documented information, including:

  • The scope of the QMS.
  • The quality policy and quality objectives.
  • Records such as evidence of competence, calibration, design and development where applicable, supplier evaluation, product release, nonconformities and corrective actions, internal audit results and management review results.

Beyond these, the organization decides what is needed for effective processes. A quality manual is no longer mandatory, though some organizations keep a short one as an overview.

Decide what to document

Document a process when its absence could cause errors, when tasks are infrequent or complex, when several people do it, or when customers or regulations require it. Keep documents short, visual and at the point of use: flowcharts, checklists, photos and short work instructions beat long narrative procedures.

Control

  • Documents are reviewed and approved before use and when changed.
  • Current versions are available where needed; obsolete versions are removed or marked.
  • Records are legible, identifiable, protected and kept for a defined period.

Cloud document systems with version control, approval workflows and access rights make this much easier than paper. Many SMEs run their entire QMS in shared drives or simple QMS software.

Key takeaways

  • Some documented information is required; the rest depends on what processes need.
  • Prefer short, visual documents at the point of use.
  • Control approval, versions, availability and record retention.

Apply it at work

List the documents your business uses to run its main processes. Mark any that are outdated, duplicated or never used, and any process that needs one but has none.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 16 · 7 min

Customer requirements and operational planning

Clause 8 covers operations: how products and services are planned, made and delivered. It starts with understanding what the customer needs.

Operational planning

For each product or service, plan the requirements, the processes, acceptance criteria, resources and records needed, and control planned changes. For many SMEs this means a clear workflow from order to delivery, with checkpoints where quality is confirmed.

Customer communication

Provide information about products and services, handle enquiries, orders and changes, obtain customer feedback including complaints, handle customer property and set contingency actions where relevant.

Review before you commit

Before accepting an order, review that you can meet its requirements: stated requirements (specifications, quantities, delivery dates), requirements the customer didn't state but are necessary, legal and regulatory requirements, and your own requirements. Resolve differences before accepting. Keep a record of the review and of any changes.

A simple order review checklist prevents many problems:

  • Specification and drawing revision confirmed?
  • Materials, certifications and testing requirements understood?
  • Delivery date feasible with current capacity?
  • Special packaging, labelling or documentation?
  • Price and terms agreed?

Changes

When a customer changes an order, update all affected documents and tell everyone involved. Unclear or uncommunicated changes are a common cause of nonconformities.

Key takeaways

  • Plan operations with requirements, processes, acceptance criteria and records.
  • Review orders before accepting them and resolve differences.
  • Control changes and tell everyone affected.

Apply it at work

Draft a one-page order review checklist for your business and test it on your next five orders.

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 16 · 7 min

Design and development, and external providers

Design and development (clause 8.3)

If you design products or services, the standard asks for a controlled design process:

  • Planning: stages, responsibilities, reviews, verification and validation.
  • Inputs: functional and performance requirements, standards, regulations, lessons from previous designs.
  • Controls: reviews at key stages, verification that outputs meet inputs, validation that the result works for its intended use.
  • Outputs: drawings, specifications, instructions and acceptance criteria.
  • Changes: reviewed, controlled and recorded.

Companies that build only to customer designs can usually exclude this clause, with justification.

Control of externally provided processes, products and services (clause 8.4)

Suppliers and subcontractors affect your quality directly. The standard asks you to:

  1. Set criteria to evaluate, select, monitor and re-evaluate external providers, based on their ability to supply to requirements.
  2. Decide the controls needed, such as incoming inspection, certificates of conformity or audits, in proportion to the risk.
  3. Communicate requirements clearly on purchase orders: specifications, quantities, approvals, competence and verification activities.
  4. Keep records of evaluations.

A simple supplier programme

StepExample
Approved supplier listCritical suppliers approved by questionnaire, sample or audit
Performance monitoringOn-time delivery and rejection rate reviewed quarterly
FeedbackShare results; raise corrective action requests for repeated problems
Re-evaluationYearly review of critical suppliers

Key takeaways

  • If you design, control planning, inputs, reviews, verification, validation and changes.
  • Evaluate, select and monitor suppliers in proportion to their impact on quality.
  • Put clear requirements on purchase orders and keep evaluation records.

Apply it at work

List your ten most important suppliers, the criteria you would use to approve them and how you will monitor their performance.

Module 3 · Lesson 11 of 16 · 7 min

Production, traceability and release

Clause 8.5 and 8.6 cover how products are made or services delivered under controlled conditions, and how they are released.

Controlled conditions

  • Documented information describing the product and the activities, such as drawings, specifications and work instructions.
  • Suitable equipment and monitoring and measuring resources.
  • Monitoring and measurement at appropriate stages.
  • Competent people.
  • Validation of processes whose results can't be fully verified afterwards, such as welding, heat treatment or sterilization.
  • Actions to prevent human error, such as checklists and mistake-proofing.

Identification and traceability

Identify outputs and their inspection status throughout the process, for example with labels, tags or system status. Where traceability is required by customers or regulations, control and record the unique identification of outputs, such as lot or serial numbers, so you can trace materials and processes and recall if needed.

Customer property and preservation

Take care of property belonging to customers or suppliers, such as customer-supplied materials, tooling or data. Preserve products through handling, storage, packaging and delivery.

Release

Products and services are released only when planned checks show requirements are met, unless a relevant authority or the customer approves otherwise. Keep records showing conformity and who authorized release.

Key takeaways

  • Produce under controlled conditions with instructions, suitable equipment, competent people and checks.
  • Identify status and maintain traceability where required.
  • Release only after planned checks, with records of conformity and authorization.

Apply it at work

Walk one product or service through your process and note where its status is identified, what checks are done and who authorizes release.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 of 16 · 8 min

Nonconformities and corrective action

Problems happen. What matters is controlling them and stopping them from recurring.

Control of nonconforming outputs (clause 8.7)

When a product or service doesn't meet requirements, identify and control it so it isn't used or delivered by mistake. Options include correction (rework or repair), segregation, return to supplier, informing the customer, obtaining a concession or scrapping. Record the nonconformity, the action taken and who decided.

Corrective action (clause 10.2)

For significant or recurring nonconformities, including customer complaints and audit findings:

  1. React: control and correct the immediate problem and deal with consequences.
  2. Find the root cause: use tools such as 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram. Check whether similar problems exist or could occur elsewhere.
  3. Act on the cause: implement actions that remove the root cause, not just the symptom.
  4. Verify effectiveness: check later that the problem hasn't recurred.
  5. Update the system: change risks, documents, training or processes as needed.

Correction versus corrective action

CorrectionCorrective action
PurposeFix this instancePrevent recurrence
ExampleRework the mislabelled cartonsAdd a label check at packing and fix the template that caused the error

"Retrain the operator" is rarely a sufficient corrective action on its own. Ask what in the process made the error possible. The Lean Six Sigma Foundations course covers root cause analysis in depth.

Key takeaways

  • Identify and control nonconforming outputs so they aren't used by mistake.
  • Corrective action finds and removes the root cause, then verifies effectiveness.
  • Correction fixes this instance; corrective action prevents recurrence.

Apply it at work

Take one recent customer complaint and work through the five corrective action steps. Write down how you will verify the action worked.

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 16 · 7 min

Monitoring, measurement and customer satisfaction

Clause 9.1 asks what you need to monitor and measure, how, when, and when results are analyzed and evaluated.

Typical QMS measures

AreaMeasures
CustomersOn-time delivery, complaints, returns, satisfaction ratings
Products and processesFirst-pass yield, scrap, rework, process capability
SuppliersOn-time delivery, rejection rate
QMSObjectives progress, corrective actions open and overdue, audit findings

Customer satisfaction

Monitor customers' perception of how well their needs and expectations are met. Methods range from simple to detailed: complaint and compliment trends, short surveys after delivery, periodic customer interviews, warranty claims, repeat orders and lost-customer reviews. Choose methods that suit your customers and act on what you learn.

Analysis and evaluation

Data is only useful if it is analyzed. Review trends, compare with objectives and targets, look for patterns using tools such as Pareto charts, and use results to decide where to improve. Keep analysis results as input to management review.

Use the same data to run the business

The best QMS measures are the ones managers already use. If quality measures sit in a separate report reviewed only before audits, the QMS is not integrated. Put the key quality measures on the management dashboard.

Key takeaways

  • Decide what to measure, how, when and when to analyze.
  • Monitor customer satisfaction with methods that suit your customers.
  • Analyze trends and put quality measures on the management dashboard.

Apply it at work

List the five quality measures that matter most to your customers and check whether they appear on your management dashboard.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 16 · 8 min

Internal audit and management review

Internal audit (clause 9.2)

Internal audits check whether the QMS conforms to your own requirements and ISO 9001, and whether it is effectively implemented and maintained.

  • Plan an audit programme covering all processes and clauses, usually over a year, with more frequent audits of important or problem areas.
  • Define the criteria and scope of each audit.
  • Use auditors who are objective and impartial: they should not audit their own work.
  • Audit by following processes: talk to people, observe the work and sample records.
  • Report findings to the relevant managers and track corrections and corrective actions.

Small companies can train two or three staff as internal auditors who audit each other's areas, or use an outside auditor for some audits.

Management review (clause 9.3)

Top management reviews the QMS at planned intervals, at least once a year and often quarterly. Inputs include:

  • Status of actions from previous reviews.
  • Changes in context and interested parties.
  • Performance: customer satisfaction, objectives, process performance, nonconformities and corrective actions, monitoring results, audit results, supplier performance.
  • Adequacy of resources.
  • Effectiveness of actions on risks and opportunities.
  • Opportunities for improvement.

Outputs are decisions and actions on improvement, changes to the QMS and resource needs, recorded with owners. Many SMEs combine management review with a regular leadership or business review meeting, using a standing agenda, which keeps it practical rather than a once-a-year formality.

Key takeaways

  • Plan internal audits of all processes with impartial auditors and follow up findings.
  • Hold management reviews at planned intervals with the required inputs.
  • Record decisions and actions; combine review with regular leadership meetings.

Apply it at work

Draft a 12-month internal audit plan covering your main processes, and a standing management review agenda with the inputs listed.

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 16 · 7 min

Continual improvement

Clause 10 asks the organization to determine and select opportunities for improvement and to continually improve the suitability, adequacy and effectiveness of the QMS.

Where improvement ideas come from

  • Analysis of data: trends in complaints, scrap, delivery.
  • Internal audits and management reviews.
  • Customer feedback and complaints.
  • Risks and opportunities.
  • Staff suggestions.
  • Supplier feedback and benchmarking.

Improvement tools

ISO 9001 doesn't prescribe methods. Common choices:

ApproachFits
PDCA cyclesSmall, everyday improvements
Kaizen eventsFocused improvement of one area in a few days
Lean tools: 5S, standard work, flowWaste and lead time
Six Sigma DMAICComplex, recurring defects with unclear causes

Keep an improvement list

Track improvement projects with owners, status and results. Review the list at management review. Celebrate successes and share them, so staff see that raising problems leads to change.

ISO 9001 and Lean Six Sigma together

ISO 9001 provides the system: defined processes, measures, audits and reviews. Lean and Six Sigma provide the methods to improve. Together they create a cycle where the QMS reveals problems and improvement methods solve them, with standards and controls holding the gains.

Key takeaways

  • Draw improvement ideas from data, audits, customers, risks and staff.
  • Use PDCA, kaizen, Lean and Six Sigma as fits the problem.
  • Track improvements, review them and share successes.

Apply it at work

Start an improvement list with five ideas from recent complaints, scrap data or staff suggestions. Assign owners and target dates.

Module 4 · Lesson 16 of 16 · 8 min

Getting certified

Certification typically takes 6 to 12 months for an SME starting with basic processes in place, depending on size, complexity and how much staff time is available.

A typical path

PhaseActivities
Commit and planLeadership commitment, project lead, scope, budget, timeline
Gap assessmentCompare current practice with ISO 9001 requirements, clause by clause
Build the QMSProcess map, policy, objectives, risks, documents, records, training matrix, supplier controls
Run itUse the system for at least a few months to generate records: corrective actions, measures, supplier reviews
Internal audit and management reviewComplete a full internal audit cycle and at least one management review
Certification auditStage 1 (documentation and readiness) and Stage 2 (implementation and effectiveness)

Choosing a certification body

Choose an accredited certification body. In Canada, the Standards Council of Canada accredits certification bodies; others are accredited by members of the International Accreditation Forum. Customers generally expect accredited certification. Compare quotes on audit days, auditor experience in your industry, scheduling and travel costs.

After certification

Certificates are typically valid for three years, with surveillance audits at least yearly and a recertification audit before the certificate expires. The system must keep working between audits. Organizations that only prepare just before audits struggle; those that use the QMS to run the business find audits routine.

Costs

Costs include the certification body's audit fees, any consultant support, staff time, training, and possibly QMS software and calibration. Staff time is often the largest cost. Some government programmes support certification projects; check the Funding page and confirm eligibility with each programme.

Key takeaways

  • Certification usually takes 6 to 12 months: plan, gap assess, build, run, audit, certify.
  • Choose an accredited certification body; in Canada, SCC accredits certification bodies.
  • Certificates last three years with yearly surveillance; keep the system working between audits.
