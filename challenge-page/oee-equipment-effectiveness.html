Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 20 · 6 min

What OEE measures and why it matters

Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) answers one question: of the time a machine or line was scheduled to run, what share produced good product at full speed? It multiplies three factors:

OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality

FactorQuestionLosses it captures
AvailabilityWas it running when it was scheduled to?Breakdowns, changeovers, waiting for material or operators
PerformanceWhen running, was it running at its ideal speed?Minor stops, slow cycles, reduced speed
QualityWas what it made good the first time?Scrap, rework, start-up rejects

There is a simpler equivalent: OEE = (good units × ideal cycle time) ÷ planned production time. It is the share of scheduled time that would have been needed to make the good output at full speed, with no stops. An OEE of 60% means 40% of scheduled time was lost somewhere.

Why it matters

Many plants believe they are capacity-constrained and plan to buy another machine or add a shift. OEE often shows a large hidden capacity inside the existing equipment: time lost to stops, slow running and scrap. Recovering part of it can deliver more output for far less money than new equipment, and it reveals exactly where the losses are.

Where to use it

OEE suits repetitive equipment with a definable ideal rate: packaging lines, CNC machines, presses, moulding, filling, bottling, extrusion and similar. It is most valuable on the bottleneck, the equipment that limits the whole plant's output, because every hour gained there is an hour gained for the business. The Insights article What OEE really measures covers the concept in more depth.

Key takeaways

  • OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality.
  • It shows the share of scheduled time that produced good output at full speed.
  • Use it first on the bottleneck, where gains become extra output.

Apply it at work

List the three pieces of equipment in your operation that most often limit output. Which one would you measure OEE on first, and why?

Check your understanding: A line has OEE of 65%. What does this mean?

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 20 · 7 min

Availability: is it running when it should be?

Availability compares the time the equipment actually ran with the time it was scheduled to run.

Availability = run time ÷ planned production time

Planned production time is the shift time minus planned breaks and periods when no production was scheduled, such as no orders or planned maintenance (more on what to exclude in a later lesson). Run time is planned production time minus stop time.

What counts as a stop

Availability losses are stops long enough to be recorded with a reason. Many plants use a threshold, such as five minutes, with shorter stops counted as minor stops under performance. Typical availability losses:

  • Breakdowns and equipment failures.
  • Changeovers and setups between products.
  • Waiting for materials, operators, quality approval or maintenance.
  • Upstream starving or downstream blocking, if you count them here; decide once and be consistent.

Example

An eight-hour shift has a 30-minute meal break, so planned production time is 450 minutes. The line stopped for a 25-minute changeover and a 20-minute jam. Run time is 450 − 45 = 405 minutes. Availability = 405 ÷ 450 = 90%.

Changeovers belong in OEE

Some plants leave changeovers out of OEE because "they are planned". Do not. A changeover is time the equipment could have produced. Counting it keeps changeover reduction visible, and changeover time is often one of the largest and most fixable losses.

Key takeaways

  • Availability = run time ÷ planned production time.
  • Set a threshold between recorded stops and minor stops, and keep it consistent.
  • Count changeovers as availability losses so they stay visible.

Apply it at work

For one shift on your bottleneck, list each stop over five minutes with its duration and reason. Calculate availability.

Check your understanding: Planned production time is 400 minutes and the machine stopped for 60 minutes. What is availability?

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 20 · 7 min

Performance: is it running at full speed?

Performance compares what the equipment actually produced in its run time with what it could have produced at its ideal cycle time.

Performance = (ideal cycle time × total units) ÷ run time

Total units include good and bad. The ideal cycle time is the fastest the equipment can reliably make one unit of that product under good conditions, from the equipment design rating or the best sustained rate you have demonstrated. Do not use the average or the "standard" rate that already includes losses; that hides the performance gap you want to see.

What performance losses look like

  • Minor stops: brief jams, misfeeds, sensor trips and blocked chutes, often under five minutes and rarely recorded. They add up quickly.
  • Reduced speed: running below the ideal rate because of material problems, worn equipment, operator caution or habit.

Example

Continuing the example: the line ran 405 minutes. The ideal cycle time is 0.5 minutes per unit (120 units an hour), and it made 700 units. Performance = (0.5 × 700) ÷ 405 = 350 ÷ 405 ≈ 86.4%. About 55 minutes of run time were lost to minor stops and slow running that nobody logged.

A check on your numbers

Performance above 100% means the ideal cycle time is set too slow. Fix it rather than capping the result. If products have different ideal rates, calculate the ideal time for each product's units and add them up.

Key takeaways

  • Performance = ideal cycle time × total units ÷ run time.
  • Use a true ideal rate, not a standard that already includes losses.
  • Minor stops and slow running are often large and unrecorded.

Apply it at work

Find the ideal cycle time for your bottleneck's most common product, from the design rating or the best sustained hour. Compare it with the rate used in your plans.

Check your understanding: A machine ran for 300 minutes with an ideal cycle time of 0.25 minutes and made 1,000 units. What is performance?

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 20 · 6 min

Quality: good the first time

Quality compares the good units with all the units produced.

Quality = good units ÷ total units

Good means good the first time: units that passed without rework. Reworked units count as bad for OEE even if they are eventually shipped, because the equipment's time to make them the first time was wasted and the rework uses more time and labour.

Quality losses

  • Start-up rejects: scrap made while warming up, after a changeover or after a stop, until the process stabilizes.
  • Production rejects: scrap and rework during stable running.

Start-up losses are often treated as normal and never measured. On lines with frequent changeovers they can be a large share of all scrap.

Completing the example

Of the 700 units made, 672 were good first time. Quality = 672 ÷ 700 = 96%.

OEE = 90% × 86.4% × 96% ≈ 74.6%. Check with the simple formula: good units × ideal cycle time ÷ planned production time = 672 × 0.5 ÷ 450 = 336 ÷ 450 ≈ 74.7%. The small difference is rounding.

Notice how three factors that each look respectable multiply into an OEE well below any of them. That is why OEE is useful: it shows the combined effect of all losses in one number. Use the lab after this lesson to calculate OEE from one shift of your own data.

Key takeaways

  • Quality = good-first-time units ÷ total units.
  • Reworked units count as bad for OEE.
  • Three good-looking factors multiply into a much lower OEE.

Apply it at work

Count good-first-time, reworked and scrapped units on your bottleneck for one shift, including start-up scrap. Calculate quality and then OEE.

Check your understanding: A line made 1,000 units: 920 good first time, 50 reworked and shipped, 30 scrapped. What is quality for OEE?

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 20 · 7 min

The six big losses

The total productive maintenance (TPM) tradition groups equipment losses into six big losses, two for each OEE factor. Classifying every loss into one of the six makes Pareto analysis and improvement focus straightforward.

OEE factorBig lossExamples
AvailabilityEquipment failure (breakdowns)Motor failure, broken tooling, hydraulic leak
Setup and adjustmentsChangeovers, tool changes, warm-up, first-piece approval
PerformanceIdling and minor stopsJams, misfeeds, sensor trips, blocked product
Reduced speedRunning below ideal rate because of wear, material or habit
QualityStart-up rejectsScrap until the process stabilizes after start-up or changeover
Production rejectsScrap and rework during stable production

Turn time into a loss waterfall

Express every loss in minutes of equipment time. Start with planned production time, subtract each loss in turn and finish with fully productive time: the time it would take to make the good units at ideal speed. A waterfall chart of the six losses shows where the time goes at a glance and makes the biggest loss obvious.

Quality losses can be converted to time as rejected units × ideal cycle time; performance losses as run time minus (total units × ideal cycle time).

The lab after this lesson asks you to classify common losses. Getting the categories right matters: a jam logged as a breakdown sends maintenance after the wrong problem.

Key takeaways

  • The six big losses split each OEE factor into two causes.
  • Express all losses in minutes to compare them.
  • A loss waterfall makes the biggest loss obvious.

Apply it at work

Take one week of your bottleneck's losses and sort them into the six big losses in minutes. Which loss is largest?

Check your understanding: Scrap produced while the line stabilizes after a changeover is which big loss?

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 20 · 7 min

Planned time, utilization and TEEP

OEE measures how well equipment uses the time it was scheduled to run. It does not tell you how much of the calendar is scheduled. Two more measures complete the picture.

MeasureCalculationAnswers
Utilization (loading)Planned production time ÷ all calendar timeHow much of the week do we schedule the equipment?
OEEAvailability × Performance × QualityHow well do we use the scheduled time?
TEEPOEE × UtilizationHow much of all calendar time produces good units at full speed?

Example

A line runs two eight-hour shifts, five days a week, with breaks excluded: about 75 planned hours out of 168 in a week, a utilization of about 45%. With OEE of 70%, TEEP is about 31%. The line has two very different sources of extra capacity: improve OEE within the current schedule, or schedule more time.

What to exclude from planned time

Decide once and write it down. Common exclusions are planned breaks, no orders, planned preventive maintenance windows and planned shutdowns. Everything else, including changeovers, waiting for material and unplanned maintenance, stays in planned time as a loss. Be wary of definitions that quietly exclude awkward losses: they raise OEE on paper while hiding the problem.

Which measure for which decision

Use OEE to drive daily improvement on the shop floor. Use TEEP and utilization when deciding whether to add shifts, buy equipment or outsource: those decisions are about total capacity, not just how well scheduled time is used.

Key takeaways

  • Utilization is scheduled time over calendar time; TEEP = OEE × utilization.
  • Write down what is excluded from planned time and keep it consistent.
  • Use OEE for daily improvement and TEEP for capacity decisions.

Apply it at work

Calculate your bottleneck's utilization for last month: planned production hours divided by calendar hours. Combine it with OEE to estimate TEEP.

Check your understanding: A machine has OEE of 80% and is scheduled 50% of calendar time. What is its TEEP?

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 20 · 7 min

Benchmarks and common pitfalls

An OEE of 85 percent is often quoted as "world class", usually broken down as 90 percent availability, 95 percent performance and 99.9 percent quality. Treat it as a rough reference, not a target for every machine. Many plants that measure OEE honestly for the first time find it well below that, and that is normal.

Compare equipment with itself

OEE depends on product mix, batch sizes, how many changeovers the schedule requires and how ideal cycle times and planned time are defined. A filling line making one product for a week and a CNC cell making forty different parts will never have comparable OEE. The useful comparison is each piece of equipment against its own baseline over time.

Pitfalls that make OEE misleading

  • Generous ideal cycle times: using a slow "standard" rate makes performance look high and hides speed losses.
  • Excluding losses from planned time: calling changeovers or material shortages "planned" inflates availability.
  • Counting rework as good: hides quality losses.
  • Averaging OEE across machines: a simple average gives a slow machine the same weight as the bottleneck. Weight by time, or focus on the bottleneck.
  • Using OEE to judge operators: people will protect themselves by bending definitions. OEE should point at losses and causes, not at people.
  • Chasing OEE on non-bottlenecks: raising OEE on equipment with spare capacity may just build inventory.

Honesty first

A lower but honest OEE that shows where time is lost is far more valuable than a high number nobody trusts. When you introduce OEE, explain that the first figure will probably look low and that the point is to find and remove losses.

Key takeaways

  • Treat 85% as a rough reference, not a universal target.
  • Compare each machine with its own history.
  • Avoid generous ideal rates, hidden exclusions and using OEE to blame people.

Apply it at work

Write down your OEE definitions: ideal cycle time source, planned time exclusions, stop threshold and how rework is counted. Share them with the team before you start measuring.

Check your understanding: Why is comparing the OEE of a high-mix CNC cell with a single-product filling line misleading?

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 20 · 8 min

What lost time is worth

To decide which losses to attack and how much to spend, convert lost time into money. The answer depends on one question: is this equipment a bottleneck?

On a bottleneck with sellable demand

If the equipment limits output and customers would buy more, an hour recovered becomes an hour of extra sales. Its value is the contribution margin of the extra units: selling price minus the variable costs of making them (materials, energy, consumables, variable labour and freight). Do not use full revenue, and do not use the full-cost margin that includes fixed overhead already paid for. The Insights article What OEE losses cost works through this in detail.

Example: a bottleneck makes 120 units an hour at ideal rate, and each extra unit sold brings $2.50 of contribution margin. One hour recovered is worth up to 120 × $2.50 = $300. Recovering 400 hours a year is worth up to $120,000 a year, but only if the extra units are sold.

On a non-bottleneck, or without extra demand

Extra output that cannot be sold is just inventory. Here the value of better OEE comes from cost avoidance:

  • Fewer overtime hours or a shift no longer needed.
  • A machine purchase that can be deferred or avoided.
  • Less scrap: material, energy and disposal costs.
  • Less expediting and fewer late deliveries.

Scrap is valued differently

A scrapped unit loses its materials and processing cost even on a non-bottleneck. On a bottleneck it also loses the margin of the unit that could have been made in its place. Count both where they apply, but never count the same benefit twice.

Key takeaways

  • Recovered bottleneck time is worth the contribution margin of extra units sold.
  • On non-bottlenecks the value is cost avoidance: overtime, capital, scrap.
  • Do not use full revenue, and do not double count.

Apply it at work

Calculate the contribution margin per hour of your bottleneck at ideal rate. Ask sales whether extra output could be sold, and which customers or products would take it.

Check your understanding: A non-bottleneck machine's OEE rises from 60% to 70%, but sales cannot use more output. Where does the value come from?

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 20 · 7 min

Starting to collect OEE data

You do not need sensors or software to start measuring OEE. A well-designed paper or tablet form, filled in during the shift, is enough for the first months and teaches the team what matters.

What to record each shift

DataWho records itNotes
Shift start and end, breaks, no-order timeOperator or supervisorGives planned production time
Each stop over the threshold: start, end, reason codeOperatorGives availability losses by reason
Product run and its ideal cycle timePre-printed or from the scheduleGives performance
Total units and good-first-time unitsOperator or counterGives quality and performance
Comments on unusual eventsOperatorContext for the daily review

Design the form with operators

Operators know which stops happen and what to call them. Involve them in designing the form and the reason codes, keep it to one page, and make it quick: tick boxes and times, not paragraphs. A form that takes more than a few minutes per shift will be filled in at the end of the shift from memory.

Start on one machine

Begin with the bottleneck. Run the form for two to four weeks before drawing conclusions, so the data covers different products, shifts and conditions. Expect the first week's data to be patchy; review it daily with the operators, fix confusing codes and thank people for recording problems honestly.

Hourly counts reveal hidden losses

Adding a simple hourly output count, written on a board next to the target for that hour, is one of the most effective additions. It shows when performance drops and prompts the question "what happened in that hour?" while the answer is still fresh.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a simple shift form; sensors can come later.
  • Design the form and reason codes with operators.
  • Hourly counts against target expose hidden performance losses.

Apply it at work

Draft a one-page OEE shift form for your bottleneck with the five data groups above. Ask two operators to review it before you start.

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 20 · 7 min

Downtime reason codes and Pareto analysis

OEE tells you how much time is lost. Reason codes tell you why. Good codes turn a pile of stop records into a Pareto chart that points improvement at the biggest causes.

Designing reason codes

  • Two levels: a category (breakdown, changeover, material, quality, minor stop, other) and a specific reason within it (for example breakdown → conveyor motor, sealing jaw, vision system).
  • Short list: 10 to 25 codes per machine is usually enough. Too many and people pick the first one; too few and the data says nothing.
  • Clear definitions with examples, so two operators would code the same stop the same way.
  • Limit "other": if "other" exceeds about 10 percent of stop time, review the comments and add codes.

From codes to action

After two to four weeks, sum stop minutes by code and sort them. Typically a handful of reasons account for most lost time. Then drill down: for the top reason, look at when it happens (product, shift, time since changeover) and ask why using 5 Whys or a fishbone.

ReasonMinutes in 4 weeksShare
Changeover1,86036%
Labeller jams1,12022%
Waiting for materials78015%
Sealing jaw failures64012%
All others76015%

In this example the first two reasons account for 58 percent of lost time, so they become the first improvement projects. Count frequency as well as minutes: many short stops of one type may matter more to operators than one long one, and have different causes.

Key takeaways

  • Use two-level reason codes with clear definitions.
  • Keep 'other' small and review it.
  • Pareto by minutes and by frequency, then drill into the top reasons.

Apply it at work

Draft two-level reason codes for your bottleneck: five categories and up to five reasons in each. Write a one-line definition for each reason.

Module 3 · Lesson 11 of 20 · 7 min

Automating data capture

Once manual tracking has shown where the losses are and the team trusts the definitions, automatic data capture can make OEE more accurate and less work.

Options from simple to advanced

OptionWhat it capturesConsiderations
Counter or sensor on the outputUnits made, running or stopped, cycle timesCheap; shows minor stops that manual logs miss
Signals from the machine controller (PLC)Run state, fault codes, counts, speedsNeeds controls expertise; fault codes rarely match business reasons one to one
OEE or monitoring software with an operator tabletAutomatic states and counts; operators add reasons for stopsThe usual sweet spot for SMEs; many subscription products exist
Manufacturing execution system (MES)OEE plus scheduling, quality, traceability and labourLarger investment; justify on more than OEE alone

Automation catches minor stops

The biggest surprise when automatic capture starts is usually minor stops. Stops of 20 seconds to two minutes are almost never logged by hand but can add up to hours a shift. Seeing them by time and product is often the first big improvement opportunity.

People still provide the why

Sensors know that the machine stopped, not why. Keep operators adding reason codes for stops above the threshold, with a quick touchscreen list. Plan for network access, cybersecurity on connected machines and who maintains the system.

Before buying

Prove the value with manual data first, define what you will do with the information, and pilot on one line. A dashboard nobody acts on is just another screen.

Key takeaways

  • Start manual; automate once definitions are trusted.
  • Automatic capture reveals minor stops that manual logs miss.
  • Operators still provide stop reasons; plan for network and cybersecurity.

Apply it at work

List what manual OEE tracking has not shown you clearly. Decide whether a simple counter or monitoring software would close that gap, and what you would do with the data.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 of 20 · 7 min

Visual boards and shift reviews

Data only helps if people look at it and act. Simple routines around a visual board turn OEE into daily improvement.

The production board

Place a board at the line showing, for each hour: target output, actual output, the gap and the reason for any gap. Add the shift's OEE, the top three losses and open actions with owners. Anyone walking past can see in seconds whether the line is on track and what is being done.

A tiered daily review

TierWhoWhen and how longFocus
1Operators and team leadStart of shift, 10 minutesYesterday's results, top loss, today's plan, safety
2Supervisors, maintenance, quality, planningDaily, 15 minutesIssues across lines, support needed, escalations
3Plant manager and leadsWeekly, 30 minutesTrends, bigger projects, resources

Rules that make it work

  • Stand at the board; keep to time.
  • Every significant loss gets an owner and a date, or is escalated to the next tier.
  • Review actions from the last meeting first.
  • Celebrate improvements and thank people for raising problems.

The aim is a fast loop: a loss happens, it is visible within hours, a cause is found and a fix is tried within days. Monthly OEE reports alone are too slow; by the time they are read, the details are forgotten.

Key takeaways

  • Hourly target-versus-actual boards make gaps visible at once.
  • Run short tiered reviews: shift, daily, weekly.
  • Every significant loss gets an owner and date or is escalated.

Apply it at work

Sketch a production board for your bottleneck and an agenda for a 10-minute start-of-shift review. Run it for one week and note what changes.

Check your understanding: What is the main weakness of relying on a monthly OEE report alone?

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 20 · 6 min

Choosing where to improve first

With a few weeks of data, pick improvement targets deliberately. Three questions guide the choice.

  1. Which equipment? Start with the bottleneck, where recovered time becomes extra output. If there is no clear bottleneck, choose the equipment with the most overtime, late orders or scrap cost.
  2. Which loss? Use the six-loss waterfall and reason-code Pareto. Pick the biggest loss that the team can realistically influence.
  3. Which method? Match the method to the loss.
Biggest lossMethods that fit
BreakdownsAutonomous maintenance, preventive maintenance, root cause analysis of repeat failures
ChangeoversSMED, standard changeover work, scheduling to reduce changeovers
Minor stopsObservation, 5 Whys on frequent stops, cleaning and adjustment standards
Reduced speedFind what limits the rate: material, wear, settings, operator confidence
Start-up rejectsStandard start-up settings, pre-heating, first-piece approval
Production rejectsDMAIC problem solving, process parameter control

Small teams, short cycles

Form a small team of operators, maintenance and an engineer or supervisor for each focused improvement. Give it one loss, a measurable target and four to eight weeks. Results on one loss build the credibility to tackle the next.

Waiting for materials or operators is an OEE loss whose cause usually sits outside the machine, in scheduling, supply or staffing. Raise it through the daily review rather than asking maintenance to fix it.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the bottleneck and its biggest controllable loss.
  • Match the method to the type of loss.
  • Use small cross-functional teams with one loss and a target.

Apply it at work

Using your loss data, choose one equipment, one loss and one method. Write a one-line target, such as 'cut changeover time on line 2 from 60 to 35 minutes in eight weeks'.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 20 · 8 min

Reducing breakdowns: autonomous and preventive maintenance

Breakdowns are rarely random. Most have early signs: loose bolts, leaks, wear, dirt, heat, noise and vibration. Total productive maintenance (TPM) aims to catch these signs early and remove their causes, with operators and maintenance working together.

Autonomous maintenance

Operators take on basic care of their equipment: cleaning, inspecting, lubricating and tightening. Cleaning is inspection: wiping down a machine reveals cracks, leaks and loose parts before they cause failures. Typical steps:

  1. Initial clean and inspection, tagging every abnormality.
  2. Eliminate sources of contamination and hard-to-clean areas.
  3. Set clean, inspect and lubricate standards with simple checklists and visual aids.
  4. Train operators to understand the equipment and spot problems.

Maintenance technicians then focus on skilled work instead of routine checks.

Planned and preventive maintenance

Planned maintenance schedules work before failure: time-based (every 500 hours), usage-based (every 100,000 cycles) or condition-based (when vibration, temperature or wear crosses a limit). Use your breakdown history to set priorities: components that fail repeatedly need either a better maintenance task or a design change.

Learn from every significant failure

For each breakdown over a set duration, record the failed component and ask why it failed, not only how it was repaired. Track mean time between failures (MTBF) and mean time to repair (MTTR) for the bottleneck. Rising MTBF shows prevention is working; falling MTTR shows better spares, tools and procedures.

Maintenance and operating work on equipment must follow your lockout and safety procedures; autonomous maintenance tasks should be limited to work operators are trained and authorized to do safely.

Key takeaways

  • Most breakdowns have early warning signs that daily care can catch.
  • Operators clean, inspect and lubricate; maintenance focuses on skilled work.
  • Analyze significant failures and track MTBF and MTTR.

Apply it at work

Run a 30-minute clean-and-inspect on your bottleneck with an operator and a technician. Tag every abnormality and decide who fixes each.

Check your understanding: What does rising mean time between failures (MTBF) on the bottleneck indicate?

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 20 · 8 min

Quick changeover with SMED

Changeovers are often the largest availability loss on high-mix equipment. SMED (single-minute exchange of die), developed by Shigeo Shingo, is a structured way to cut them. The goal of "single minute" means under ten minutes; even halving a long changeover is a large gain.

The SMED steps

  1. Observe and record: film a complete changeover and list every task with its time, from the last good part of the old product to the first good part at full speed of the new one.
  2. Separate internal and external tasks: internal tasks can only be done with the machine stopped; external tasks can be done while it runs.
  3. Move external tasks out: fetch tools, dies, materials and paperwork, pre-heat, and pre-set before the machine stops, using a checklist and a changeover cart.
  4. Convert internal to external: pre-assemble parts, use standard heights so tools need no shimming, pre-set settings off-line.
  5. Streamline what remains: quick clamps instead of bolts, one-turn fasteners, eliminate adjustments with fixed stops, work in parallel with two people.
  6. Standardize: write the new method as standard work and train every shift.

Example

A packaging line's 70-minute changeover was filmed. Twenty-five minutes went to fetching parts and tools, finding the right settings and waiting for first-article approval. A changeover cart, a settings sheet per product and a quicker approval routine cut it to 40 minutes, with no capital spending. Quick clamps on the forming section later brought it to 28 minutes.

Use the time well

Shorter changeovers can be taken as extra running time or as more frequent changeovers with smaller batches, which cuts inventory and improves responsiveness. Decide which matters more for this equipment.

Key takeaways

  • SMED separates internal from external setup tasks and moves external ones out.
  • Convert internal tasks to external, then streamline what remains.
  • Use the saved time for more output or for smaller batches.

Apply it at work

Film one changeover on your bottleneck. List the tasks, mark each internal or external and identify three tasks that could be done before the machine stops.

Module 4 · Lesson 16 of 20 · 7 min

Minor stops, speed losses and start-up scrap

Performance and quality losses are often the least visible and the most persistent. They respond to careful observation and small, specific fixes.

Minor stops

Minor stops are short and frequent: a carton jams, a sensor misreads, product tips over on a conveyor. Each takes seconds to clear, so nobody logs it, but dozens a shift can cost an hour. To attack them:

  • Stand at the machine for an hour and tally every stop by location and type.
  • Pick the most frequent and ask why five times: worn guide rail, dirty sensor, inconsistent material, wrong setting.
  • Fix the cause and set a cleaning or adjustment standard so it stays fixed.

Reduced speed

Ask why the equipment is not run at its ideal rate. Common answers: it jams at higher speed, a particular material runs badly, the operator was told years ago to slow it down, or a worn component limits it. Test speed increases in small steps, with maintenance and quality present, and fix what breaks at each step.

Start-up and changeover scrap

Track scrap for the first period after each start-up or changeover separately. Standard start-up settings per product, pre-heating, checked materials and a clear first-piece approval routine often cut it sharply.

Production rejects

Scrap and rework during stable running are process problems. Use the DMAIC method from the Lean Six Sigma Foundations course: measure, find the verified causes, fix and control.

Key takeaways

  • Observe and tally minor stops; fix the most frequent cause.
  • Find out why equipment runs below ideal rate and test increases carefully.
  • Standardize start-up settings to cut start-up scrap.

Apply it at work

Stand at your bottleneck for one hour and tally every stop under five minutes by type and location. Pick the most frequent and ask why five times.

Module 5 · Lesson 17 of 20 · 7 min

Valuing an OEE improvement

Before investing in an OEE programme or a specific fix, estimate what it is worth. The method from the earlier lesson on lost time applies to the gain.

Extra output on a bottleneck

Extra good units per year = (target OEE − current OEE) × planned production hours × ideal rate per hour.

Example: a bottleneck runs 4,000 planned hours a year with an ideal rate of 120 units an hour. Raising OEE from 55% to 65% gives 0.10 × 4,000 × 120 = 48,000 extra good units a year. At $2.50 contribution margin per extra unit sold, that is up to $120,000 a year, if the market will take the extra units.

Capacity freed for the same output

If demand is fixed, higher OEE means the same output in fewer hours. Hours needed at the new OEE = current hours × current OEE ÷ target OEE. Here, 4,000 × 55 ÷ 65 ≈ 3,385 hours, so about 615 hours a year are freed. Their value depends on what you do with them: cut overtime, drop a weekend shift, delay a new machine or take on new work.

Other benefits

  • Lower scrap: material, energy and disposal savings.
  • Less expediting, better on-time delivery and fewer penalties.
  • Lower maintenance cost per unit as breakdowns fall.

Be conservative

Count only benefits the business will actually realize, say how each is calculated, and ask finance to agree the method. A believable estimate of $80,000 beats an inflated $300,000 that nobody trusts. The lab after the next lesson calculates extra units, freed hours, value and payback.

Key takeaways

  • Extra units = OEE gain × planned hours × ideal rate.
  • With fixed demand, value the hours freed through overtime, shifts or capital avoided.
  • Count only realizable benefits and agree the method with finance.

Apply it at work

Estimate the extra good units a year from a 5-point OEE gain on your bottleneck. Then estimate the hours freed if demand stays the same, and what you would do with them.

Check your understanding: A bottleneck runs 3,000 hours a year at an ideal rate of 100 units an hour. OEE rises from 60% to 70%. How many extra good units a year is that?

Building the business case and payback

Some OEE improvements cost almost nothing: changeover carts, settings sheets, cleaning standards. Others need money: quick-release tooling, new sensors, a monitoring system, a rebuild of worn equipment or a maintenance contractor. For those, build a short business case.

What the business case contains

SectionContent
ProblemCurrent OEE, the targeted loss in hours, and its effect on output, overtime or delivery
ProposalThe change, its cost and timeline
BenefitsExpected OEE gain and its value, with the method agreed with finance
Payback and returnOne-time cost ÷ monthly benefit; simple return over three years
Risks and assumptionsDemand to sell extra output, staffing, installation downtime
MeasurementHow and when the result will be checked

Payback example

A $40,000 changeover upgrade on a bottleneck is expected to lift OEE from 55% to 65%, worth $120,000 a year in contribution margin. Payback is $40,000 ÷ ($120,000 ÷ 12) = 4 months. Even at half the expected benefit, payback is 8 months, which makes the decision robust. The Insights article OEE project payback walks through a larger worked example.

Funding

Larger productivity projects may qualify for government programmes or financing. Check the Funding page on this site for programmes that support productivity and technology adoption, and confirm eligibility directly with each programme before relying on it.

Close the loop

After implementation, measure the OEE gain and the realized benefit against the business case. Sharing honest results, good or bad, makes the next business case more credible.

Key takeaways

  • Build a short business case for improvements that need money.
  • Payback = one-time cost ÷ monthly benefit; test it at half the expected benefit.
  • Measure results against the case after implementation.

Apply it at work

Write a one-page business case for one OEE improvement on your bottleneck, using the six sections above. Calculate payback at the expected and at half the expected benefit.

Module 5 · Lesson 19 of 20 · 7 min

Sustaining gains: standards, TPM and daily management

OEE gains fade if nothing holds them: settings drift, cleaning standards slip, new operators learn shortcuts and a key person leaves. Sustaining needs structure.

Standards

  • Standard work for operation, changeover, start-up and cleaning, with photos, posted at the machine.
  • Settings sheets for each product, kept current and controlled.
  • Clean, inspect and lubricate checklists with owners and frequencies.

Routines

  • Daily tiered reviews of OEE and the top losses, as covered earlier.
  • Weekly review of open actions and the loss Pareto.
  • Monthly review of trends, MTBF and MTTR and the improvement pipeline.
  • Regular audits of standards, with findings closed out.

Skills

Cross-train operators on changeovers and basic care. Train team leads to run the daily review and basic problem solving. Make sure maintenance knowledge is written down, not held by one person.

Watch the definitions

When OEE suddenly improves, check that nothing changed in how it is calculated: a new ideal rate, a new exclusion from planned time or a change in what counts as good. Keep a log of definition changes with dates.

Spread what works

Once the bottleneck is stable at a better level, a new bottleneck may appear elsewhere. Apply the same approach there, reusing the forms, codes and standards that worked.

Key takeaways

  • Hold gains with standards for operation, changeover, start-up and cleaning.
  • Keep the daily, weekly and monthly review routines running.
  • Log definition changes so OEE improvements are real.

Apply it at work

List the standards and routines that would need to exist for your bottleneck's gains to last a year. Mark which already exist and which you need to create.

Your 90-day OEE programme

A focused 90-day programme on one bottleneck is a practical way to prove OEE's value and build the habits to spread it.

DaysFocusMain actions
1–30Define and measureChoose the bottleneck; agree definitions (ideal rate, planned time, stop threshold, good units); design the shift form and reason codes with operators; collect data daily; start an hourly board
31–60Analyze and improveBuild the six-loss waterfall and reason Pareto; pick the biggest loss; run a focused improvement (SMED, clean-and-inspect or minor-stop attack); start tier 1 and 2 daily reviews
61–90Sustain and valueWrite standards for the improvements; measure the OEE gain and its value; agree it with finance; plan the next loss and the next machine

Measures to report

  • Weekly OEE with availability, performance and quality.
  • Minutes lost by top reason.
  • Actions opened and closed.
  • Output, overtime and scrap cost on the bottleneck.

Signs it is working

Operators raise problems and suggest fixes, maintenance plans more work and fights fewer fires, the daily review is short and practical, and the biggest loss at day 90 is different from the one at day 30.

Related courses

Key takeaways

  • Run a 90-day programme on one bottleneck: measure, improve, sustain.
  • Report OEE factors, top losses, actions and money measures weekly.
  • Success shows in behaviour as well as in the number.
