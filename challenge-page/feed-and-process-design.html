Course overview

About this course

A practical introduction to front-end engineering design (FEED) for owners, project managers and engineers of process plants in chemicals, energy, food, materials and similar industries. You will learn the project stage gates, the design basis, mass and energy balances, PFDs, equipment sizing, P&IDs, control philosophy and Canadian codes and approvals, then process hazard analysis, relief, layers of protection, cost estimate classes, schedule and the FEED package that supports the investment decision. The course is educational and does not replace qualified engineering or the codes and regulations that apply to a project.

Who it is for

  • Owners and managers planning a new plant, expansion or major modification
  • Project managers and engineers joining a FEED or front-end team
  • Process, mechanical and instrumentation engineers early in their careers
  • Operations and maintenance leaders who review designs before construction

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the stage-gate path and what FEED decides before the investment decision
  • Set out a design basis and read mass and energy balances, PFDs and stream tables
  • Read P&IDs, instrument tags, control philosophies and cause-and-effect charts
  • Identify Canadian codes, standards and approvals that shape process plant design
  • Describe HAZID, HAZOP, relief design and layers of protection
  • Interpret estimate classes and contingency, and judge a FEED package at the gate review

Course structure

Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 20 · 7 min

From concept to construction: the project stages

Process plant projects move through a series of stages, each ending in a gate: a decision to continue, change course or stop. Each stage spends more money and fixes more decisions. The stage names vary between companies, but the logic is the same.

StageMain questionTypical outputs
Concept (identify)Is there an opportunity worth studying?Process idea, rough capacity, order-of-magnitude cost
Feasibility / pre-FEED (select)Which option should we develop?Options compared, selected technology and site, preliminary balances
FEED (define)What exactly will we build, for how much, and when?Design basis, PFDs, P&IDs, equipment datasheets, hazard reviews, cost estimate for investment decision
Detailed engineering (execute)How is every part built and installed?Construction drawings, specifications, procurement
Construction and commissioningIs it built correctly and does it work?Mechanical completion, commissioning, start-up, handover

Why the early stages matter most

The ability to influence cost and performance is highest at the start, when changes are made on paper, and falls quickly once equipment is ordered and steel is up. The cost of a change rises at the same time: moving a line on a P&ID during FEED takes an hour; moving it after the pipe is installed takes cutting, welding, re-testing and schedule delay. Front-end work is cheap insurance against expensive surprises later.

About this course

This course explains how front-end engineering works so owners, project managers and engineers can plan, review and use FEED well. It is educational. It does not replace a qualified engineering team or the codes, standards and regulators that apply to your project. The Insights article From concept to construction covers the stages in more depth.

Key takeaways

  • Projects pass through stage gates: concept, select, define (FEED), execute, operate.
  • Influence over cost is highest early; the cost of change rises later.
  • Front-end work is cheap insurance against expensive changes in construction.

Apply it at work

Place a project you know on the stage-gate path. Which gate has it passed, and which decisions were made, or skipped, at each one?

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 20 · 7 min

What FEED is and why it pays

Front-end engineering design (FEED), sometimes called basic engineering or the define stage, develops the selected process into a design complete enough to estimate cost reliably, plan the schedule, identify the main risks and support the final investment decision (FID).

What FEED fixes and what it leaves for later

Fixed in FEEDLeft for detailed engineering
Process design: balances, PFDs, operating conditionsIsometric piping drawings
P&IDs at a reviewed, near-final levelStructural and foundation design details
Equipment sizes, materials and process datasheetsVendor detailed drawings and fabrication
Plot plan and major layoutCable schedules and wiring details
Control philosophy and safety systems conceptControl system configuration and programming
Hazard reviews (HAZID, HAZOP of the P&IDs)Detailed constructability and installation sequences
Cost estimate and schedule for FIDConstruction work packages

Why it pays

  • Better estimates: FEED typically moves the cost estimate to an accuracy suitable for funding decisions, which lenders and boards expect.
  • Fewer late changes: decisions are made while they are cheap to change.
  • Earlier procurement: long-lead equipment can be specified and sometimes ordered.
  • Safer design: hazards are found and designed out on paper.
  • Competitive bids: a complete FEED package lets contractors bid on the same scope.

Cutting FEED short to save time or money is one of the most common reasons projects overrun later. The Insights article What a FEED package contains lists the documents in detail.

Key takeaways

  • FEED develops the selected design enough for a reliable estimate and the investment decision.
  • It fixes the process, P&IDs, equipment and layout; details come later.
  • A short-changed FEED is a common cause of overruns.

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 20 · 7 min

Roles, contracts and the owner's job

Several parties contribute to FEED, and the contract structure shapes how they work together.

PartyRole
OwnerDefines what is needed, makes decisions, approves the design, funds the project and will operate the plant
Engineering contractorPerforms FEED engineering across disciplines: process, mechanical, piping, electrical, instrumentation, civil
Technology licensorProvides the process design package for licensed technology, with performance guarantees
Owner's engineerIndependent engineering support that reviews and represents the owner's interests
VendorsProvide equipment data, quotes and drawings for key equipment
Regulators and authoritiesApprove permits, pressure equipment registration and other requirements

Common contracting models after FEED

  • EPC (engineering, procurement and construction): one contractor delivers the plant, often lump sum. The owner gets cost certainty but pays for the contractor's risk, and the FEED must be solid enough to bid on.
  • EPCM (engineering, procurement and construction management): the contractor engineers and manages on the owner's behalf; the owner holds construction contracts and more cost risk, with more control.
  • Owner-managed: common for smaller projects; the owner coordinates engineering firms and contractors directly.

The owner's job in FEED

FEED is not something to hand over and collect at the end. The owner must provide the design basis inputs, answer questions quickly, make decisions on options, involve operations and maintenance staff in reviews, and approve key documents. Slow owner decisions are a common cause of FEED delays. Name one owner's representative with authority to decide, and agree a weekly decision log with the engineering team.

Key takeaways

  • Owner, engineers, licensors, vendors and regulators each play a role.
  • EPC, EPCM and owner-managed models allocate risk and control differently.
  • The owner must decide promptly and involve operations throughout FEED.

Apply it at work

For a project you know, name who filled each role in the table. Which role was missing or unclear, and what effect did it have?

The design basis: decisions to fix first

The design basis is the document that records the assumptions and requirements every engineer designs to. Agreeing it at the start of FEED prevents rework later, because a change in capacity or feed composition ripples through every calculation and drawing.

What a design basis covers

TopicExamples of decisions
Capacity and turndownDesign throughput, operating hours per year, minimum stable rate
FeedstocksComposition ranges, impurities, supply conditions
Products and specificationsPurity, quality limits, packaging or delivery form
Site conditionsElevation, temperature extremes, seismic zone, snow and wind loads, soil
UtilitiesAvailable power, steam, cooling water, instrument air, nitrogen and their conditions
Design marginsMargins on flow, heat duty and pressure; how they are applied
Codes, standards and owner specificationsWhich editions apply; owner preferences for equipment and materials
Environmental limitsAir emissions, effluent, noise, waste handling
Battery limitsExactly where the project's scope starts and ends
Operating philosophyContinuous or batch, staffing, automation level, maintenance approach, sparing

Margins: agree them once

If each discipline adds its own safety margin, the plant ends up oversized and expensive. Agree in the design basis where margins apply and how large they are, and record the reason. Oversized equipment is not always safer: pumps and control valves that run far below their design point can operate poorly.

Keep it controlled

Issue the design basis with a revision number, have the owner sign it off and route any change through a formal change process with an impact check on cost and schedule. The Insights article The design basis: ten decisions goes through each in more detail.

Key takeaways

  • The design basis records the assumptions every engineer designs to.
  • Agree design margins once, centrally, with reasons.
  • Control the design basis with revisions, sign-off and change management.

Apply it at work

Draft the headings of a design basis for a project you know. Mark which decisions were firm at the start of FEED and which were still open.

Check your understanding: Why should design margins be agreed centrally in the design basis?

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 20 · 8 min

Mass and energy balances

Every process design starts with the mass and energy balance: what goes in, what comes out and what happens in between, stream by stream. It is the foundation for sizing every piece of equipment and every utility.

The principles

  • Mass balance: at steady state, mass in = mass out for every unit and for the whole plant. For reacting systems, balance each element or use reaction stoichiometry and conversion.
  • Energy balance: heat in + work in = heat out + work out + change in enthalpy. It gives heating and cooling duties, which size exchangers and utilities.

Where the data come from

For established processes, data come from licensors, literature and process simulators. For new or scaled-up processes, they come from pilot plant and laboratory work: conversions, selectivities, yields, residence times, heats of reaction and physical properties. Gaps in data are a major FEED risk. Identify them early and decide whether to fill them with more testing or carry them as a design margin.

The stream table

The balance is presented as a stream table: for each numbered stream, its flow, composition, temperature, pressure and phase. Stream numbers link the table to the process flow diagram.

Stream1 Feed2 Reactor outlet3 Product4 Recycle
Mass flow (kg/h)1,0001,400950400
Temperature (°C)251804060
Pressure (barg)310111

Check the balance closes: here feed (1,000 kg/h) equals product (950) plus a 50 kg/h purge not shown, and the reactor outlet equals feed plus recycle. A balance that does not close points to an error or a missing stream.

Cases, not just one point

Design for more than the normal case: maximum and minimum throughput, start-up, different feed compositions and summer and winter conditions. Equipment is sized to the governing case for each item. The Insights article From pilot data to a mass and energy balance works through an example.

Key takeaways

  • Mass and energy balances are the foundation for sizing everything.
  • Pilot and lab data fill gaps for new or scaled-up processes; gaps are a major risk.
  • Check the balance closes, and design for several operating cases.

Apply it at work

Take a simple process you know and sketch its main streams. Write a stream table with mass flows and check that inputs equal outputs.

Check your understanding: A balance does not close: inputs exceed outputs by 5%. What does this most likely mean?

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 20 · 7 min

The process flow diagram

The process flow diagram (PFD) shows how the process works: the major equipment, the main process streams and how they connect, the key operating conditions and the main control loops. It is the bridge between the balance and the detailed design.

What a PFD shows, and what it leaves out

ShownNot shown
Major equipment with tag numbers and key data (duty, size, design conditions)Every valve, drain, vent and small line
Main process streams with numbers linked to the stream tablePipe sizes and line numbers
Main utility connections (steam, cooling water)Every instrument
Key control loops (flow, level, temperature, pressure)Safety relief devices in detail
Operating temperatures and pressures at key pointsSpare equipment details

Reading a PFD

Follow the main stream from feed to product. At each piece of equipment, ask what it does to the stream: heat it, react it, separate it, pump it. Note recycles and purges, which often control how impurities build up. Look at the control loops: what is being controlled and what is manipulated.

Why the PFD matters in FEED

The PFD is where process options are compared and fixed: one reactor or two in series, which separation method, how heat is recovered between streams. Changes are easy at this stage. Once P&IDs and equipment datasheets are developed from the PFD, changes become slower and costlier, so review the PFD carefully with operations and the owner before moving on. The Insights article How to read a PFD and a P&ID has worked drawings.

Key takeaways

  • A PFD shows major equipment, main streams, conditions and key controls.
  • Stream numbers link the PFD to the stream table.
  • Fix process options on the PFD before P&IDs and datasheets are developed.

Apply it at work

Find a PFD from your workplace or a public example. Trace the main stream from feed to product and list what each piece of equipment does to it.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 20 · 8 min

Equipment sizing and process datasheets

With balances and the PFD fixed, the process engineer sizes each piece of equipment and records the requirements on a process datasheet. Mechanical engineers and vendors then complete the mechanical design.

What gets sized, and the key inputs

EquipmentKey sizing inputsTypical outputs
Vessels and drumsFlow, residence or hold-up time, phases, liquid levelsDiameter, height, nozzles, design pressure and temperature
Heat exchangersDuty, inlet and outlet temperatures, allowable pressure drop, foulingType, area, design conditions
PumpsFlow, suction and discharge conditions, fluid propertiesRated flow, differential head, NPSH available, driver power estimate
Compressors and blowersFlow, inlet conditions, pressure ratioType, power estimate, stages
ReactorsKinetics or pilot data, residence time, heat removalVolume, cooling or heating surface, internals
ColumnsSeparation required, vapour-liquid dataStages, diameter, internals

An example: heat exchanger duty and area

To cool 10,000 kg/h of a liquid with heat capacity 2.5 kJ/kg·K from 120 °C to 50 °C, the duty is Q = m × cp × ΔT = (10,000 ÷ 3,600) × 2.5 × 70 ≈ 486 kW. Area follows from Q = U × A × LMTD, with the overall coefficient U from experience or detailed rating. The Insights article Sizing a heat exchanger completes the calculation.

Design conditions

Each item needs a design pressure and design temperature above the maximum it could see in operation, upsets and start-up, set by an agreed method. They drive wall thickness, flange ratings, relief settings and cost, so set them carefully and consistently.

Long-lead and critical equipment

Flag equipment with long delivery times, such as compressors, large or alloy vessels, specialty reactors and large electrical equipment, so vendor quotes and data come in early.

Key takeaways

  • Size each item from the balance and record it on a process datasheet.
  • Design pressure and temperature must cover upsets and start-up, set consistently.
  • Flag long-lead equipment early to get vendor data and quotes.

Apply it at work

Pick one pump or exchanger in a process you know. List the inputs needed to size it and find which ones you could get from the stream table.

Check your understanding: A liquid of 3,600 kg/h with cp 2.0 kJ/kg·K is heated from 20 °C to 70 °C. What is the duty?

Utilities, offsites and battery limits

A process unit cannot run without utilities and offsites. They are often a large part of the total installed cost, and they are easy to underestimate when attention is on the main process.

SystemTypical FEED questions
Electrical powerTotal load, largest motor start, available supply, substation or utility upgrade, backup power
Steam and heatingPressure levels, boiler size, condensate return, or electric or hot oil alternatives
CoolingCooling water or towers, air coolers, chillers; design temperatures for summer
Instrument and plant airDemand, dryness, compressor sparing
Nitrogen and inert gasPurging, blanketing, supply by tank or generator
Water and effluentRaw and process water quality; effluent treatment and discharge limits
Storage and handlingFeed and product tanks, silos, loading and unloading, containment
Flare, vents and emissionsRelief disposal, emission control, permits
Buildings and servicesControl room, laboratory, warehouse, fire protection

Utility balances

Sum each utility's demand from every consumer in each operating case, including start-up and peak cases, and compare with what is available. For a project on an existing site, a utility constraint such as limited electrical supply can drive cost and schedule more than the process itself, because utility upgrades can take a long time to arrange.

Battery limits

Define exactly where the project's scope ends: each line, cable and service crossing the boundary, with its conditions at the tie-in point. Unclear battery limits cause gaps, where nobody designs a needed item, and overlaps, where two parties design the same thing. A battery limit table, signed off by both sides, prevents both.

Key takeaways

  • Utilities and offsites are a large, often underestimated part of cost.
  • Build utility balances for every operating case, including start-up and peaks.
  • Define battery limits and tie-in conditions in a signed table.

Apply it at work

List every utility and service the process you know needs. For each, note the source and whether its capacity has been confirmed for the new load.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 20 · 8 min

Piping and instrumentation diagrams

The piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) is the central engineering document of a process plant. It shows every piece of equipment, line, valve, instrument and control and safety function, and how they connect. Operators, engineers, maintenance staff and hazard reviews all rely on it for the life of the plant.

What a P&ID shows

  • All equipment with tags, including spares and packaged units.
  • All process and utility lines with line numbers that encode size, service, sequence and piping class.
  • Manual valves, control valves and their fail positions, check valves, drains and vents.
  • Instruments and their functions using standard symbols and tag letters, commonly based on ISA-5.1.
  • Pressure relief devices with set pressures.
  • Interlocks and safety instrumented functions.
  • Specification breaks, insulation and heat tracing, and sample points.

Reading instrument tags

The first letter is the measured variable and the following letters the function. FIC-101 is a flow indicating controller; LT-205 a level transmitter; PSV-301 a pressure safety valve; TAH a temperature alarm high. Learn the common letters and most P&IDs become readable.

P&ID development in FEED

P&IDs go through several issues: for review, for HAZOP, after HAZOP and approved for design. Each review adds detail and resolves comments. By the end of FEED they should be stable enough that detailed engineering adds detail rather than changing the design. After that, any change follows management of change.

Operators and maintenance staff should review P&IDs before the HAZOP: they spot missing drains, unreachable valves and isolation that does not allow safe maintenance. The lab after this lesson tests which FEED document records which decision.

Key takeaways

  • The P&ID shows every line, valve, instrument and safety function.
  • Instrument tags follow a standard letter code, such as FIC or PSV.
  • P&IDs mature through reviews and HAZOP before detailed engineering.

Apply it at work

Take one P&ID sheet and decode five instrument tags. Then check whether every piece of equipment can be isolated and drained for maintenance.

Check your understanding: On a P&ID, what is LT-205?

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 20 · 7 min

Control philosophy, alarms and interlocks

How the plant will be operated and controlled is decided in FEED and recorded in a control philosophy (or control narrative) and related documents.

What the control philosophy covers

  • The level of automation and what operators do manually.
  • The main control loops and strategies: what is measured and what is adjusted to hold each key variable.
  • Start-up, normal operation, shutdown and emergency shutdown sequences.
  • The control system architecture: basic process control system (BPCS), any separate safety instrumented system (SIS), and links to packaged equipment controls.
  • Operator interface, alarm philosophy and data historian.

Alarms

An alarm should require an operator action. Too many alarms, or alarms that come in floods during upsets, lead operators to miss the important ones. An alarm philosophy sets rules for when an alarm is justified, how it is prioritized and how operators should respond. Recognized practices for alarm management, such as ISA-18.2, give a structured approach.

Interlocks and cause-and-effect charts

Interlocks take automatic action when a condition occurs: trip the feed pump on high-high level, close the fuel valve on low air flow. A cause-and-effect chart lists causes (initiating conditions) in rows and effects (actions) in columns, with a mark where a cause triggers an effect. It is a compact, reviewable record of all automatic actions and is used later to program and test the system.

Separate control from safety

Normal control keeps the process at target. Safety functions bring it to a safe state when control fails. Where a safety function is needed to reduce risk to a tolerable level, it is typically implemented in an independent SIS designed to a recognized standard such as IEC 61511, covered later in the course.

Key takeaways

  • The control philosophy defines automation, control strategies and operating sequences.
  • Every alarm should require an operator action; avoid alarm floods.
  • Cause-and-effect charts record all automatic actions for review and testing.

Apply it at work

Write a cause-and-effect chart with three causes and three effects for a simple tank and pump. Check that each high-high or low-low condition has a defined action.

Lists, layouts and the 3D model

Alongside the drawings, FEED produces lists and layouts that turn the design into quantities for estimating, procurement and construction planning.

DocumentContentsUsed for
Equipment listEvery item with tag, service, size, design conditions, material, powerEstimate, procurement, electrical load list
Line listEvery line with size, service, design conditions, piping class, insulation, test pressurePiping estimate, stress review, pressure testing
Instrument indexEvery instrument with tag, type, service and locationInstrument estimate and specification
Electrical load listEvery electrical consumer and its powerSizing transformers, switchgear and supply

Hazardous area classificationZones where flammable atmospheres may occurSelecting electrical equipment

Layout decisions with lasting effects

The plot plan affects safety, operation, maintenance and cost for decades. Consider:

  • Separation distances between hazardous equipment, occupied buildings and site boundaries.
  • Prevailing wind for gas releases and the location of control rooms and air intakes.
  • Access for cranes, maintenance, tube bundle pulling and emergency response.
  • Gravity flow and pump suction requirements, which may fix equipment elevations.
  • Space for future expansion.

3D models

Many FEED projects build a 3D model of the main equipment and large piping. Model reviews with operations and maintenance catch access problems that are hard to see on 2D drawings, such as a valve that can only be reached with a ladder.

Key takeaways

  • Lists turn the design into quantities for estimates and procurement.
  • Plot plan decisions affect safety, operability and cost for decades.
  • 3D model reviews with operations catch access problems early.

Codes, standards and approvals in Canada

Process plants in Canada are designed and built under provincial and federal requirements, with recognized codes and standards adopted by reference. FEED must identify what applies early, because some approvals take months.

AreaTypical codes, standards and bodies
Pressure equipmentCSA B51 (boiler, pressure vessel and pressure piping code), ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section VIII for vessels, ASME B31.3 for process piping; provincial safety authorities such as Technical Safety BC or ABSA in Alberta
Design registrationPressure vessels, boilers and many fittings need a Canadian Registration Number (CRN) for each province where they are used
Pressure reliefASME Section VIII requirements for overpressure protection; API 520 and API 521 commonly used for sizing and relief system design
ElectricalCanadian Electrical Code, including Section 18 for hazardous locations; CSA-certified equipment
Instrumentation and safety systemsISA-5.1 symbols; IEC 61511 for safety instrumented systems
Buildings and fireNational and provincial building and fire codes
EnvironmentProvincial environmental permits for air emissions, effluent and waste; federal requirements where they apply
Professional practiceEngineering is regulated by provincial associations, such as Engineers and Geoscientists BC, APEGA and PEO; engineering documents are authenticated by registered professionals as each province requires

What FEED should produce

  • A codes and standards list naming editions, agreed with the owner.
  • A regulatory and permitting plan with lead times, required studies and responsibilities.
  • Early meetings with authorities on novel aspects, such as new technology or unusual materials.

Requirements differ between provinces and change over time, and some sites have specific municipal or Indigenous consultation requirements. Confirm the current requirements for your project with the authorities and qualified professionals involved; this table is an orientation, not a compliance checklist.

Key takeaways

  • Identify applicable codes, standards and approvals early in FEED.
  • Pressure equipment in Canada needs registration such as CRNs, province by province.
  • Build a permitting plan with lead times, and confirm requirements with authorities.

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 20 · 8 min

Process hazard analysis: HAZID and HAZOP

FEED is the best time to find and design out hazards. Two structured reviews are common.

HAZID

A hazard identification review early in FEED looks at the whole facility: the materials, site, layout, external events and major hazard scenarios such as fires, explosions and toxic releases. It shapes layout, separation distances and the overall safety concept.

HAZOP

A hazard and operability study examines the P&IDs line by line. The plant is split into nodes, sections with a defined design intent. For each node, the team applies guidewords to process parameters to generate deviations:

GuidewordParameterDeviation
NoFlowNo flow to the heater
MorePressureHigh pressure in the separator
LessTemperatureLow temperature at the reactor inlet
ReverseFlowBackflow from the reactor into the feed tank
Other thanCompositionWrong material delivered to the feed tank

For each credible deviation the team records causes, consequences, existing safeguards and, where safeguards are not enough, recommendations. Each recommendation gets an owner and is tracked to closure.

Running a good HAZOP

  • An experienced, independent facilitator and a scribe.
  • Process, operations, instrumentation, mechanical and safety participants.
  • P&IDs at the HAZOP issue, with the design basis, cause-and-effect charts and datasheets available.
  • Realistic scheduling: tired teams miss things, so keep sessions to a sensible length.

HAZOP also covers operability: start-up, shutdown, maintenance and sampling. Many recommendations improve how the plant will be run, not only its safety. The Insights article A worked HAZOP walks through a node in detail. The lab after this lesson asks you to place hazard scenarios on a risk grid.

Key takeaways

  • HAZID reviews facility-wide hazards early; HAZOP examines P&IDs node by node.
  • Guidewords applied to parameters generate deviations to analyze.
  • Track every recommendation to closure with an owner.

Apply it at work

Choose one line or vessel you know. Apply the guidewords No, More, Less and Reverse to flow and pressure, and write one cause and one safeguard for each deviation.

Check your understanding: In a HAZOP, what does applying the guideword 'Reverse' to 'Flow' produce?

Relief and overpressure protection

Every piece of equipment that could be overpressured needs protection. FEED identifies the overpressure scenarios, sizes the relief devices and designs the system that safely disposes of what they release.

Common overpressure scenarios

  • Blocked outlet: a valve closes downstream while a pump or compressor keeps feeding.
  • External fire: heat input to a vessel causes liquid to boil and pressure to rise.
  • Control valve failure: a valve fails open and lets a high-pressure source into a lower-pressure system.
  • Tube rupture: high-pressure fluid leaks into the low-pressure side of a heat exchanger.
  • Utility failure: loss of cooling water or power, which can stop condensers or cooling.
  • Runaway reaction: an exothermic reaction accelerates beyond cooling capacity.
  • Thermal expansion: liquid trapped between closed valves is heated.

Relief design steps

  1. List credible scenarios for each piece of equipment.
  2. Calculate the required relief rate for each; the largest credible case governs the size.
  3. Select and size the device, a pressure safety valve or rupture disc, commonly using methods in API 520 and API 521.
  4. Design the inlet and outlet piping so pressure losses do not stop the device from working properly.
  5. Design the disposal system: to atmosphere where safe and allowed, or to a closed system, knock-out drum, flare or scrubber.

Runaway reactions need special care

Relief for runaway reactions can involve two-phase flow and very large rates. Get reaction calorimetry data and specialist input early. The Insights articles on runaway reactions and pressure relief basics cover these topics further.

Relief design is a specialist, code-governed task. It must be done and checked by qualified engineers.

Key takeaways

  • Identify every credible overpressure scenario for each item.
  • The largest credible relief rate governs the device size.
  • Design the inlet, outlet and disposal system, not just the valve.

Layers of protection and safety instrumented systems

No single safeguard is perfect. Process safety relies on several independent layers of protection, each able to stop a hazard scenario from becoming an incident.

LayerExample
Inherently safer designSmaller inventory of hazardous material; lower pressure; less hazardous solvent
Basic process controlLevel controller keeps the tank within normal range
Alarms and operator responseHigh-level alarm with a defined operator action and enough time to act
Safety instrumented functionIndependent high-high level trip that stops the feed pump
Physical protectionRelief valve, rupture disc, overflow to a safe location
MitigationDikes and bunds, fire protection, gas detection
Emergency responseSite and community response plans

Inherently safer design comes first

The most reliable layer is a hazard that is not there. During FEED, ask whether you can eliminate, reduce, substitute or simplify: store less, operate at milder conditions, choose a less hazardous material, or design equipment that cannot be overpressured. These choices are cheap on paper and expensive later.

LOPA and SIL

Layer of protection analysis (LOPA) takes high-consequence scenarios from the HAZOP and checks, semi-quantitatively, whether the independent layers reduce the frequency to a tolerable level. If not, a safety instrumented function may be needed, with a safety integrity level (SIL) that sets how reliable it must be. Safety instrumented systems for the process industry are commonly designed under IEC 61511, which covers the whole lifecycle from specification to testing and maintenance.

A layer only counts if it is independent of the cause and of the other layers. A high-level alarm from the same transmitter that failed and caused the overflow is not independent.

Key takeaways

  • Process safety relies on several independent protection layers.
  • Inherently safer design is the most reliable layer and cheapest in FEED.
  • LOPA tests whether layers are enough; SIL sets the reliability of a safety function.

Apply it at work

For one hazardous scenario you know, list its protection layers and check whether each is truly independent of the cause and of the others.

Check your understanding: A high-level alarm uses the same transmitter whose failure causes the tank to overfill. Can it count as an independent layer for that scenario?

Constructability, operability and maintainability

A plant can be safe and correctly sized on paper and still be hard to build, run and maintain. FEED reviews these qualities while changes are still cheap.

Constructability

  • Can equipment be transported to site (road limits, bridges, rail) and lifted into place?
  • Can parts be modularized or pre-assembled off-site to reduce site work and schedule risk?
  • Is there laydown space and crane access?
  • Does the sequence allow tie-ins to an operating plant with minimum shutdown time?

Operability

  • Can operators start up, shut down and handle upsets with the controls and instruments provided?
  • Are sample points, sight glasses and local indicators where operators need them?
  • Is the operating window wide enough for real feed variations?

Maintainability

  • Can each item be isolated, drained, purged and made safe for maintenance?
  • Is there access for removing exchanger bundles, pump motors and agitators?
  • Are spares, sparing philosophy and maintenance intervals defined?

The best reviewers are experienced operators and maintenance technicians from a similar plant. Hold structured reviews on the P&IDs and the 3D model, and record actions like HAZOP recommendations.

Change control starts in FEED

Once key documents are approved, changes should go through a design change process: describe the change, assess impact on safety, cost and schedule, get approval and update every affected document. The same discipline continues through detailed engineering, construction and operation as management of change. The Insights article Management of change for small plants covers the operating phase.

Key takeaways

  • Review constructability, operability and maintainability during FEED.
  • Involve experienced operators and maintenance technicians in reviews.
  • Control changes to approved documents through a design change process.

Cost estimates and contingency

A cost estimate is only as good as the design behind it. The AACE International recommended practice 18R-97 defines estimate classes for process industries by how complete the project definition is.

ClassTypical stageProject definitionTypical accuracy range
Class 5Concept screening0–2%Low −20% to −50%; high +30% to +100%
Class 4Feasibility1–15%Low −15% to −30%; high +20% to +50%
Class 3Budget authorization (end of FEED)10–40%Low −10% to −20%; high +10% to +30%
Class 2Control or bid30–75%Low −5% to −15%; high +5% to +20%
Class 1Check estimate or bid65–100%Low −3% to −10%; high +3% to +15%

The ranges are wide and asymmetric: estimates are more likely to come in under-scoped than over. The actual range for a project depends on its technical and execution risks.

What a FEED estimate includes

  • Equipment costs from vendor quotes for major items and estimating data for the rest.
  • Bulk materials: piping, steel, concrete, electrical, instrumentation, from the lists and layouts.
  • Construction labour, productivity and site conditions.
  • Indirect costs: engineering, construction management, temporary facilities, commissioning, spares, owner's costs.
  • Contingency: an amount for costs that will occur within the defined scope but cannot be itemized yet.

Contingency and escalation

Contingency covers uncertainty within scope; it is not for scope changes. Base it on a risk analysis rather than a fixed percentage habit. Separately, allow for escalation: price changes between the estimate date and when money is spent. The lab after this lesson shows the plausible range around a base estimate for each class.

Key takeaways

  • AACE 18R-97 links estimate class to the maturity of project definition.
  • A FEED estimate is typically Class 3, with a range such as −10/−20% to +10/+30%.
  • Contingency covers uncertainty within scope; escalation and scope changes are separate.

Apply it at work

For a project you know, identify the estimate class at each gate. Was the contingency based on a risk analysis or a habit?

Module 5 · Lesson 18 of 20 · 7 min

Schedule, procurement and long-lead items

FEED produces a project schedule that the investment decision relies on. The critical path in process projects often runs through engineering and procurement of a few key items, not through construction.

Typical long-lead items

  • Compressors and large rotating equipment.
  • Large, thick-walled or alloy pressure vessels and reactors.
  • Specialty heat exchangers.
  • Large transformers, switchgear and utility connections.
  • Control and safety systems, when highly customized.
  • Items made from specialty materials with limited supply.

Lead times change with market conditions, so get current vendor quotes during FEED rather than relying on past experience.

Early procurement

To protect the schedule, the owner may authorize ordering long-lead equipment before FID. This commits money early and is only sensible when the design of that item is firm and the risk of cancellation is acceptable. Cancellation terms should be agreed with the vendor.

The FEED schedule should show

  • Engineering durations and key document issues.
  • Vendor data dependencies: detailed engineering needs certified vendor drawings.
  • Procurement of each major item, from enquiry to delivery.
  • Permits and approvals with their lead times.
  • Construction, mechanical completion, commissioning and start-up.
  • Planned shutdowns for tie-ins on existing sites.

Realism over optimism

Compare the schedule with similar completed projects. Optimistic durations become a problem later, when costs are committed. Identify the critical path and the few items that, if late, delay everything, and manage them closely.

Key takeaways

  • The critical path often runs through engineering and long-lead equipment.
  • Get current vendor lead times during FEED.
  • Consider early procurement only for firm designs with acceptable cancellation risk.

Apply it at work

List the three longest-lead items for a project you know and check their current delivery times with vendors. Are they on the critical path?

The FEED package and the investment decision

At the end of FEED, the deliverables are assembled into a FEED package that supports the investment decision and becomes the basis for detailed engineering or for EPC bids.

Typical contents

GroupDocuments
BasisDesign basis, codes and standards list, battery limit table
ProcessMass and energy balances, PFDs, process datasheets, utility balances
EngineeringP&IDs, equipment list, line list, instrument index, electrical load list, single-line diagram, plot plan, hazardous area classification
Control and safetyControl philosophy, cause-and-effect charts, HAZID and HAZOP reports with action status, relief study
CommercialClass 3 cost estimate, schedule, procurement plan, vendor quotes
ExecutionExecution plan, risk register, permitting plan, contracting strategy

The FEED gate review

Before FID, a review, ideally with people independent of the project team, checks:

  • Is the design complete enough for the estimate class claimed?
  • Are HAZOP and other review actions closed or tracked?
  • Are the main risks identified with owners and responses?
  • Are schedule and cost realistic compared with similar projects?
  • Do the economics still work at the high end of the estimate range?

The risk register

List each significant risk with its likelihood, impact, owner and response: avoid, reduce, transfer or accept. Typical FEED-stage risks include scale-up uncertainty, feedstock variability, long-lead delivery, permitting delays, labour availability and interface risks on existing sites.

A decision not to proceed is a legitimate outcome of FEED. Stopping a weak project before construction is far cheaper than finishing it.

Key takeaways

  • The FEED package collects basis, process, engineering, safety, commercial and execution documents.
  • An independent gate review tests completeness, risks and realism before FID.
  • Stopping a weak project at FEED is a valid and valuable result.

From FEED to start-up, and your FEED checklist

After FID, the project moves into execution. Knowing what follows helps you make FEED complete.

PhaseWhat happensHow FEED quality shows
Detailed engineeringConstruction drawings, specifications, vendor documentsFew changes to P&IDs and equipment; steady progress
ProcurementEquipment and bulk materials ordered and expeditedAccurate quantities; no surprise long-lead items
ConstructionSite work, installation, testingFewer field changes and rework
Mechanical completion and commissioningSystems checked, tested and commissionedSystems and documents ready; operators trained
Pre-startup safety review and start-upConfirm readiness, then introduce process materialsDesign and procedures match; HAZOP actions closed
Performance test and handoverShow the plant meets design and guaranteesThe design basis was right

A FEED checklist for owners

  • Design basis signed off and under change control.
  • Pilot and data gaps identified and resolved or covered by margins.
  • Balances closed for all key cases; utilities confirmed.
  • P&IDs through HAZOP with actions tracked; relief study done.
  • Operations and maintenance staff took part in reviews.
  • Codes, standards and permitting plan agreed.
  • Class 3 estimate with risk-based contingency; realistic schedule with long-lead items.
  • Risk register with owners; independent gate review completed.

The Insights articles on commissioning, the pre-startup safety review and start-up and performance testing continue the story. This course is educational; real projects need qualified engineers and must meet the codes and regulations that apply.

Key takeaways

  • FEED quality shows later as fewer changes, accurate procurement and smooth start-up.
  • Use an owner's FEED checklist before the investment decision.
  • Real projects need qualified engineers and applicable codes and regulations.
