Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 16 · 6 min

What S&OP is and the problems it solves

Sales and operations planning (S&OP) is a monthly management process that brings sales, operations, purchasing and finance together to agree one plan for the coming months, balancing demand, supply and money.

The problems it solves

  • Several plans: sales forecasts one number, operations plans another, purchasing buys to a third and finance budgets a fourth.
  • Surprises: peaks and shortages discovered too late to respond without expediting, overtime or lost orders.
  • Too much and too little inventory at the same time: excess of some items, shortages of others.
  • Firefighting: managers spend their time reacting instead of planning.
  • Cash surprises: inventory builds or capacity spending that finance did not see coming.

What S&OP is not

S&OP is not detailed production scheduling or item-by-item purchasing. It works at the level of product families and months, looking typically 3 to 18 months ahead. Detailed plans follow from it.

The benefits

Companies that run S&OP well typically report better customer service, lower and better-balanced inventory, steadier production, fewer expedites and more confidence in financial forecasts. The biggest change is often cultural: departments work to one shared plan and resolve trade-offs openly, with the leader deciding.

The term integrated business planning (IBP) is often used for a more mature form of S&OP that ties the plan more tightly to financial and strategic planning. For most SMEs, a disciplined monthly S&OP is the right place to start.

Key takeaways

  • S&OP aligns sales, operations, purchasing and finance on one monthly plan.
  • It works with product families and months, typically 3 to 18 months ahead.
  • It reduces surprises, balances inventory and builds trust in one plan.

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 16 · 7 min

Planning levels, horizons and product families

S&OP sits in a hierarchy of plans, each with its own level of detail and horizon.

PlanDetailHorizonTypical owner
Strategic or business planCompany, markets, revenue and profit1–5 yearsOwner and leadership
S&OPProduct families, units and dollars by month3–18 monthsLeadership team
Master production schedule or purchasing planItems by weekWeeks to a few monthsPlanning
Detailed scheduleOrders by day or shiftDays to weeksOperations

Choosing product families

A product family groups items that share demand patterns and, ideally, the same production resources or suppliers. Good families let you plan demand and capacity together. Most SMEs need between 5 and 15 families; more makes the meeting unmanageable, fewer hides important differences.

Examples: a cabinet maker might use families such as kitchen cabinets, bathroom vanities and custom projects; a distributor might group by product category and main supplier.

Units and dollars

Plan in units (or another physical measure such as tonnes or hours) for operations, and convert to dollars for finance. Both views must agree.

Time fences

Near-term plans are harder to change because materials are ordered and schedules set. Many companies define a frozen period, often a few weeks, where changes need special approval, followed by a slushy zone with limited changes and a liquid zone where S&OP can change plans freely.

Key takeaways

  • S&OP sits between the strategic plan and detailed schedules.
  • Plan 5 to 15 product families that share demand patterns and resources.
  • Use units for operations, dollars for finance, and time fences to control changes.

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 16 · 8 min

The monthly S&OP cycle

S&OP runs as a monthly cycle of steps, each feeding the next.

StepTimingWhoOutput
1. Data gatheringWorking days 1–3Planning, financeLast month's actuals, forecast accuracy, inventory and backlog by family
2. Demand reviewWeek 1–2Sales, marketing, planningUpdated demand plan by family with assumptions and risks
3. Supply reviewWeek 2Operations, purchasing, planningSupply plan and capacity check against demand; constraints and options
4. Pre-S&OP (reconciliation)Week 3Department heads, financeResolved issues, scenarios for unresolved trade-offs, financial view
5. Executive S&OPWeek 3–4Owner or general manager and leadershipDecisions on trade-offs; approved plan

How it flows

Each step resolves what it can and passes only the unresolved issues upward. By the time the executive meeting happens, most issues are settled and the leadership spends its time on a few real trade-offs: for example, whether to add a shift, build inventory ahead of a peak or accept a large order with a tight deadline.

For small companies

In a small business, the five steps may compress into two meetings: a working meeting that combines the demand and supply reviews, and a short leadership decision meeting. The discipline of reviewing demand, checking supply and deciding trade-offs each month matters more than the number of meetings. The lab after this lesson asks you to match issues to the right step.

Key takeaways

  • S&OP runs monthly: data, demand review, supply review, pre-S&OP, executive meeting.
  • Each step resolves what it can and passes trade-offs upward.
  • Small companies can compress the steps into two meetings.

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 16 · 7 min

Roles, ownership and starting small

S&OP succeeds when the leader owns it and each function brings honest input.

Roles

RoleContribution
Owner or general managerChairs the executive meeting, makes trade-off decisions, insists on one plan
S&OP coordinator (often a planner)Runs the calendar, prepares data and the meeting pack, tracks actions
Sales and marketingOwn the demand plan and its assumptions
Operations and purchasingOwn the supply plan, capacity and material constraints
FinanceConverts plans to dollars, checks against budget and cash

Getting started

  1. Get the leader's commitment to chair the monthly meeting.
  2. Define product families and gather 12 to 24 months of history by family.
  3. Build a simple spreadsheet: demand, supply, inventory and backlog by family by month.
  4. Run the first cycle, imperfect as it will be.
  5. Improve a little each month: better forecasts, capacity data and financial views.

Common pitfalls

  • The leader delegates the meeting and decisions drift.
  • Too much detail: reviewing items instead of families.
  • Sales treats the forecast as a target or a hope rather than an honest estimate.
  • Meetings report numbers but make no decisions.

Expect it to take several monthly cycles before the process feels natural and the benefits are clear.

Key takeaways

  • The leader must own and chair S&OP.
  • Start with families, history and a simple spreadsheet, then improve monthly.
  • Avoid delegation, excess detail, forecasts used as targets and meetings without decisions.

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 16 · 8 min

Building the demand plan

The demand plan is the best estimate of what customers will buy, by family and month. It is an honest forecast, not a sales target or budget.

Two inputs

  1. Statistical baseline: a forecast from history, using methods such as moving averages, exponential smoothing and seasonal indices.
  2. Market intelligence: what the history cannot know: new customers, lost accounts, price changes, promotions, competitor moves, economic conditions and large projects.

Start with the baseline, then adjust it for known events, recording each adjustment and its reason.

Assumptions and risks

Every demand plan rests on assumptions, such as "Customer X places its annual order in March" or "the housing market stays flat". Write them down. List risks and opportunities with rough sizes and probabilities, such as "a 30% chance of a new contract worth 2,000 units a month from September".

Unconstrained demand

The demand plan should show what customers want, not what you think you can supply. If demand exceeds capacity, that gap is exactly what the supply review and executive meeting need to see and decide on. Hiding demand to match capacity removes the chance to respond.

Forecast, budget and target

NumberPurpose
Forecast (demand plan)Honest best estimate, updated monthly
BudgetFinancial plan for the year, fixed at the start
Sales targetStretch goal to motivate the sales team

Mixing them causes trouble: forecasting to the target builds excess inventory; forecasting to the budget hides changes in the market.

Key takeaways

  • Combine a statistical baseline with market intelligence and record adjustments.
  • Write down assumptions and size risks and opportunities.
  • Show unconstrained demand and keep forecasts separate from budgets and targets.

Apply it at work

For your largest product family, write the baseline forecast for the next six months, list three assumptions and one risk and one opportunity with rough sizes.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 16 · 7 min

Forecast accuracy, bias and consensus

Measuring forecast performance builds trust and shows where to improve.

Accuracy and bias

  • Accuracy measures the size of errors, for example mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) by family, or a weighted measure that gives large families more weight.
  • Bias measures the direction: consistently over- or under-forecasting. Track the sum of errors with their signs over several months.

Bias is often more damaging than random error because it accumulates: steady over-forecasting builds excess inventory month after month; steady under-forecasting causes repeated shortages.

Measure at the right lag

Measure the forecast made at the point when supply decisions are locked, such as the forecast made two months ahead if your lead time is two months. Measuring last week's forecast for this week flatters accuracy and says little about the plan you actually acted on.

Forecast value added

Compare the accuracy of the statistical baseline with the final forecast after adjustments. If adjustments make the forecast worse, which is common, review who adjusts and why. Keep adjustments that add value and stop those that don't.

Consensus

The demand review ends with one agreed demand plan that sales, marketing and planning stand behind. Disagreements are resolved by discussing assumptions and evidence, not by averaging opinions. Where a real difference remains, show it as a scenario for the executive meeting.

Key takeaways

  • Track accuracy for size and bias for direction; bias accumulates.
  • Measure at the lag when supply decisions are locked.
  • Check whether adjustments add value and agree one consensus plan.

Apply it at work

Calculate MAPE and bias for your top three families over the last six months, using forecasts made at your typical lead time.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 16 · 7 min

Big customers, promotions and new products

Some demand is lumpy or unusual and needs special handling in the demand plan.

Large customers

When a few customers make up a large share of demand, plan them individually. Ask for their forecasts, share yours, and agree how far ahead firm orders arrive. Collaborative planning with key customers often improves accuracy more than any forecasting method.

Projects and one-off orders

Treat large project orders separately from the baseline so they don't distort history. Track them as named opportunities with probabilities and expected timing, and include them in the plan when they reach an agreed probability or are confirmed.

Promotions and price changes

Promotions shift demand: a lift during the promotion and often a dip afterwards as customers stocked up. Record past promotion effects to estimate future ones, and agree promotion calendars with operations early.

New products and phase-outs

  • New products: with no history, forecast using similar past launches, customer commitments and market estimates. Review weekly at first and plan supply flexibly.
  • Phase-outs: agree the last date to sell, plan the run-down of inventory and stop purchasing in time to avoid obsolete stock.

Product lifecycle review

Many companies add a short product review at the start of the S&OP cycle covering launches, changes and phase-outs, so demand and supply plans reflect them from the start.

Key takeaways

  • Plan large customers individually and collaborate with them.
  • Keep projects and promotions separate from the baseline with probabilities and timing.
  • Forecast new products from analogues and plan phase-outs to avoid obsolete stock.

Apply it at work

List your five largest customers and decide which you could share forecasts with. Draft the questions you would ask each.

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 16 · 7 min

The demand review meeting

The demand review turns inputs into the agreed demand plan for the month.

Agenda (60 to 90 minutes)

  1. Last month's actual demand versus forecast by family; accuracy and bias.
  2. Changes in the market and key customers since last month.
  3. The updated plan by family for the next 3 to 18 months, with changes highlighted.
  4. Assumptions, risks and opportunities.
  5. New products, promotions and phase-outs.
  6. Agreement on the plan and issues to pass to the supply review.

Focus on change and exceptions

Don't review every family in detail every month. Focus on families where the forecast changed significantly, where accuracy or bias is poor, or where big risks and opportunities exist.

A simple display

For each family, a chart showing the last 12 months of actuals, last month's forecast and the new forecast for the coming months, with last year for comparison, makes changes obvious. Add a table of the numbers and key assumptions.

Output

The agreed demand plan goes to the supply review. Sales owns it and should be able to explain each number. Any unresolved disagreement goes forward as a scenario, such as "base case" and "if the new contract is won".

Key takeaways

  • Review accuracy, market changes, the updated plan, assumptions and product changes.
  • Focus on families with big changes, poor accuracy or major risks.
  • Sales owns the agreed plan; unresolved differences become scenarios.

Apply it at work

Prepare a one-page demand review chart for one family: 12 months of actuals, last month's forecast, the new forecast and last year.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 16 · 8 min

Rough-cut capacity planning

The supply review checks whether the demand plan can be met with the capacity and materials available. Rough-cut capacity planning does this at family level, focusing on the few resources that limit output.

The method

  1. Identify the critical resources: the bottleneck machine or line, key skilled labour, a supplier with limited capacity, warehouse space.
  2. For each family, estimate how much of each critical resource one unit requires, such as hours per 100 units.
  3. Multiply planned volumes by these rates to get the load on each resource by month.
  4. Compare load with available capacity, allowing for realistic efficiency (OEE), holidays and planned maintenance.

Example

A family's plan is 15,000 units in October. The bottleneck line's ideal rate is 60 units an hour and it runs at about 70% OEE, so it effectively makes 42 units an hour. It needs about 357 hours, but only 320 are available on two shifts. That is a shortfall of about 37 hours, roughly 1,550 units, which the supply review must address.

Look ahead

A load chart showing demand against capacity for each month of the horizon reveals peaks long before they arrive. A peak in four months may be met by building inventory now, scheduling overtime or arranging outsourcing; discovered in the same month, it means lost orders or expensive expediting.

The lab after this lesson calculates load, shortfall and maximum output for a family and resource.

Key takeaways

  • Check load against capacity for the few critical resources.
  • Use realistic efficiency, holidays and maintenance in available capacity.
  • Look months ahead so peaks can be handled calmly.

Apply it at work

Identify your two most critical resources and estimate next quarter's load on each from your demand plan. Mark any month over 90% load.

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 16 · 7 min

Supply options: level, chase and in between

When demand and capacity don't match month by month, there are several ways to respond.

Two basic strategies

StrategyHow it worksProsCons
LevelProduce at a steady rate; inventory absorbs the peaks and valleysStable workforce, efficient operationsInventory carrying cost; risk if demand changes
ChaseChange production to match demand each monthLow inventoryOvertime, hiring and layoffs, idle time, quality risk

Most businesses use a mix: a fairly level base rate with some flexibility for peaks.

Ways to add capacity

  • Overtime or extra shifts, short term.
  • Temporary staff or cross-trained employees.
  • Outsourcing or subcontracting some work.
  • Improving OEE on the bottleneck.
  • Building inventory ahead of the peak.
  • Longer term: new equipment, a new line or more space.

Ways to manage demand

  • Agree delivery dates with customers to smooth the load.
  • Offer incentives for off-peak orders.
  • Prioritize the most profitable or strategic customers when capacity is short.

Present options with their costs

The supply review shouldn't simply say "we can't do it". It should present options with their costs and service effects, for example: overtime for 37 hours at a given cost, building 1,500 units in August at a given carrying cost, or delaying some orders with a given risk to customer relationships.

Key takeaways

  • Level strategies use inventory; chase strategies change output each month.
  • Add capacity with overtime, staff, outsourcing, OEE gains or pre-builds.
  • Present options with costs and service effects, not just 'we can't'.

Apply it at work

For your peak month, list three ways to close a capacity gap and estimate the cost and service effect of each.

Module 3 · Lesson 11 of 16 · 7 min

Materials and suppliers

Capacity is not only internal. Materials and suppliers can constrain the plan just as much as machines and people.

Critical materials

Identify materials and components that are long lead time, single-sourced, capacity-limited at the supplier or volatile in price. Check planned demand for them against supplier capacity and lead times each month.

Share the plan

Share family-level forecasts with key suppliers for the next three to six months. Suppliers who see your plan can reserve capacity and materials. Agree how firm each part of the forecast is: for example, the next month firm, months two and three flexible within 20%, beyond that indicative.

Long lead times

For items with lead times longer than your firm order window, S&OP decisions effectively commit purchases. Make sure the supply review flags these, so the executive meeting understands which decisions lock in spending.

Supply risk

List the main supply risks with likelihood and impact: a single supplier, a long ocean shipment, a commodity price swing. Agree mitigation: a second source, safety stock, a forward purchase or an alternate material. Review the list at each supply review.

Key takeaways

  • Identify long-lead, single-source and capacity-limited materials.
  • Share forecasts with key suppliers and agree how firm each horizon is.
  • Flag decisions that commit long-lead purchases and review supply risks monthly.

Apply it at work

List your five most critical materials or components, with lead times and suppliers. Mark single-source items and decide a mitigation for each.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 of 16 · 7 min

Inventory and backlog targets by family

S&OP plans inventory and backlog by family, not just production. The plan shows how they will move month by month.

Make-to-stock families

For families sold from stock, the plan balances demand and production to reach a target inventory, often expressed in days of supply:

Ending inventory = beginning inventory + production − demand

If the plan shows inventory falling below target ahead of a peak, production must rise earlier; if it climbs well above, production should slow.

Make-to-order families

For families made to order, the equivalent measure is the backlog of orders waiting: a healthy backlog gives a steady workload; a growing backlog means lead times are stretching; a shrinking one means capacity may sit idle.

Ending backlog = beginning backlog + new orders − shipments

Setting targets

Set inventory targets by family from service level needs, demand variability and supply lead times, using the methods in the Inventory Management for SMEs course. Set backlog targets from the lead time customers accept. Review targets quarterly.

A simple S&OP sheet

Family A (units)JulAugSepOct
Demand plan10,00011,00013,00015,000
Production plan12,00012,50013,00013,400
Ending inventory (start 6,000)8,0009,5009,5007,900

Building inventory in July and August covers the October peak that exceeds capacity.

Key takeaways

  • Plan inventory for make-to-stock and backlog for make-to-order families.
  • Ending inventory = beginning + production − demand.
  • Set targets from service, variability and lead times; review quarterly.

Apply it at work

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 16 · 7 min

The financial view

S&OP becomes a management process, not just a planning exercise, when the plan is expressed in money.

Converting volume to dollars

Finance multiplies planned volumes by average prices and costs by family to show:

  • Revenue by month and family.
  • Gross margin.
  • Inventory value and its change.
  • Capacity costs: overtime, temporary labour, outsourcing.
  • Cash effects of inventory builds and capital spending.

Compare with budget

Show the S&OP plan against the annual budget. Gaps are not failures; they are early warnings. If demand is running below budget, leadership can decide to push sales, cut costs or accept a lower result months before year-end. If above budget, capacity and cash may need attention.

The latest estimate

Combining year-to-date actuals with the S&OP plan for the remaining months gives a rolling latest estimate of the year's results, usually far more reliable than the original budget by mid-year.

Price the options

When the supply review presents options, finance adds their effect on margin and cash. Choosing between overtime and an inventory build becomes a clear comparison: extra labour cost against carrying cost and risk.

Key takeaways

  • Convert the volume plan into revenue, margin, inventory value, capacity costs and cash.
  • Compare with budget to get early warnings, not to assign blame.
  • Use a rolling latest estimate and price each supply option.

Apply it at work

Convert next quarter's plan for your largest family into revenue, gross margin and inventory value, and compare it with the budget.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 16 · 7 min

Pre-S&OP: resolve issues and prepare scenarios

The pre-S&OP meeting is the working session that prepares the executive meeting. Its job is to resolve what can be resolved and frame the rest as clear decisions.

Agenda

  1. Review the demand and supply plans and the financial view.
  2. Resolve imbalances within the team's authority, such as shifting production between months or using approved overtime.
  3. For issues that need leadership decisions, prepare scenarios with costs, risks and recommendations.
  4. Check performance measures and open actions.
  5. Prepare the executive meeting pack.

Good scenarios

Present two or three options for each decision, each with:

  • What happens to customer service.
  • Cost and margin effect.
  • Inventory and cash effect.
  • Risks and assumptions.
  • A recommendation.

Example: "October demand exceeds capacity by about 1,550 units. Option A: build ahead in July–August (carrying cost and some obsolescence risk). Option B: Saturday overtime in September–October (labour cost; fatigue risk). Option C: delay lower-priority orders by two weeks (risk to two customers). Recommendation: A, with B as backup if demand rises further."

Keep the pack short

A good executive pack is a few pages: key measures, the plan summary by family, the decisions needed with scenarios, and risks. If the pack runs to 40 pages, the pre-S&OP has not done its job. The lab after this lesson asks you to compare options by cost and service.

Key takeaways

  • Pre-S&OP resolves issues within the team's authority.
  • Frame leadership decisions as two or three costed scenarios with a recommendation.
  • Keep the executive pack to a few pages.

Apply it at work

Take one current supply–demand imbalance and write two or three options with service, cost, cash and risk effects and a recommendation.

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 16 · 7 min

The executive S&OP meeting and measures

The executive meeting is where the plan becomes the company's plan. It should be short, focused and decisive.

Agenda (about 60 to 90 minutes)

  1. Performance: key measures from last month.
  2. Business and market changes.
  3. Plan summary by family: demand, supply, inventory or backlog, financial view against budget.
  4. Decisions: the scenarios prepared in pre-S&OP.
  5. Risks and opportunities.
  6. Confirm the approved plan and actions.

Key S&OP measures

AreaMeasure
DemandForecast accuracy and bias by family
SupplyProduction or purchasing plan attainment
CustomerOn-time delivery, fill rate, lead time
InventoryInventory versus target by family, days of supply
FinancialRevenue and margin versus budget and latest estimate
ProcessMeetings held on schedule, actions completed

Decide and record

Every decision is recorded with an owner and communicated to the teams who must act on it. The approved plan becomes the basis for the master schedule, purchasing and the financial forecast. If people later work to a different plan, S&OP has not yet taken hold.

Key takeaways

  • Run a short executive meeting that reviews performance and decides trade-offs.
  • Track demand, supply, customer, inventory, financial and process measures.
  • Record decisions with owners and make the approved plan the basis for all detailed plans.

Implementing S&OP: a six-cycle roadmap

S&OP matures over several monthly cycles. A realistic plan expects the first cycles to be rough and improves each month.

CycleFocus
Before cycle 1Leader commitment; families defined; coordinator named; history gathered; calendar set
Cycles 1–2Basic spreadsheet with demand, supply and inventory or backlog by family; first meetings; learn the rhythm
Cycles 3–4Add rough-cut capacity for critical resources; start forecast accuracy and bias tracking; first scenarios
Cycles 5–6Add the financial view against budget; refine families and targets; measure process discipline
BeyondSupplier and customer collaboration, longer horizon, better tools, link to strategic planning

Tools

Most SMEs start successfully with a well-structured spreadsheet. As the process matures, planning modules in ERP systems or dedicated planning software can automate data gathering and scenarios. Choose tools after the process works, not before.

Signs S&OP is working

  • One set of numbers is used across sales, operations and finance.
  • Peaks and shortages are seen months ahead.
  • Executive meetings make decisions, quickly.
  • Inventory is better balanced and expedites fall.

Related courses

Inventory Management for SMEs covers inventory targets and replenishment in depth; OEE helps raise bottleneck capacity; and Data and Dashboards for SMEs helps automate the S&OP data.

Key takeaways

  • Expect S&OP to mature over about six monthly cycles.
  • Start with a spreadsheet; add capacity, accuracy and financial views step by step.
  • Choose tools after the process works.

Apply it at work

Put the six-cycle roadmap into your calendar with dates for each monthly meeting and the improvement focus of each cycle.
