A practical cybersecurity course for owners, managers and the people who look after IT in small and mid-sized businesses. Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 20 · 6 min

Why small and mid-sized businesses are targets

Many owners assume attackers only go after banks and large corporations. In reality, most attacks are automated and opportunistic. Criminal groups scan the internet for any exposed, unpatched system and send phishing emails by the million. They are looking for an easy way in, not a particular company. A 20-person firm with a weak password on its remote access is as attractive as a large one, and usually easier.

Why SMEs are attractive

  • Fewer defences: no dedicated security staff, older systems, and security handled by whoever is "good with computers".
  • Money moves through them: invoices, payroll and supplier payments make SMEs targets for payment fraud.
  • Valuable data: customer records, employee information and designs can be sold or held for ransom.
  • A route to bigger targets: attackers use smaller suppliers to reach their larger customers.
  • Dependence on IT: when systems stop, so do orders, production and shipping, which makes paying a ransom tempting.

The good news

Most successful attacks exploit a short list of basic weaknesses: stolen or weak passwords without multi-factor authentication, unpatched systems, exposed remote access, untested backups and staff who have not been shown what phishing looks like. Fixing these basics stops the large majority of opportunistic attacks and does not need a big budget.

This course is practical guidance for business owners and managers, not a technical security manual. For specialist work such as configuring firewalls or investigating an incident, use a qualified IT or security provider. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (the Cyber Centre) publishes free, practical guidance for small and medium organizations that complements this course.

Key takeaways

  • Most attacks are automated and opportunistic; size does not protect you.
  • SMEs are attractive for their payments, data and links to larger customers.
  • A short list of basic controls stops most opportunistic attacks.

Apply it at work

List the three systems your business could not operate without for a week. For each, write down who looks after its security today.

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 20 · 7 min

How attacks get in

Attackers use a handful of entry points again and again. Knowing them tells you where to put your effort.

Entry pointHow it worksMain defence
Phishing emailA message tricks someone into clicking a link, opening an attachment or entering a password on a fake login pageEmail filtering, awareness training, MFA
Stolen or weak passwordsPasswords reused from breached websites, guessed, or captured by phishingMFA, password manager, unique passwords
Unpatched softwareKnown flaws in internet-facing systems, such as VPNs, firewalls and email servers, are exploited automaticallyPrompt patching, asset inventory
Exposed remote accessRemote desktop or other remote tools open to the internet without MFARemove direct exposure, use VPN with MFA
Business email compromiseA real or look-alike email account is used to request payments or change bank detailsCall-back verification, MFA on email
Third partiesAn IT provider, software supplier or partner with access to your systems is compromisedVendor controls, limited access, MFA

Spotting phishing

Phishing messages often share signs: urgency ("your account will be closed today"), a request to log in, pay or open a file, a sender address that does not quite match, generic greetings, and links whose real address differs from the text. Generative AI now makes phishing messages fluent and personalized, so spelling mistakes are no longer a reliable sign. The safest habit is to treat any unexpected request to log in, pay or change details as suspicious, and to verify it through a separate channel you already trust.

The lab after this lesson asks you to sort messages into safe, verify first, and report as phishing.

Key takeaways

  • Phishing, stolen passwords, unpatched systems and exposed remote access are the main ways in.
  • Business email compromise targets payments and bank details.
  • Verify any unexpected request to log in, pay or change details through a separate channel.

Apply it at work

Look at the last ten external emails that asked you to click, log in or pay. For each, decide how you would verify it if you were unsure.

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 20 · 7 min

Ransomware explained

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts files and systems so they cannot be used, followed by a demand for payment, usually in cryptocurrency, for a decryption key. Modern ransomware groups operate like businesses, with affiliates, help desks and negotiators.

How a typical attack unfolds

  1. Initial access: through phishing, stolen credentials, exposed remote access or an unpatched system.
  2. Foothold and reconnaissance: the attacker quietly explores the network, often for days or weeks.
  3. Privilege escalation: they obtain administrator accounts to control more systems.
  4. Data theft: sensitive files are copied out.
  5. Backup destruction: connected backups are deleted or encrypted.
  6. Encryption and demand: systems are encrypted, often overnight or at a weekend, and a ransom note appears.

Double extortion

Because good backups let victims recover without paying, many groups now also steal data first and threaten to publish it. Even a company that restores everything from backup may face a demand to stop the leak, plus legal duties to notify people whose information was taken.

Why the timeline matters

Because attackers often spend time inside the network before encrypting, there are chances to detect and stop them: alerts from endpoint protection, unusual logins, new administrator accounts or large data transfers. Each stage also has a matching defence: MFA blocks many initial access attempts, limited admin rights slow escalation, and offline backups survive the destruction step.

Key takeaways

  • Ransomware encrypts systems and demands payment; groups run like businesses.
  • Attacks unfold in stages, often over days, which gives chances to detect them.
  • Double extortion adds data theft, so backups alone do not remove all harm.

Apply it at work

For each of the six attack stages, write down which control in your business, if any, would stop or detect it today.

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 20 · 7 min

What is at stake

A serious cyber incident affects much more than IT. Knowing the full cost helps you decide how much protection is worth.

ImpactExamples
DowntimeOrders, production, shipping, invoicing and phones stop; staff cannot work
Lost revenue and marginMissed orders, late deliveries, penalties, customers who switch
Response and recovery costsIncident response specialists, legal advice, rebuilding systems, overtime, new hardware
Data and legalNotifying affected people and regulators, possible claims, contractual breaches with customers
Fraud lossesPayments sent to criminals' accounts, often unrecoverable
ReputationLoss of customer and partner trust; harder sales conversations

Downtime is usually the biggest cost

Rebuilding systems after ransomware often takes days to weeks, even with good backups, because every system must be checked, cleaned and restored in the right order. Estimate the cost of a week without systems: lost gross margin on sales that could not be made, wages for staff who could not work, and recovery costs. The lab after this lesson calculates it.

Legal duties

Under Canada's federal private-sector privacy law (PIPEDA), organizations must report breaches of security safeguards involving personal information to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada when it is reasonable to believe there is a real risk of significant harm, notify affected individuals, and keep records of all such breaches. Some provinces have their own laws and requirements. A later lesson covers this in more detail.

With a realistic cost in hand, spending a few thousand dollars a year on the basics usually looks like good value, and the conversation with your insurer and bank becomes easier.

Key takeaways

  • Cyber incidents bring downtime, recovery, legal, fraud and reputation costs.
  • Downtime is usually the largest cost; recovery takes days to weeks.
  • Privacy law can require reporting breaches and notifying affected people.

Apply it at work

Estimate what one week without your main systems would cost: lost margin, idle staff and recovery. Use the lab after this lesson to check your figure.

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 20 · 7 min

Multi-factor authentication and passwords

If you do only one thing after this course, turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere it is available. MFA requires a second proof of identity, such as a code from an app, a push notification or a security key, in addition to the password. A stolen password alone is then not enough to log in.

Where MFA matters most

  1. Email accounts, which are the key to resetting every other password.
  2. Remote access: VPN, remote desktop gateways and remote support tools.
  3. Administrator accounts for systems, cloud services and networks.
  4. Banking and payment platforms.
  5. Cloud applications holding business data: accounting, CRM, file sharing.

Stronger and weaker forms

Authenticator apps and hardware security keys are stronger than text-message codes, which can be intercepted or redirected. Phishing-resistant methods, such as security keys and passkeys, cannot be captured by a fake login page. Use the strongest option each system supports, especially for administrators. Train staff to reject MFA prompts they did not start: attackers sometimes send repeated prompts hoping someone approves one.

Passwords still matter

  • Use a password manager so everyone can have long, unique passwords without remembering them.
  • Use passphrases of several random words where you must remember one.
  • Never reuse work passwords on personal sites; reused passwords leaked from other breaches are a common way in.
  • Change default passwords on routers, printers, cameras and other devices.
  • Remove accounts promptly when staff leave.

Key takeaways

  • Turn on MFA everywhere, starting with email, remote access, admin and banking.
  • Prefer authenticator apps or security keys over text messages.
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords; remove leavers' accounts promptly.

Apply it at work

List every system your business uses that holds data or money. Mark which have MFA turned on for all users, and set a date to turn it on for the rest.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 20 · 7 min

Patching, secure setup and knowing what you have

Attackers scan for systems with known, unpatched flaws within days, sometimes hours, of a fix being published. Keeping software up to date closes these doors.

Start with an asset inventory

You cannot protect what you do not know you have. Keep a simple list of:

  • Computers, servers, laptops and phones that access business data.
  • Network equipment: firewalls, routers, Wi-Fi access points, VPN devices.
  • Software and cloud services, with who owns each.
  • Internet-facing systems: anything reachable from outside your office.

Patch by priority

PriorityWhatTarget timing
HighestInternet-facing systems (firewalls, VPNs, email, remote access) with flaws known to be exploitedAs fast as possible, within days
HighOperating systems and browsers on staff devicesAutomatic updates, checked monthly
NormalOther applications and internal systemsRegular monthly cycle

Turn on automatic updates wherever practical. Replace systems that no longer receive security updates, such as old operating systems and end-of-life firewalls; they cannot be patched.

Secure configuration

  • Change default passwords and turn off features you do not use.
  • Do not expose remote desktop directly to the internet.
  • Use a business-grade firewall and keep its firmware updated.
  • Separate guest Wi-Fi from the business network.

Production equipment controls and building systems that connect to the network need the same attention, coordinated with their suppliers.

Key takeaways

  • Keep a simple inventory of devices, network equipment, software and internet-facing systems.
  • Patch internet-facing systems first and fastest; automate updates where you can.
  • Replace unsupported systems and remove direct internet exposure of remote access.

Apply it at work

Build a one-page inventory of your internet-facing systems. For each, write when it was last updated and who is responsible.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 20 · 8 min

Backups that survive ransomware

Backups are your recovery plan when everything else fails. But attackers know this, and they look for backups to delete or encrypt before they launch ransomware. A backup that is always connected to the network can be destroyed along with everything else.

The 3-2-1 rule, updated

  • 3 copies of important data: the original plus two backups.
  • 2 different types of storage or locations.
  • 1 copy offline or immutable: disconnected from the network, or stored in a way that cannot be changed or deleted for a set period, even by an administrator.

Many cloud backup services offer immutability or object lock. Make sure backup administration uses separate credentials with MFA, not the same accounts used day to day.

Back up the right things

  • File shares and documents.
  • Business systems: accounting, ERP, CRM databases.
  • Cloud data: do not assume your cloud provider keeps restorable backups of your email and files for as long as you need; check the terms and consider a separate backup.
  • System configurations, so servers and network devices can be rebuilt.

Test restores

A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a plan. Test restoring individual files every month and a full critical system at least once or twice a year. Time the full restore: if it takes four days to restore your ERP, that is part of your downtime estimate. Document the restore steps so someone other than your usual IT person can follow them.

Decide recovery targets

For each critical system, agree how much data you can afford to lose (for example, one day's work) and how long it can be down (for example, two days). These targets drive backup frequency and the recovery approach.

Key takeaways

  • Keep at least one backup offline or immutable, with separate admin credentials.
  • Back up business systems, cloud data and configurations, not just files.
  • Test restores regularly and time a full restore of critical systems.

Apply it at work

Find out when your last full restore of a critical system was tested and how long it took. If never, schedule a test this month.

Check your understanding: Why must at least one backup be offline or immutable?

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 20 · 7 min

Endpoint protection, email filtering and admin rights

Several technical controls work together to stop attacks that get past the first line.

ControlWhat it does
Endpoint detection and response (EDR)Monitors computers and servers for suspicious behaviour, such as mass file encryption or credential theft tools, and can isolate a device automatically; more capable than traditional antivirus
Email filteringBlocks known malicious attachments, links and spoofed senders before they reach inboxes
Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC)Makes it harder for criminals to send email that appears to come from your domain
DNS or web filteringBlocks connections to known malicious websites
FirewallControls traffic between your network and the internet

Least privilege

Give people and systems only the access they need for their job. In particular:

  • Staff should not use administrator accounts for daily work like email and browsing. Use a separate admin account only when needed.
  • Limit who can install software.
  • Restrict access to sensitive folders and systems by role, and review access when people change jobs or leave.
  • Protect and monitor the few accounts that can control everything, such as domain and cloud tenant administrators.

Least privilege slows attackers down: when a user's account is compromised, the attacker gets only that user's access, not the keys to everything.

Monitoring

Controls produce alerts. Someone must watch them and act, whether an internal person, your IT provider or a managed detection and response (MDR) service that monitors around the clock. An alert at 2 a.m. on Saturday is when ransomware often starts.

Key takeaways

  • EDR, email filtering and authentication, and DNS filtering catch what gets past the first line.
  • Use least privilege: no daily use of admin accounts; access by role.
  • Make sure someone watches alerts, including outside business hours.

Apply it at work

Check who in your business has administrator rights on computers and cloud services. Remove rights that are not needed and give admins separate accounts.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 20 · 7 min

Security awareness that works

People are often called the weakest link. A better view is that people are your largest sensor network: well-trained staff who report suspicious messages quickly can stop an attack before it spreads.

What effective awareness looks like

  • Short and frequent: a few minutes a month beats an annual hour-long video.
  • Practical: real examples from your industry: fake invoices, delivery notices, shared-document links, payroll change requests.
  • Phishing simulations: safe test emails show who clicks, followed by a short lesson on the spot, not punishment.
  • Easy reporting: a "report phishing" button or a simple email address, and a quick thank-you every time.
  • Role-specific: extra training for finance, payroll, executives and IT administrators, who are targeted most.

A reporting culture

The most important message is: if you clicked something you shouldn't have, tell us immediately. Someone who reports a mistake within minutes lets IT reset a password or isolate a computer before damage is done. Someone afraid of blame stays quiet, and the attacker keeps going. Thank people who report, even when it turns out to be a false alarm.

Cover the basics for everyone

  • Recognizing phishing and verifying unusual requests.
  • Using MFA and a password manager; never approving unexpected MFA prompts.
  • Locking screens, protecting devices and reporting lost ones.
  • Handling sensitive data and what not to paste into public AI tools.
  • Whom to call and what to do if something seems wrong.

Key takeaways

  • Treat staff as sensors: train them to spot and report quickly.
  • Short monthly training and safe phishing simulations work better than annual sessions.
  • Reward reporting, including of mistakes, so problems surface fast.

Apply it at work

Set up a simple way for staff to report suspicious emails and announce it with a short message. Plan one 5-minute awareness topic per month for the next six months.

Module 3 · Lesson 10 of 20 · 7 min

Stopping payment fraud and business email compromise

Business email compromise (BEC) and invoice fraud are among the costliest cyber crimes for businesses, and they often need no malware at all. A criminal impersonates a supplier, customer or executive and asks for a payment or a change of bank details. Money sent to a fraudulent account is often impossible to recover.

Common scenarios

  • A "supplier" emails to say its bank account has changed and asks that the next invoices be paid to the new account.
  • An "executive" asks finance to make an urgent, confidential payment.
  • A real supplier's email account has been hacked, and the fraudulent request comes from their genuine address in an existing email thread.
  • An "employee" asks payroll to change the account where their pay is deposited.

Controls that work

ControlHow it works
Call-back verificationAny change to bank details is confirmed by phoning the supplier on a number already on file, never one given in the request
Dual approvalPayments above a threshold, and all new or changed payees, need two people to approve
Hold periodNew or changed bank details take effect only after a short waiting period and confirmation
MFA on emailMakes your own accounts harder to take over and use for fraud
Clear policyStaff know they will never be asked to bypass these steps, even by the owner

If it happens

Contact your bank immediately: the sooner they act, the better the chance of stopping or recovering the funds. Report to the police and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, preserve the emails, and check whether your email account was compromised.

Key takeaways

  • BEC and invoice fraud target payments and often use no malware.
  • Verify bank detail changes by calling a number already on file; use dual approval.
  • If money is sent, contact the bank immediately, then report and investigate.

Apply it at work

Write a one-paragraph rule for changing supplier or employee bank details, including call-back verification and dual approval. Share it with everyone who handles payments.

Module 3 · Lesson 11 of 20 · 7 min

Remote work, devices and mobile phones

Staff now work from home, job sites, hotels and phones. Each place and device is another way in if it is not managed.

Company devices

  • Use device management to enforce updates, screen locks, disk encryption and the ability to wipe a lost device.
  • Install endpoint protection on laptops as well as office desktops.
  • Encrypt laptop drives so a stolen laptop does not mean stolen data.

Personal devices

If staff use personal phones or computers for work, decide what is allowed. Many SMEs allow email and approved apps on personal phones through management tools that separate work data and can remove it without touching personal content. Avoid storing business files on unmanaged personal computers.

Remote access

  • Use a VPN or a secure remote access service with MFA; never expose remote desktop directly to the internet.
  • Prefer cloud services with strong authentication over remote access to office servers where practical.
  • Remove remote access when staff or contractors no longer need it.

Networks

  • Home routers should have updated firmware and a strong, non-default password.
  • Public Wi-Fi is risky for sensitive work; use the VPN or a phone hotspot.
  • In the office, keep guest Wi-Fi separate from business systems.

Lost or stolen devices

Make reporting a lost device immediate and easy. With device management, IT can lock or wipe it, and accounts can be signed out remotely. Check whether personal information was on the device, as this can trigger privacy obligations.

Key takeaways

  • Manage company devices: updates, encryption, screen locks, remote wipe.
  • Set clear rules for personal devices and keep business data in managed apps.
  • Use VPN or secure remote access with MFA; report lost devices at once.

Apply it at work

List every device that accesses business email or files, including personal phones. Mark which are managed, encrypted and can be wiped remotely.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 of 20 · 7 min

IT providers, cloud services and suppliers

Most SMEs rely on outside parties: a managed IT provider (MSP), cloud software, payment processors and suppliers who connect to their systems. Each brings expertise, and each is also a possible way in.

Managed IT providers

An MSP often has administrator access to many clients at once, which makes it a valuable target. Ask your provider:

  • Do you use MFA on all accounts that can access our systems, including your remote management tools?
  • Who on your team has access to our systems, and how is it reviewed?
  • How are our backups protected from deletion, and when was a restore last tested?
  • What do you monitor, and who responds to alerts outside business hours?
  • What will you do, and how fast, if we have an incident? Is incident response included or extra?

Put the answers in your contract or service agreement where you can.

Cloud services: shared responsibility

Cloud providers secure their infrastructure, but you are responsible for how you use it: user accounts, MFA, sharing settings, access permissions and, in many cases, backups of your own data. Misconfigured sharing, such as files shared publicly by link, is a common cause of data exposure.

Suppliers and partners

  • Give third parties only the access they need, for as long as they need it.
  • Require MFA for any external account on your systems.
  • Ask key suppliers about their security, especially those handling your customers' data.
  • Expect your larger customers to ask you the same questions; good basics help you win and keep contracts.

Key takeaways

  • MSPs and suppliers with access to your systems are possible ways in.
  • Ask your IT provider about MFA, access, backups, monitoring and incident response.
  • In the cloud, you remain responsible for accounts, settings and often backups.

Apply it at work

Send your IT provider the five questions in this lesson and review the answers with them. Note any gaps and agree dates to close them.

Check your understanding: In a cloud service, who is usually responsible for turning on MFA and managing sharing settings for your users?

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 20 · 7 min

Your incident response plan

When an incident hits, people panic, systems are down and decisions are needed fast. A short plan written in advance makes the difference between a bad week and a disaster.

What the plan should contain

SectionContent
ContactsIncident lead and deputy, IT provider, cyber insurer's hotline, incident response firm, lawyer, bank, key customers and suppliers; printed and stored offline
RolesWho decides, who handles technical work, who communicates with staff, customers and media
First stepsHow to contain different incidents: ransomware, compromised email, lost device, payment fraud
CommunicationHow to talk if email and phones are down; holding statements for staff and customers
Legal and reportingWhen to involve legal counsel, the insurer, police and privacy regulators
RecoveryPriority order for restoring systems, linked to backup and restore procedures
After the incidentReview what happened and improve controls

Keep it short and reachable

Two to five pages is enough for most SMEs. Keep printed copies at home and the office, and a copy on a phone, because the network may be unavailable during an incident. Include a simple decision tree for the first hour.

Practise it

Run a tabletop exercise once a year: gather the leadership team for 90 minutes, walk through a realistic scenario ("Monday 6 a.m., screens show a ransom note") and talk through each decision. Exercises reveal missing phone numbers, unclear authority and untested assumptions, cheaply and safely. The Cyber Centre and many insurers offer free scenarios.

Know your insurer's rules

Many cyber policies require you to call their hotline first and to use their approved response firms. Calling someone else first can affect coverage, so put the insurer's number at the top of the contact list.

Key takeaways

  • Write a short plan with contacts, roles, first steps, communication and recovery order.
  • Keep printed and phone copies; the network may be down.
  • Run a tabletop exercise each year and follow your insurer's first-call rules.

Apply it at work

Draft the contact page of your incident response plan today: names, roles and phone numbers, including your insurer's hotline. Print it and store a copy at home.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 20 · 8 min

The first hours of a ransomware attack

What you do in the first hours shapes how bad the incident becomes. The goals are to stop the spread, preserve evidence and get expert help quickly.

Do

  1. Isolate affected devices: disconnect them from the network by unplugging network cables or turning off Wi-Fi. Do not switch them off unless your responders advise it, because memory can hold evidence and sometimes encryption keys.
  2. Call your incident lead and your insurer's hotline, then the incident response firm and IT provider as the plan says.
  3. Protect backups: make sure offline backups stay disconnected until responders confirm it is safe.
  4. Preserve evidence: photograph ransom notes and screens, keep logs, and write down a timeline of what was seen and done.
  5. Use out-of-band communication: phones or a separate messaging service, since attackers may be watching email.
  6. Reset credentials from a clean device, starting with administrator and email accounts, as responders direct.

Don't

  • Don't wipe or rebuild systems before evidence is preserved and the entry point is understood; attackers may still be inside and reinfect restored systems.
  • Don't contact the attackers or pay anything before talking to your insurer, responders and legal counsel.
  • Don't announce details publicly before you understand the situation and have legal advice.

Recovery in the right order

Responders will typically confirm the attacker has been removed, then restore in priority order: identity systems and core infrastructure first, then critical business systems, then the rest, checking each before reconnecting. Report the incident to the Cyber Centre and the police; they can sometimes share decryption tools or intelligence and use reports to warn others.

Key takeaways

  • Isolate affected devices from the network; don't power off or wipe without advice.
  • Call your incident lead and insurer, protect backups and preserve evidence.
  • Restore in priority order only after the attacker has been removed.

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 20 · 7 min

Should you pay a ransom?

Paying a ransom is one of the hardest decisions a business can face. Canadian authorities, including the Cyber Centre and the RCMP, discourage paying. The decision ultimately rests with the business, but it should never be made alone or in a hurry.

Reasons not to pay

  • No guarantee: decryption tools may be slow, faulty or never delivered; stolen data may still be published or sold.
  • Repeat targeting: organizations that pay may be seen as willing to pay again.
  • Funding crime: payments fund further attacks.
  • Legal risk: paying a group subject to sanctions can be unlawful; legal advice is essential.
  • Recovery is still needed: even with a working key, systems must be cleaned, rebuilt and secured.

Why some businesses consider it

When backups were destroyed or never existed and the business cannot survive weeks of downtime, owners may feel they have no choice. This is exactly why tested, offline backups matter: they turn a desperate decision into a recovery project.

If the question arises

  • Involve your insurer, legal counsel and incident response firm before any contact with attackers.
  • Check what your backups can recover and how long it would take, so the choice is based on facts.
  • Report the incident to the police and the Cyber Centre whatever you decide.
  • Remember that payment does not remove legal duties, such as notifying people whose data was stolen.

The best answer to this question is to make sure you never have to ask it.

Key takeaways

  • Canadian authorities discourage paying ransoms; payment guarantees nothing.
  • Paying can carry legal risk and does not remove recovery work or notification duties.
  • Tested offline backups are what make 'don't pay' a realistic choice.

Apply it at work

Ask yourself honestly: if ransomware struck tonight, could you recover without paying? List what would need to be true, and what to fix if the answer is no.

Module 4 · Lesson 16 of 20 · 7 min

Breach notification and legal duties in Canada

If an incident involves personal information, privacy law may require you to report it and to notify the people affected. Understanding the basics in advance helps you act correctly under pressure.

Federal law: PIPEDA

Under the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act, which applies to many private-sector organizations in Canada, an organization must:

  • Report to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada any breach of security safeguards involving personal information under its control if it is reasonable to believe the breach creates a real risk of significant harm to an individual.
  • Notify affected individuals in those cases, as soon as feasible, with enough information to understand the harm and protect themselves.
  • Notify other organizations that may be able to reduce the harm, such as banks.
  • Keep a record of every breach of security safeguards, whether or not it was reportable, for at least 24 months.

Significant harm includes things like identity theft, financial loss, humiliation and damage to reputation. The assessment considers how sensitive the information is and how likely it is to be misused.

Provincial laws

Some provinces have their own private-sector privacy laws with their own breach rules, such as Quebec's private-sector law as amended by Law 25 and Alberta's Personal Information Protection Act. Public bodies and health information are often covered by separate laws. Which law applies depends on where you operate and what information is involved.

Contracts

Customer contracts may require you to notify them of security incidents within a set time, sometimes 24 to 72 hours. Check your key contracts now, not during an incident.

This lesson is general information, not legal advice. Involve a lawyer with privacy experience as soon as an incident might involve personal information.

Key takeaways

  • PIPEDA requires reporting breaches with a real risk of significant harm and notifying affected people.
  • Keep a record of every breach for at least 24 months.
  • Provincial laws and customer contracts can add requirements; get legal advice early.

Apply it at work

Find out which privacy laws apply to your business and check your three largest customer contracts for incident notification requirements. Add both to your incident plan.

Module 5 · Lesson 17 of 20 · 7 min

Cyber insurance

Cyber insurance can cover costs such as incident response, legal advice, notification, data recovery, business interruption and, in some policies, fraud losses. It also gives access to experienced responders through the insurer's hotline, which many SMEs could not arrange on their own at short notice.

Insurers expect the basics

Application forms and renewals increasingly ask detailed questions, and some insurers scan applicants' internet-facing systems. Common requirements include:

  • MFA on email, remote access and administrator accounts.
  • Offline or immutable backups that are tested.
  • Endpoint detection and response on computers and servers.
  • Prompt patching and no exposed remote desktop.
  • Security awareness training and payment verification procedures.
  • An incident response plan.

Answer truthfully: an inaccurate answer on an application can put a claim at risk. If you cannot answer yes to a requirement, fixing it often improves both your security and your premium.

Read the policy

  • What is covered: response, business interruption, extortion, data restoration, fraud, regulatory costs?
  • Limits and sub-limits for each type of loss, and the deductible or waiting period for business interruption.
  • Exclusions, for example for unpatched known vulnerabilities, war or state-sponsored attacks, or social engineering fraud unless specifically added.
  • Who you must call first, and whether you must use approved vendors.

Work with a broker who understands cyber policies, and check how cyber cover interacts with your other insurance. Insurance transfers part of the financial risk; it does not replace the controls that prevent incidents.

Key takeaways

  • Cyber insurance covers response, recovery and interruption costs and provides expert help.
  • Insurers expect MFA, tested offline backups, EDR, patching and training.
  • Answer applications truthfully and read limits, exclusions and first-call rules.

Apply it at work

Review your cyber insurance application or policy against the six common requirements. List any you cannot honestly answer yes to and plan how to close them.

Frameworks and certification for SMEs

Frameworks give you a structured checklist so you do not have to invent a security program from scratch. Three are especially useful for Canadian SMEs.

FrameworkWhat it isGood for
Cyber Centre baseline cyber security controls for small and medium organizationsFree Canadian guidance listing a prioritized set of practical controlsA starting checklist sized for SMEs
CyberSecure CanadaA federal certification program for small and medium organizations based on the baseline controlsShowing customers and partners that you meet a recognized standard
NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0A widely used framework organized into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and RecoverOrganizing a growing program and talking with larger customers

The six functions in plain language

  • Govern: someone owns cyber risk, with policies, roles and a budget.
  • Identify: know your assets, data, suppliers and risks.
  • Protect: MFA, patching, backups, training, access control.
  • Detect: monitoring and alerts that someone acts on.
  • Respond: the incident plan, contacts and first steps.
  • Recover: tested restores and lessons learned.

Larger customers will ask

Manufacturers, distributors and service firms supplying large companies or governments increasingly receive security questionnaires or contract clauses requiring specific controls or certifications. Defence supply chains have their own, stricter requirements. Having a framework-based program, with evidence, turns these questionnaires from a scramble into a routine task.

Pick one framework, use it as your checklist and review progress every quarter. Check the Funding page on this site for programmes that may help SMEs pay for technology and cybersecurity projects, and confirm eligibility with each programme.

Key takeaways

  • Use a framework as your checklist rather than inventing one.
  • The Cyber Centre baseline controls and CyberSecure Canada suit Canadian SMEs.
  • NIST CSF 2.0 organizes a program into Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover.

Apply it at work

Download the Cyber Centre's baseline controls for small and medium organizations and mark each control as done, partly done or not started.

Setting priorities and a realistic budget

No SME can do everything at once. Prioritize the controls that stop the most common attacks for the least effort, then build up.

A practical priority order

PriorityControlWhy first
1MFA on email, remote access, admin and bankingBlocks most attacks that use stolen passwords
2Offline or immutable backups, testedMakes recovery possible without paying
3Patch internet-facing systems; remove exposed remote desktopCloses the doors attackers scan for
4Endpoint detection and response with someone watching alertsCatches attacks in progress
5Payment verification rulesStops costly fraud that needs no malware
6Awareness training and phishing simulationsReduces clicks and speeds reporting
7Least privilege and admin account separationLimits damage from a compromised account
8Incident response plan and tabletop exerciseMakes the worst day manageable

Thinking about cost

Several of these cost little beyond time: MFA is included in most business email and cloud services; payment verification is a procedure; an incident plan is a few pages. Others, such as EDR, monitoring, backup services and training platforms, are usually priced per user or per device per month. Ask your IT provider for a quote per item, and compare the total yearly cost with your estimate of a week of downtime from the earlier lab.

Assign ownership

Name one person in the business as accountable for cyber risk, even if the technical work is done by an outside provider. Review progress at a management meeting each quarter, using a short scorecard: percentage of accounts with MFA, last restore test date, patch status of internet-facing systems, phishing simulation results and open actions.

Key takeaways

  • Prioritize MFA, tested offline backups and patching of internet-facing systems.
  • Many top controls cost little beyond time; compare spending with your downtime estimate.
  • Name an accountable owner and review a short scorecard quarterly.

Apply it at work

Score your business against the eight priorities. For the first two that are not done, write the action, owner, cost estimate and date.

Your 90-day cybersecurity plan

A focused 90-day plan gets the most important controls in place and builds the habits to keep improving.

DaysFocusActions
1–30Lock the doorsTurn on MFA for email, remote access, admin and banking; set payment verification rules; list assets and internet-facing systems; check backups include an offline or immutable copy
31–60Detect and recoverPatch or replace internet-facing systems; deploy EDR with monitoring; remove unneeded admin rights; test a full restore of a critical system; start monthly awareness topics and a reporting button
61–90Prepare and governWrite the incident response plan and contact sheet; run a tabletop exercise; review cyber insurance; ask your IT provider the key questions; set up the quarterly scorecard

Measures to track

  • Share of accounts with MFA (target: 100% for email, remote access and admins).
  • Date and duration of the last successful restore test.
  • Internet-facing systems fully patched and supported.
  • Devices with EDR installed and reporting.
  • Phishing simulation click and report rates.

Keep going

After 90 days, use the Cyber Centre baseline controls or the NIST functions to choose the next steps, and consider CyberSecure Canada certification if customers ask for evidence. Security is not a project with an end date: threats change, staff change and systems change, so the quarterly review is what keeps you protected.
