Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 16 · 7 min

Cloud AI and private options

Most people meet AI through cloud services such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Microsoft Copilot. These are powerful and easy to use, but your prompts and files are processed on the provider's servers. For many tasks that is fine. For some, such as confidential contracts, customer records, health information or proprietary designs, businesses want more control.

A spectrum of options

OptionWhere data is processedControlTypical use
Consumer AI appsProvider's cloud; data may be used to improve services depending on settingsLowPublic information, personal use
Business and enterprise AI plansProvider's cloud, with contractual data protections and admin controlsMediumMost everyday business work under policy
Cloud AI in your own cloud accountModels hosted in your cloud tenant or regionMedium-highData residency needs, custom applications
Local or on-premises AIYour own computers or serversHighHighly sensitive data, offline use, full control

What "local AI" means

Local AI runs an open-weight language model, one whose model files can be downloaded, on hardware you control: a desktop with a capable graphics card, a laptop, or a small server for a team. Prompts and documents never leave your premises unless you send them elsewhere.

Not all or nothing

Most organizations use a hybrid approach: approved business cloud AI for everyday work, and local AI for the most sensitive tasks. This course helps you decide where local AI fits, set it up, connect it to your documents, run it safely and measure whether it is worth it.

Key takeaways

  • AI options range from consumer apps to business cloud plans to fully local models.
  • Local AI runs open-weight models on hardware you control.
  • Most businesses use a hybrid: business cloud for everyday work, local for the most sensitive.

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 16 · 7 min

When local AI makes sense

Local AI brings control, but also responsibility and some limits. It makes most sense when one or more of these apply.

Reasons to consider local AI

  • Sensitive data: legal, financial, health, personnel or proprietary technical information that your policy or clients don't allow in third-party services.
  • Client or contract requirements: customers who require that their data stays on your systems.
  • Data residency: a need to keep data in Canada or on-premises.
  • Offline or remote work: field sites, plants or vessels with poor connectivity.
  • Cost at volume: heavy, repetitive use, such as processing thousands of documents, where per-use cloud fees add up.
  • Control: a fixed model version that won't change behaviour without notice.

Reasons to stay with business cloud AI

  • You need the most capable models for complex reasoning, writing or coding.
  • You have no one to set up and maintain local systems.
  • Usage is light, so subscriptions cost less than hardware and time.
  • Enterprise plans already meet your data protection needs.

Be honest about trade-offs

Open-weight models that run on modest hardware are capable for many tasks, such as summarizing, drafting, extraction and document Q&A, but typically less capable than the largest frontier cloud models on difficult reasoning. Local AI also needs setup, updates, security and support. The lab after this lesson asks you to decide where different data belongs.

Key takeaways

  • Consider local AI for sensitive data, client requirements, residency, offline work, high volume or control.
  • Stay with business cloud AI when you need top capability or lack support capacity.
  • Local models on modest hardware are capable but usually behind frontier models on hard tasks.

Apply it at work

For your most sensitive AI use case, write down which of the six reasons apply and what would happen if the data went to a cloud provider.

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 16 · 7 min

Open-weight models explained

Open-weight models are language models whose trained parameters (weights) are published for download. Several AI labs and research groups release them in families of different sizes, such as Meta's Llama, Mistral, Alibaba's Qwen, Google's Gemma and OpenAI's open-weight models, among others. New versions appear frequently.

Size and capability

Model size is measured in parameters, usually billions (B). Larger models are generally more capable but need more memory and run more slowly. Small models (a few billion parameters) run on laptops and handle simple tasks; medium models (roughly 7 to 15B) run on a single good graphics card and handle most office tasks well; larger models (30B and up) need high-end hardware but come closer to cloud quality.

Specialized models

  • Chat or instruct models: tuned to follow instructions and converse.
  • Coding models: tuned for writing and explaining code.
  • Embedding models: turn text into numerical vectors for search; essential for chatting with documents.
  • Vision models: can read images and scanned documents.

Licences matter

"Open-weight" doesn't always mean "open source" or "free for any use". Licences vary: some allow broad commercial use, others restrict use above a certain number of users or for certain purposes. Read the licence before using a model in your business.

Source and integrity

Download models from the publisher or reputable repositories through established tools. Model files from unknown sources can be tampered with. Record which model and version you use, so results can be reproduced and issues traced.

Key takeaways

  • Open-weight models can be downloaded and run locally in various sizes.
  • Larger models are more capable but need more memory; embedding models power document search.
  • Check licences and download from reputable sources.

Apply it at work

Pick two open-weight model families and look up their sizes and licence terms. Note any commercial use restrictions.

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 16 · 8 min

Hardware: memory, GPUs and quantization

The main hardware limit for local AI is memory: the model must fit in the memory of the graphics card (GPU) or, more slowly, in system memory.

Quantization

Models are usually run in quantized form, storing each parameter with fewer bits, commonly 4 to 8 bits instead of 16. This cuts memory needs several-fold with a modest loss of quality. As a rough guide, a 4-bit quantized model needs about 0.5 to 0.7 GB of memory per billion parameters, plus extra for the conversation context.

Model sizeApproximate memory at 4-bitTypical hardware
3–4B2–3 GBRecent laptops, even without a dedicated GPU (slower)
7–8B5–6 GBGraphics card with 8 GB or more
12–14B8–10 GBGraphics card with 12 to 16 GB
30–32B18–22 GBGraphics card with 24 GB, or a high-memory workstation
70B40 GB or moreMultiple GPUs, a workstation GPU or a high-memory unified-memory computer

These are approximate; actual needs vary with the model, quantization method and context length.

Other options

  • Unified memory computers, such as Apple silicon Macs, share memory between processor and graphics and can run fairly large models.
  • CPU only: works for small models but is much slower.
  • Small servers with one or more GPUs can serve a team.

Speed

Speed is measured in tokens per second. For interactive chat, a reply that streams at reading speed or faster feels comfortable. Models that spill out of GPU memory into system memory slow down sharply, so choose a model that fits.

Key takeaways

  • Memory is the main constraint; the model must fit in GPU or unified memory.
  • 4-bit quantization needs roughly 0.5 to 0.7 GB per billion parameters plus context.
  • A 12–16 GB graphics card comfortably runs 7–14B models.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 16 · 7 min

Choosing a model for the task

No single model is best at everything. Choose by task, hardware and licence, then test with your own examples.

Match model to task

TaskWhat to look for
Summarizing and draftingA good general instruct model in the size your hardware allows
Document Q&AA general instruct model plus a good embedding model; long-context support helps
Extracting data into tablesModels that follow formatting instructions well
Coding helpA coding-tuned model
Scanned documents and imagesA vision-capable model, or OCR before the language model
French or other languagesModels trained well in those languages; test them

Build a small test set

Collect 10 to 20 realistic tasks from your work, with what a good answer looks like. Run each candidate model on the set and score the results for accuracy, completeness, format and speed. This takes an afternoon and is far more reliable than general rankings, because it reflects your documents and your questions.

Settings that matter

  • Context length: how much text the model can consider at once; longer contexts use more memory.
  • Temperature: lower values give more consistent, focused answers, which suit business tasks.
  • System prompt: standing instructions such as "answer only from the provided documents and say when you don't know".

Revisit regularly

New open-weight models are released often, and a newer model of the same size may be much better. Re-run your test set when a promising model appears.

Key takeaways

  • Choose models by task, hardware and licence.
  • Test candidates on 10 to 20 of your own tasks rather than relying on rankings.
  • Tune context, temperature and system prompt, and retest when new models appear.

Apply it at work

Write ten realistic test tasks from your work with notes on a good answer. Run two candidate models and score them.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 16 · 8 min

Chatting with your documents: RAG

Language models don't know your company's documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a model answer questions using your files, such as procedures, contracts, manuals and reports, without retraining it.

How RAG works

  1. Ingest: documents are loaded and converted to text.
  2. Chunk: text is split into passages, for example a few hundred words each, often with some overlap.
  3. Embed: an embedding model converts each chunk into a vector that represents its meaning.
  4. Store: vectors are saved in a vector database.
  5. Retrieve: when a user asks a question, the question is embedded and the most similar chunks are found.
  6. Generate: the model receives the question plus the retrieved chunks and writes an answer, ideally citing the sources.

Why it works well locally

All steps can run on your own hardware: the embedding model, the vector database and the language model. Documents never leave your systems. Tools such as AnythingLLM and Open WebUI handle these steps for you.

Good uses

  • "What is our procedure for returning a damaged shipment?" from the quality manual.
  • "What are the termination terms in the Acme contract?" from a contract library.
  • "What torque does the maintenance manual specify for this pump?"

Limits

RAG answers are only as good as the documents and the retrieval. If the right passage isn't retrieved, the model may answer from general knowledge or guess. Always show sources so users can check the original. The next lesson covers making RAG more accurate.

Key takeaways

  • RAG lets a model answer from your documents without retraining.
  • Steps: ingest, chunk, embed, store, retrieve, generate with sources.
  • All steps can run locally; answers depend on document quality and retrieval.

Apply it at work

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 16 · 7 min

Making document answers accurate

Most RAG accuracy problems come from documents and settings, not the model. A few practices make a large difference.

Prepare documents

  • Use clean, text-based files; run OCR on scanned PDFs and check the result.
  • Remove outdated versions and duplicates so old answers don't appear.
  • Keep headings and structure; they help chunking and retrieval.
  • Organize documents into separate workspaces by topic or department so retrieval searches the right set.

Tune retrieval

  • Chunk size: too small loses context, too large dilutes relevance. A few hundred words with some overlap is a common start.
  • Number of chunks retrieved: enough to cover the answer without flooding the model; test a few values.
  • Embedding model: a better embedding model often improves accuracy more than a bigger language model.

Instruct the model

Use a system prompt such as: "Answer only from the provided documents. Quote or cite the source. If the answer isn't in the documents, say so." This reduces made-up answers.

Evaluate

Create 20 to 30 test questions with known answers and their source documents. Run them after each change and track how many are answered correctly with the right source. Ask users to flag wrong answers, and add those questions to the test set.

Document Q&A supports people; for decisions with legal, safety or financial consequences, users should read the cited source.

Key takeaways

  • Prepare clean, current, well-structured documents in focused workspaces.
  • Tune chunk size, number of chunks and the embedding model.
  • Instruct the model to cite and admit gaps, and evaluate with a test set.

Apply it at work

Write 20 test questions with answers and sources for your document set. Run them, change one setting, and compare the score.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 16 · 7 min

Securing local AI

Local AI keeps data in-house, but the system itself must be secured like any other server or application.

Key practices

  • Don't expose it to the internet. Local model servers and chat interfaces should be reachable only on your internal network or through a VPN, never directly from the internet.
  • Require logins with strong passwords and multi-factor authentication where the tool supports it; give each person their own account.
  • Limit access by workspace: HR documents should only be searchable by HR, contracts by those who need them.
  • Keep software updated: the model engine, front end, operating system and drivers.
  • Protect the machine: disk encryption, endpoint protection and physical security for the computer or server.
  • Back up configurations and the document store, and keep the original documents in their normal managed location.

Model and tool provenance

Download models and plug-ins only from reputable sources. Prefer widely used tools with active maintenance. Be careful with community add-ons that run code.

Prompt injection

Documents can contain hidden or malicious instructions, such as text telling the AI to ignore its rules. This matters most when the AI can take actions, such as sending email or running tools. Limit what local agents can do, require human approval for actions and treat content from outside sources with caution.

The Cybersecurity and Ransomware Basics for SMEs course covers broader security practices that apply here.

Key takeaways

  • Never expose local AI directly to the internet; use internal networks or VPN.
  • Require individual logins, limit workspaces by role and keep everything updated.
  • Use reputable models and tools, and guard against prompt injection when AI can act.

Apply it at work

Check where your local AI tool listens on the network and who can log in. Close any internet exposure and set up per-user accounts.

Privacy and compliance

Running AI locally helps with privacy, but it doesn't remove legal duties for the personal information you process.

Privacy principles still apply

Canadian privacy law, including PIPEDA, applies to personal information wherever it is processed. Key principles:

  • Purpose: use personal information only for the purposes it was collected for, or with consent.
  • Minimization: load only the documents and information the use case needs.
  • Safeguards: protect it with security appropriate to its sensitivity.
  • Access: limit who can query workspaces containing personal information.
  • Retention: remove documents and chat logs when no longer needed.

Logs and chat history

Local tools often store every prompt and answer. Those logs can contain sensitive information. Decide whether to keep them, for how long and who can see them, and configure the tool accordingly.

Client and contract obligations

If clients have shared confidential information under agreements, check whether using it in any AI system, even a local one, is permitted, and whether you must tell them.

Document your approach

Write a short record of each local AI use case: purpose, data used, who has access, retention and safeguards. It helps answer client questions, supports privacy obligations and makes reviews easier. Get privacy or legal advice for high-risk uses, such as health information or decisions about individuals.

Key takeaways

  • Privacy law applies to personal information processed by local AI too.
  • Minimize data, limit access, and manage logs and retention.
  • Check client agreements and document each use case.

Desktop or team server

Local AI can start on one person's computer and grow into a shared team service. Each has its place.

Personal desktopTeam server
UsersOne personSeveral people through a browser
HardwareA PC or laptop with a suitable GPU or unified memoryA server or workstation with one or more GPUs
SetupSimple; desktop appsNeeds IT skills: networking, accounts, backups
ConcurrencyNot an issueSeveral simultaneous users slow responses; size hardware accordingly
GovernancePersonal disciplineWorkspaces, permissions, logs and support

Start small

Many SMEs begin with a pilot on one capable desktop used by a few people for a defined use case, such as searching the quality manual or summarizing contracts. If it proves useful, they move to a small server with proper accounts and backups.

Operating a team server

  • Name an owner responsible for updates, access and support.
  • Monitor usage and response times to know when hardware needs upgrading.
  • Plan for power, cooling and noise: GPUs under load use significant power.
  • Keep a simple runbook: how to restart services, update models and restore from backup.

Managed private options

Some providers offer private AI hosted in Canadian data centres or in your own cloud account, managed for you. These can give much of the control of local AI without running hardware yourself. Compare them on data location, contract terms and cost.

Key takeaways

  • Start with a desktop pilot; move to a team server when value is proven.
  • Team servers need an owner, accounts, backups, monitoring and a runbook.
  • Consider managed private hosting in Canada as an alternative to running hardware.

Limits and the hybrid approach

Local AI is a valuable tool, not a replacement for every cloud service. Knowing its limits avoids disappointment.

Common limits

  • Capability: models that fit on modest hardware typically trail the largest frontier models on complex reasoning, nuanced writing and advanced coding.
  • Hallucination: local models can make things up, like any model; RAG with citations helps but doesn't eliminate it.
  • Speed: large models on limited hardware can be slow.
  • Features: cloud services often include web search, advanced file handling, voice and integrations out of the box.
  • Maintenance: someone must update, secure and support the system.

A sensible hybrid

WorkWhere to run it
Public information, general writing, researchApproved business cloud AI
Internal non-sensitive documentsBusiness cloud AI under policy, or local
Confidential client, legal, HR or technical documentsLocal or private-hosted AI
Credentials and banking detailsNever in any AI tool

Watch the trend

Open-weight models have improved quickly, and hardware keeps getting more capable. Tasks that needed cloud models a year ago can often run locally today. Revisit your split every six to twelve months using your test set.

Key takeaways

  • Local models usually trail frontier models on hard tasks and need maintenance.
  • Use a hybrid: cloud for general work, local for confidential documents, never for credentials.
  • Re-test every six to twelve months as models improve.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 16 · 7 min

Comparing the cost of local and cloud AI

Local AI swaps per-user or per-use fees for upfront hardware and ongoing upkeep. Compare them honestly over a realistic period.

Local costs

  • Hardware: a capable GPU workstation or small server; possibly upgrades later.
  • Setup time: installation, model testing, document preparation, security configuration.
  • Running costs: electricity, maintenance, updates, support time and backups.

Cloud costs

  • Per-user subscriptions for business AI plans, or per-use fees for API-based tools.
  • Possible add-ons for document features, higher usage or admin controls.

Example

Fifteen users on a business AI plan at $40 a month each cost $7,200 a year, or $21,600 over three years. A local setup with $4,000 of hardware, 40 hours of setup at $90 an hour and $150 a month of upkeep costs $7,600 upfront and $5,400 in upkeep over three years: $13,000 in total. In this example local breaks even in about 17 months. With fewer users or more upkeep, cloud wins.

Not just cost

The comparison isn't only financial. Cloud plans often give access to more capable models and features; local gives control and privacy. Many businesses keep both, using local only for sensitive work, which changes the numbers: compare the cost of the local system with the value of being able to use AI on documents you otherwise couldn't use it on at all.

The lab after this lesson compares the two for your own numbers.

Key takeaways

  • Local swaps subscriptions for hardware, setup time and upkeep.
  • Compare over a realistic period, such as three years.
  • Weigh capability and the value of using AI on otherwise off-limits data, not only cost.

Apply it at work

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 16 · 7 min

Local agents and connecting tools

Beyond chat and document Q&A, local models can power agents: AI that uses tools to take steps, such as searching files, querying a database, filling a form or drafting an email for approval.

How local agents connect to tools

  • Function or tool calling: many models can request a specific tool with structured inputs, which the application then runs.
  • Model Context Protocol (MCP): an open standard for connecting AI applications to tools and data sources. Some local front ends support MCP servers.
  • Built-in skills: tools such as AnythingLLM offer agent skills like web search, file saving and summarization.

Start with read-only tools

The safest first agents only read: search documents, look up an order status in a read-only database view, or summarize files. Tools that change data, send messages or spend money carry more risk.

Safeguards

  • Give agents the minimum permissions they need, with separate service accounts.
  • Require human approval for any action with external, financial or irreversible effects.
  • Log every tool call and review the logs during pilots.
  • Test with tricky inputs, including documents containing misleading instructions.

Capability matters

Reliable multi-step tool use is demanding, and smaller local models are often less reliable at it than large cloud models. Keep local agents to simple, well-defined tasks and test them thoroughly. The AI Agents for Business Operations course covers agent design in depth.

Key takeaways

  • Local models can act as agents through tool calling and MCP.
  • Start with read-only tools and require human approval for consequential actions.
  • Smaller models are less reliable at multi-step tool use; keep tasks simple and test.

Apply it at work
