Module 1 · Lesson 1 of 16 · 6 min

Why data matters, and what good decisions need

Most SMEs have more data than they use. Orders, invoices, inventory, timesheets, quotes and customer records sit in accounting software, ERP systems, CRMs and spreadsheets. Turned into a few clear measures, that data shows what is working, what is slipping and where to act.

Common symptoms of data that isn't working

  • Monthly reports take days to assemble by copying and pasting.
  • Different people quote different numbers for the same thing.
  • Problems such as falling margins or late deliveries are found weeks after they start.
  • Decisions rely on gut feel because the numbers are hard to get.

What good decisions need

NeedWhat it means
The right measuresA few KPIs linked to goals, not everything that can be counted
Trustworthy dataComplete, accurate and consistently defined
Timely accessAvailable when the decision is made, ideally automatically refreshed
Clear presentationEasy to read in seconds, showing trends and targets
A routineRegular reviews where data leads to actions

Start small

You don't need a data warehouse or a data scientist to start. A handful of well-defined KPIs, clean spreadsheets or a basic business intelligence tool, and a monthly review meeting deliver most of the value. This course walks through each step, from choosing KPIs to building and using dashboards.

Key takeaways

  • SMEs usually have plenty of data that is hard to use.
  • Good decisions need the right measures, trustworthy data, timely access, clear presentation and a routine.
  • Start small with a few KPIs and a regular review.

Apply it at work

List the reports your business produces each month, how long each takes and who uses them. Mark the ones that lead to decisions.

Module 1 · Lesson 2 of 16 · 7 min

Choosing KPIs that matter

A key performance indicator (KPI) is a measure that tells you whether you are achieving an important goal. The word "key" matters: a business with 60 KPIs effectively has none.

Start from goals

Begin with your business goals, then ask: what would tell us we are on track? A goal to grow profitably might lead to KPIs for revenue, gross margin percentage and new customers. A goal to improve service might lead to on-time delivery and complaint rate.

A KPI tree

Break a top-level result into the drivers that cause it. Profit depends on revenue and costs; revenue depends on number of customers, order frequency and average order value; and so on. A KPI tree shows managers which driver they control and how it links to the result.

Leading and lagging

Lagging indicators, such as monthly revenue and profit, show results. Leading indicators, such as quotes sent, pipeline value, on-time delivery or machine downtime, predict them. Include both.

Define every KPI

FieldExample: on-time delivery
DefinitionOrder lines delivered complete on or before the promised date ÷ all order lines delivered
Data sourceERP sales order lines and shipment dates
FrequencyWeekly, with a monthly trend
OwnerOperations manager
Target95%

Avoid vanity metrics

Some numbers look good but don't drive decisions, such as total website visits or social media followers. Ask of each KPI: if this changed, would we do something differently? If not, drop it. The lab after this lesson asks you to spot useful KPIs.

Key takeaways

  • Start from goals and keep KPIs few.
  • Use a KPI tree to link drivers to results; include leading and lagging indicators.
  • Define each KPI's formula, source, frequency, owner and target.

Apply it at work

Pick one business goal and draw a KPI tree with three levels. Write a full definition for the two most useful KPIs.

Module 1 · Lesson 3 of 16 · 7 min

Where your data lives

Before building reports, map where the data comes from. In most SMEs it is spread across several systems.

SourceTypical data
Accounting softwareInvoices, payments, expenses, financial statements
ERP or operations systemOrders, inventory, purchasing, production, shipping
CRMLeads, opportunities, quotes, customer activity
SpreadsheetsPlans, trackers, manual logs, anything not in a system
Website and marketing toolsVisits, enquiries, campaign results
Shop floor or equipmentOutput counts, downtime, quality checks
Payroll and timekeepingHours, labour cost, overtime

Map your data

For each KPI, record which system holds the data, who maintains it, how it can be extracted (built-in report, export to file, direct connection) and how often it changes. This map shows where the reporting effort goes and which sources need connecting.

One version of the truth

When the same information is kept in two places, such as customer lists in the CRM and in accounting, they drift apart. Decide which system is the system of record for each type of data: customers, products, prices, inventory. Other systems should take their data from it, ideally through an integration, rather than being updated by hand.

Spreadsheets as a warning sign

Spreadsheets are flexible and useful, but critical business data kept only in personal spreadsheets is fragile: it can be lost, overwritten or understood only by one person. When a spreadsheet becomes essential to running the business, consider moving that process into a system. The lab after this lesson asks you to prioritize which KPIs to automate first.

Key takeaways

  • Map each KPI to its source system, owner and extraction method.
  • Name one system of record for each type of data.
  • Critical data kept only in personal spreadsheets is a risk.

Apply it at work

Make a table of your top ten KPIs with their source systems and how the data is extracted today. Mark any that depend on one person's spreadsheet.

Module 1 · Lesson 4 of 16 · 7 min

Data quality basics

A dashboard built on poor data gives confident wrong answers. Data quality is the unglamorous foundation of good reporting.

Dimensions of data quality

DimensionQuestionExample problem
CompletenessIs anything missing?Orders without a promised date, so on-time delivery cannot be measured
AccuracyDoes it match reality?System inventory differs from what is on the shelf
ConsistencyIs it recorded the same way everywhere?"ABC Ltd", "A.B.C. Limited" and "ABC" as three customers
TimelinessIs it up to date?Timesheets entered a week late
ValidityDoes it follow the rules?Dates in the future, negative quantities

Master data

Master data is the core reference data used across transactions: customers, suppliers, products, prices, units of measure. Errors here spread into every report. Assign an owner to each type of master data, set rules for creating and changing records (for example, check for duplicates before adding a customer) and review it regularly.

Fix at the source

Cleaning data every month in a spreadsheet treats the symptom. Fix the cause: make key fields mandatory in the system, use drop-down lists instead of free text, train staff on why the data matters and give feedback when errors occur.

Measure it

Track a few quality measures, such as the share of orders with a promised date or duplicate customer records, and show them alongside the KPIs they affect. Data quality improves when someone owns it and it is visible.

Key takeaways

  • Check completeness, accuracy, consistency, timeliness and validity.
  • Give master data owners and rules for creating and changing records.
  • Fix errors at the source with mandatory fields, drop-downs and training.

Apply it at work

Pick one important report and check 20 records for the five quality dimensions. List the top problem and how to fix it at the source.

Module 2 · Lesson 5 of 16 · 7 min

Structuring data in tables

Most spreadsheet pain comes from how the data is laid out. Analysis tools work best with data arranged as a simple table, often called tidy data.

Rules for a good data table

  • One row per record: one order line, one invoice, one shipment.
  • One column per field: date, customer, product, quantity, price.
  • One header row with clear, unique names.
  • No merged cells, blank rows or subtotals inside the data.
  • One type of data per column: dates as dates, numbers as numbers.
  • Raw data separate from reports: keep data on one sheet and summaries on others.

Wide versus long

A common layout puts months across columns: Customer, Jan, Feb, Mar and so on. It looks like a report but is hard to analyze. The tidy version has columns Customer, Month and Sales, with one row per customer per month. It is longer but much easier to filter, total, chart and refresh.

Why it matters

Tidy tables can be summarized instantly with pivot tables, loaded into business intelligence tools and refreshed by adding new rows. Report-style layouts must be rebuilt by hand each month, which is slow and error-prone.

Use Excel tables

In Excel, format a data range as a table (Insert, Table). Tables expand automatically as rows are added, keep formulas consistent and make references readable, which reduces errors in formulas and pivot tables.

Key takeaways

  • Keep one row per record and one column per field, with no merged cells or subtotals.
  • Prefer long (tidy) layouts over months-across-columns.
  • Separate raw data from reports and format data as Excel tables.

Apply it at work

Take one spreadsheet you update monthly and restructure its data into a tidy table on a separate sheet. Build the report from it.

Module 2 · Lesson 6 of 16 · 8 min

Essential spreadsheet skills: lookups and pivot tables

A few spreadsheet features do most of the work in SME reporting.

Lookups

Lookups bring data from one table into another using a shared key, such as a product code. In current versions of Excel, XLOOKUP is the simplest: =XLOOKUP(A2, Products[Code], Products[Category], "Not found") returns the category for the product code in A2. Older versions use VLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH. Lookups let you add customer type, product category or region to transaction data for analysis.

Pivot tables

A pivot table summarizes a data table in seconds: total sales by month and region, average order value by customer type, count of late shipments by carrier. Drag fields into rows, columns, values and filters. Change the view without writing formulas. When the data table grows, refresh the pivot table.

Other useful features

FeatureUse
SUMIFS, COUNTIFS, AVERAGEIFSTotals that meet conditions, such as sales for one region in one month
Conditional formattingHighlight values above or below targets
Data validationDrop-down lists that prevent inconsistent entries
Charts from pivot tablesVisuals that update with the data
SlicersClickable filters for pivot tables and charts

Let AI help

AI assistants can write and explain formulas: describe what you need in plain language and test the result on rows where you know the answer. The Prompting and AI Productivity course covers this.

Key takeaways

  • Use XLOOKUP (or VLOOKUP/INDEX-MATCH) to combine tables by a shared key.
  • Pivot tables summarize data in seconds without formulas.
  • Use SUMIFS, conditional formatting, data validation and slicers.

Apply it at work

Build a pivot table from a tidy sales table showing sales by month and customer type, add a slicer for region, and check one total against your accounting report.

Module 2 · Lesson 7 of 16 · 7 min

Cleaning and combining data automatically

Monthly reports often start with the same chore: export files from several systems, delete header rows, fix dates, remove duplicates, combine sheets and add lookups. Doing this by hand every month wastes hours and invites mistakes.

Power Query

Excel and Power BI include Power Query, a tool that records the steps you take to clean and combine data, then repeats them with one click when new data arrives. Typical steps:

  • Load an export file or a whole folder of monthly files.
  • Remove extra rows and columns, rename headers.
  • Change data types: text to dates, text to numbers.
  • Split or merge columns, trim spaces, standardize text.
  • Remove duplicates and filter out unwanted records.
  • Merge with other tables, such as adding product categories.
  • Append files with the same layout, such as twelve monthly exports.

Example

A distributor exports sales from its ERP each month into a folder. A Power Query connected to the folder combines all files, fixes dates, adds product category and customer region from lookup tables and loads one tidy table. The monthly report now takes a minute to refresh instead of half a day.

Good practice

  • Keep source files in a consistent folder and format.
  • Name query steps clearly so others can follow them.
  • Check row counts and totals after each refresh against the source system.
  • Document what each query does and who maintains it.

Key takeaways

  • Automate repeated cleaning and combining steps instead of doing them by hand.
  • Power Query records steps and re-runs them on new data.
  • Check row counts and totals after each refresh and document queries.

Apply it at work

Pick one report you build from exports every month. List every manual cleaning step, then rebuild them in Power Query and time the next refresh.

Module 2 · Lesson 8 of 16 · 7 min

Avoiding spreadsheet errors

Spreadsheet errors are common and can be costly: a wrong formula in a pricing sheet or a broken link in a forecast can go unnoticed for months. A few habits prevent most of them.

Common errors

ErrorExample
Hard-coded numbers in formulas=B2*1.13 when the rate should come from an input cell
Ranges that miss new rowsSUM(B2:B100) after data grows to row 140
Copy-paste errorsA formula copied down with a reference that should have been fixed
Inconsistent formulasOne cell in a column overwritten with a typed value
Version confusion"Budget_final_v3_REAL.xlsx" emailed around
Broken linksReferences to files that moved or were renamed

Good habits

  • Separate inputs, calculations and outputs, with inputs clearly marked.
  • Use Excel tables and named ranges so formulas adjust to new data.
  • Add check totals: for example, total by region should equal the grand total; show a warning if not.
  • Keep one shared master copy in a shared drive or cloud folder with version history, instead of emailing copies.
  • Protect formula cells in sheets others fill in.
  • Have someone else review important models before decisions are made on them.

Know when to move on

When a spreadsheet is used by many people at once, holds critical data or needs an audit trail, it may be time for a proper system or a business intelligence tool. Spreadsheets are excellent for analysis and prototypes; they are risky as the backbone of operations.

Key takeaways

  • Watch for hard-coded numbers, short ranges, copy errors and version confusion.
  • Separate inputs, calculations and outputs, use tables and add check totals.
  • Keep one shared master with version history and review important models.

Apply it at work

Review one important spreadsheet for the six common errors. Add at least one check total and move any hard-coded rates into input cells.

Module 3 · Lesson 9 of 16 · 7 min

Designing a dashboard people use

A dashboard is a one-screen view of the measures a person or team needs to make decisions. Good design is about the audience and their questions, not about fitting in as many charts as possible.

Start with the audience and questions

  • Who will use it: the owner, a sales manager, a shift supervisor?
  • What decisions do they make, and how often?
  • What three to five questions should the dashboard answer at a glance? For example: Are we on track for the month? Which customers are slipping? Where are late orders building up?

Layout principles

  • Most important at top left, where eyes go first: headline KPIs with targets.
  • Trends below: how the KPIs are moving over time.
  • Detail last: breakdowns and tables for investigation.
  • Compare with something: target, last year or last month. A number alone means little.
  • Consistent colours: use colour to highlight exceptions, not to decorate. Avoid relying on red and green alone, since some people cannot tell them apart.
  • Fewer, clearer visuals: five to eight is usually plenty.

Different dashboards for different roles

The owner needs a monthly overview across sales, margin, cash and operations. A sales manager needs pipeline and customer detail weekly. A supervisor needs today's output and issues. One dashboard trying to serve everyone usually serves no one well.

Test it

Show a draft to the users and ask them to answer their key questions in under a minute. Watch where they get stuck, and simplify.

Key takeaways

  • Design around the audience, their decisions and three to five questions.
  • Put headline KPIs top left, trends next, detail last; always compare with a target or history.
  • Build different dashboards for different roles and test them with users.

Apply it at work

Choose one audience and write the three to five questions their dashboard must answer. Sketch the layout on paper before building anything.

Choosing the right chart

The right chart makes the message obvious; the wrong one hides it. Choose the chart by what you want to show.

To showUseAvoid
Change over timeLine chart; column chart for few periodsPie charts
Comparison between categoriesBar chart, sorted largest to smallest3D effects
Part of a wholeStacked bar, or a pie with very few slicesPies with many slices
Actual versus targetBar with target line, or a bullet chartSpeedometer gauges that waste space
Relationship between two measuresScatter chartTwo unrelated axes on one chart
Contribution to a totalPareto chart (sorted bars with cumulative line)Unsorted bars
Exact values for lookupTable with conditional formattingA chart where people must estimate values

Clarity rules

  • Start bar and column axes at zero, or the differences look larger than they are.
  • Label clearly: title that states the message, axis units, data source and date.
  • Remove clutter: heavy gridlines, borders, legends you can replace with direct labels.
  • Highlight what matters: one colour for the item of interest, grey for the rest.

Write the message as the title

Instead of "Sales by region", write "West region sales fell 12% while others grew". A title that states the takeaway helps busy readers and forces you to know what the chart says.

Key takeaways

  • Choose the chart by what you need to show: time, comparison, part of whole, target, relationship.
  • Start bars at zero, label units and sources, and remove clutter.
  • Use a title that states the message.

Apply it at work

Take three charts from a current report. For each, check the chart type against the table and rewrite the title to state the message.

Module 3 · Lesson 11 of 16 · 7 min

Business intelligence tools and data models

When spreadsheets become slow or hard to maintain, a business intelligence (BI) tool is the next step. Common options for SMEs include Microsoft Power BI, Google Looker Studio, Tableau, Zoho Analytics and the reporting tools built into many ERP and accounting systems.

What BI tools add

  • Connections to many data sources, refreshed automatically.
  • Interactive dashboards with filters and drill-down.
  • Sharing through a browser or app, with access control.
  • Handling of larger data volumes than spreadsheets comfortably manage.

Data model basics

BI tools work best with a simple data model, often called a star schema:

Table typeContentsExamples
Fact tableTransactions or events with numbers to add upSales lines, shipments, timesheets, downtime events
Dimension tablesDescriptions used to filter and groupDate, customer, product, region, employee

Facts connect to dimensions through keys such as product code and date. This structure keeps calculations correct and dashboards fast. A date table with fiscal periods, weeks and months is almost always worth adding.

Choosing a tool

Consider what you already use (Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace), the data sources you need to connect, licence costs per user, who will build and maintain the reports, and whether your ERP's built-in reporting already covers your needs. Start with one dashboard and grow from there.

Key takeaways

  • BI tools add automatic refresh, interactivity, sharing and scale.
  • Use a star schema: fact tables of transactions linked to dimension tables.
  • Choose a tool that fits your existing platforms, sources, skills and budget.

Apply it at work

For your most important dashboard, list the fact tables and dimension tables it needs. Check whether your current tools can connect to each source.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 of 16 · 7 min

Automating refresh and connecting sources

A dashboard that someone must update by hand each week tends to fall behind. Automating the data flow keeps it current and frees time for analysis.

Levels of automation

LevelHow data arrivesEffort
Manual exportSomeone exports a file and refreshes the reportOngoing manual work
Scheduled export to a folderThe system emails or saves a report file on a schedule; the dashboard reads the folderLow setup
Direct connectionThe BI tool connects to the system's database or API and refreshes on a scheduleModerate setup; may need IT or a partner
Data warehouseData from several systems is copied regularly into one central databaseHigher setup; useful as sources multiply

Practical tips

  • Check which connectors your systems and BI tool support; many cloud applications offer ready-made connectors.
  • Use a service account with read-only access for data connections, not a personal login.
  • Schedule refreshes to match decisions: daily for operations, weekly or monthly for management.
  • Show the "last refreshed" time on the dashboard so users know how current it is.
  • Set up alerts when a refresh fails, and name who fixes it.

Start where the pain is

Automate the reports that take the most manual time or matter most for decisions first. Many SMEs find that automating three or four core reports covers most of their needs.

Key takeaways

  • Move from manual exports to scheduled exports, direct connections or a warehouse as needed.
  • Use read-only service accounts and show the last refresh time.
  • Automate the most time-consuming, decision-critical reports first.

Apply it at work

Rank your reports by manual hours per month. For the top one, find out whether your systems offer a scheduled export or a connector to your BI tool.

Module 4 · Lesson 13 of 16 · 7 min

Running a data-driven meeting

Dashboards create value only when they change decisions. A short, regular meeting built around the dashboard turns data into action.

A simple agenda (30 to 60 minutes)

  1. Review KPIs against target quickly: on track, watch or off track.
  2. Discuss exceptions only: spend time on what is off track or changing, not on what is fine.
  3. Ask why: use the dashboard's detail to find causes, such as which customers, products or shifts.
  4. Agree actions with owners and dates.
  5. Review last meeting's actions and whether they worked.

Good habits

  • Everyone looks at the same dashboard, on screen or printed.
  • Data arrives before the meeting, not during it.
  • Questions about data accuracy are logged and fixed, not argued over in the meeting.
  • Look at trends, not single points: one bad week may be noise; four in a row is a pattern.

Avoid common traps

  • Reporting without acting: reading numbers aloud and moving on.
  • Blame: people hide or massage numbers if KPIs are used to punish.
  • Too many measures: attention spreads thin and nothing changes.

The Business Basics for Owners course covers the wider management rhythm of weekly, monthly and quarterly reviews.

Key takeaways

  • Review KPIs quickly and spend time on exceptions.
  • Use the dashboard's detail to find causes, then agree owned actions.
  • Look at trends, fix data issues outside the meeting and avoid blame.

Apply it at work

Run your next management meeting with the five-step agenda around one dashboard. Count how many actions with owners and dates come out of it.

Module 4 · Lesson 14 of 16 · 8 min

Simple analysis that finds answers

You don't need advanced statistics to learn a lot from business data. A handful of simple techniques answer most management questions.

TechniqueQuestion it answersExample
TrendIs it getting better or worse?Gross margin by month for 24 months
VarianceHow far from plan, and why?Actual versus budget by expense line
Year-over-yearIs change real or seasonal?This March versus last March
SegmentationWhich groups behave differently?Margin by customer type, region or product line
ParetoWhich few items drive most of the result?Top 20% of customers by profit; top reasons for returns
RatiosHow efficient are we?Revenue per employee, sales per square metre

Segment before you conclude

Averages hide important differences. Overall on-time delivery of 92% might be 98% for local customers and 75% for one region served by a particular carrier. Breaking data down by customer, product, region, channel or time usually reveals where the real issue is.

Correlation is not causation

Two measures moving together does not mean one causes the other. Sales and overtime may both rise in busy months. Before acting, look for the mechanism and, where possible, test a change and measure the result.

Small numbers

Percentages based on few cases swing wildly. Three late orders out of ten is 30%; next week it could be 0%. Show counts alongside percentages, and judge small groups over longer periods.

Key takeaways

  • Use trend, variance, year-over-year, segmentation, Pareto and ratios.
  • Segment data before drawing conclusions; averages hide differences.
  • Remember correlation is not causation and beware small numbers.

Apply it at work

Take one KPI and break it down by two segments, such as customer type and region. Write one sentence on what the breakdown shows that the average hid.

Module 4 · Lesson 15 of 16 · 7 min

Data governance, security and privacy

As data becomes more central, a light set of rules keeps it trustworthy, secure and compliant.

Governance in plain language

  • Owners: each key data type and each KPI has a named owner.
  • Definitions: a shared list of KPI definitions, so everyone means the same thing.
  • Change control: changes to definitions, calculations or source systems are agreed and recorded.
  • Quality checks: regular checks and visible quality measures.

Access and security

  • Give people access to the data and dashboards they need, not everything. Salary or customer-specific data may need restricting.
  • Use work accounts with multi-factor authentication for BI tools and shared drives.
  • Be careful when exporting data to personal devices or sharing dashboards by public link.
  • Remove access when people leave.

Privacy

Customer and employee personal information is protected by privacy law, including PIPEDA. Collect only what you need, use it for the purposes people were told about, protect it and keep it no longer than necessary. For many dashboards, aggregated or anonymized data is enough; avoid showing personal details when totals will do.

AI and data

When using AI tools to analyze data, follow your organization's rules about which tools may handle which data. The Prompting and AI Productivity and Cybersecurity courses cover these practices.

Key takeaways

  • Name owners, keep shared definitions and control changes.
  • Limit access, use MFA and avoid public sharing links.
  • Follow privacy law: collect what you need, protect it and aggregate where possible.

Apply it at work

Create a one-page KPI definition list with owners for your top ten KPIs, and review who has access to your main dashboard.
